Sustainable Direct Recycling of Lithium-Ion Batteries via
Solvent Recovery of Electrode Materials
Yaocai Bai,*[a] Nitin Muralidharan,[a] Jianlin Li,[a, b] Rachid Essehli,[a] and Ilias Belharouak*[a, b]
Separation of electrode materials from their current collectors is
an enabling step toward recovering critical materials from spent
lithium-ion batteries. In the presented research, a highly
efficient, cost-effective, and environmentally sustainable separation process was developed for that purpose. Ethylene glycol, a
vital commodity chemical as an antifreeze and polymer
precursor, is used to delaminate electrode materials at low
temperatures in seconds without altering the crystalline
structure and morphology of active electrode materials. The

recovered current collectors were intact without any corrosion.
The authors also found that the solvent could be continuously
reused, leading to the development of a closed-loop ecosystem
and lithium-ion battery circular economy. The ultrafast delamination was driven by the competitive inhibition of binding
through the weakening of hydrogen bonding. The ethylene
glycol-based separation is a sustainable electrode recovery
process that paves the way for battery recycling.

Most of the recoverable value in spent lithium-ion batteries
is in the cathode since it constitutes the major value added in
production.[1b] State-of-the-art recycling approaches – pyrometallurgical and hydrometallurgical processes – have been used
for the recovery of invaluable metals from spent cathodes.
However, pyrometallurgy is energy-intensive and cannot reclaim lithium, while hydrometallurgy involves caustic reagents
(e. g., hydrochloric and nitric acids) and requires complicated
purification and separation steps.[1b,2] More recently, direct
recycling has been proposed to recover cathode materials with
still-useful morphology while preserving the chemical
structure.[1b,3] This approach likely lowers the number of
recycling steps preceding new cell manufacturing, reduces
energy consumption (and hence, battery cost), and alleviates
environmental impacts by reducing greenhouse gas and SOx
emissions.[1b,3,4]
A major challenge for both direct recycling and hydrometallurgical processes is to efficiently separate cathode
materials from aluminum foil because of the strong adhesion
bonding capability and high chemical stability of the polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) binder, which is the most widely used
binder in lithium-ion battery manufacturing. This pretreatment
is important for improving the recycling efficiency, as well as for
reducing the energy consumption associated with the subsequent separation processes.[1c,5] Direct cathode recycling requires a separation process that does not incur a penalty in
terms of damaging active materials or introducing impurities.
The morphology of the recovered electrode materials should be
maintained to save the effort in subsequent regeneration
processes, while impurities from current collectors and side
reaction products should be avoided as much as possible to
circumvent both complex separation processes and contaminations of the active materials.[1c]

Introduction
Since their commercialization in the early 1990s, lithium-ion
batteries have been widely used to power consumer electronics. Meanwhile, they are gaining increasing popularity in
electric vehicles owing to their high-energy density and long
cycle life. However, recent reports have raised the concern of
flooding the market with end-of-life batteries.[1] Between now
and 2030, 11 million metric tons of lithium-ion batteries are
projected to approach their end-of-life.[1a] Therefore, developing
profitable recycling processes is indispensable to managing the
end-of-life packs and cells.

and useful method for separation.[7] However, the commonly
used solvents, such as N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) and
dimethylformamide (DMF), are volatile and toxic, limiting their

Overall, the separation of cathode material from aluminum foil
in a highly efficient, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly
manner remains a significant challenge.
In this work, the authors propose a solvent-based delamination process for rapidly and completely peeling off the
electrode materials from metal foils. Ethylene glycol (EG),
without any additives, is proposed to serve as the functional
solvent to deactivate the bonding between the PVDF binder
and the current collector through a competitive inhibition
mechanism. By simply immersing the spent electrode into the
EG solvent at low temperature, the electrode materials were
fully detached from metal foils in seconds, which is the fastest
record to the best of the authors’ knowledge. The developed
process excellently fulfils the requirements for direct recycling,
in which no morphological or crystalline changes were
observed for the delaminated electrode materials. Furthermore,
the aluminum foil was fully recovered without any corrosion. To
close the loop of recycling, EG after separation can be
continuously reused. The authors suggest that rapid delamination paves the way for both direct recycling and a hydrometallurgical strategy in managing spent battery packs.

Experimental Section
Materials and cell assembly
A LiNi0.5Mn0.3Co0.2O2 (NMC532) cathode and graphite anode were
fabricated using a pilot-scale slot-die coater (Frontier Industrial
Technology) at the US Department of Energy Battery Manufacturing
Facility at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. The cathode consisted of
NMC532 (Toda America), carbon black (Denka), and PVDF binder
(Solvay Solef 5130) in a weight ratio of 90 : 5 : 5. The areal loading of
the NMC532 cathode was ~ 11.5 mg cm 2. A 1.4 Ah lithium-ion
pouch cell (~ 6 × 8 cm2) was then assembled in the dry room. After 4
formation cycles at C/20, the pouch cell was charged/discharged at
1 C/2 C rates for 1000 cycles and the capacity degraded to 65.7 % of
its initial capacity (Figure S1 in the Supporting Information).
Cathode scraps were from the trimmings of a LiNi0.6Mn0.2Co0.2O2
(NMC622, Targray) cathode that was also fabricated at the Battery
Manufacturing Facility. All the solvents – including dimethyl
carbonate, ethylene glycol, glycerol, and diethylene glycol – were
purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without any treatment.

manually dismantled. The pouch, separator, cathode electrode, and
anode electrode were manually separated. Second, the electrodes

Characterizations
The crystal structure, surface morphology, and composition of the
cathode materials before and after separation, as well as the
recovered aluminum foils, were all characterized by x-ray diffraction
(XRD; PANalytical X’pert PRO) and scanning electron microscopy
(SEM; Zeiss MERLIN) with energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy
(EDX). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Bruker) was
used to analyze the cathode material as well as the ethylene glycol
before and after use. The lithium content of the NMC622 cathode
materials before and after delamination was measured by using an
inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES,
Agilent Technologies 5110).

As illustrated in Figure 1b, cathode material was peeled off
from aluminum foil as a self-supported electrode because of
the cohesive bonding of PVDF binder that holds the NMC
particles and carbon black together. Figure 1c shows digital
images of the recovered cathode materials and aluminum foils
with shiny glare, indicating no observable corrosion on the
aluminum. The EG-based separation process is robust with no
requirements for the size of the electrode. Figure S2 shows that
electrodes of different sizes were all delaminated completely in
a few seconds. The size independency makes this process
ubiquitous for recycling electric vehicle batteries that have
limited formats and sizes by avoiding shredding the batteries
into small pieces that may introduce impurities and subsequently complicate separation processes.[1c]
The presence of PVDF binder in the delaminated electrode

characteristic peaks of pristine PVDF at 1,399, 1,180, and

symmetrical stretching of CF2, and out-of-plane bending

Preserving the morphology of active materials during
separation is critical to facilitating the direct regeneration of
cathode materials for new battery manufacturing. The EG-based
separation process is demonstrated to be nondestructive to the
morphology of active materials. Figure 3a, b and d, e display
that the morphologies of the electrode before and after

of NMC532 and carbon black bonded by the polymeric binder

composition was consistent before and after separation. The
EDX spectra show that the ratio between different transition
metals including Ni, Mn, and Co was well-preserved after
delamination. In addition, the existence of F peak in the EDX
spectra after separation further demonstrates that EG-based
separation does not remove PVDF binder from the cathode
particles.
Copper and aluminum have been reported as the key
contributors to emissions of SOx and NOx in the cradle-to-gate
stages of the battery life cycle.[4c,10] Especially, using all recycled
aluminum can reduce total energy consumption during electric
vehicle production by 33 %.[4c] Aluminum foils are known to be
reactive and easily subject to corrosion during separation,
causing contamination on the active materials and thus
introducing complicated separation processes.[11] The EG-based
process fully recovered the aluminum foils with shiny surfaces,
suggesting that no corrosion occurred and that no residues of
electrode materials were left on the surface. The XRD pattern in
Figure S3 shows that all the diffraction peaks can be attributed
to aluminum, indicating that the process does not corrode
aluminum. SEM images in Figure 3g and h confirmed that clean
surfaces with micro-dents imprinted from NMC532 microparticles were observed. No transition metal elements were
detected through the EDX analysis (Figure 3i), further emphasizing that no active materials were left on the reclaimed
aluminum foils.

depends on the reaction temperature, as plotted in Figure 4.

After excluding the dissolution mechanism, a possible
delamination mechanism has been proposed here based on the
concept of competitive inhibition of binding through hydrogen
bonding, as schematically illustrated in Figure 6a. Competitive
inhibition of binding is an important principle in biochemistry
and medicine, in which a chemical pathway is interrupted
because one chemical substance inhibits the effect of another
by competing with it for binding or bonding.[16] The electrode
materials are tightly bound to aluminum foil by a combination
of forces, such as hydrogen bonding and van der Waals force.
As illustrated in the scheme, PVDF can form hydrogen bonding
with the aluminum surface, which typically has a thin
passivation layer of aluminum oxide. The strength of hydrogen
bonding for a solvent can be referred to as δH, which represents

before and after delamination, suggesting that delamination
does not significantly affect the electrochemical performance of
the recovered electrode. Delamination slightly reduced the
capacity, likely because of the weaker contact between active
materials caused by the delamination. Notably, the delaminated
electrode was freestanding and tested without the current
collector, which may have affected the charge transfer of the
electrode. In addition, the lithium content of the NMC622
cathode materials revealed by ICP-OES was reduced from 1.015
to 0.993 after delamination, most probably due to the lithium
leaching in the delamination and washing steps. Nevertheless,
the delaminated cathodes exhibited satisfactory electrochemical performance comparable to the untreated electrodes.
As confirmed by FTIR and EDX spectra, the PVDF binder was
not removed or dissolved during the EG-based separation
process, which can be explained by Hansen solubility parameters (HSP) that have been used for predicting the ability for a
solvent to solubilize PVDF.[13] The HSP distance, denoted as Ra,
represents the distance between PVDF and the solvent in a
three-dimensional Hansen space.[13] The lower the Ra value, the
higher chance that PVDF can be dissolved in that solvent.

due to the strong hydrogen bonding originating from the two
hydroxyl groups in one molecule. When the spent cathode is
placed in EG, solvent molecules immediately diffuse to wet the
interface between electrode materials and the aluminum
surface through the interconnected pores in the electrode. With
a high value of δH, EG can form strong hydrogen bonds with
the thin oxidation layer of aluminum oxide, competitively
replacing the bonding between the PVDF and substrate. The
porous nature of the electrode not only guarantees rapid
delamination but also explains the size independency of
delamination since the solvent diffusion is immediate and
independent of the electrode size.
As plotted in Figure 6b, water also has strong hydrogen
bonding with a δH value of 42.3 MPa1/2. However, water is not
suitable for delamination because the aluminum foil exhibited
corrosion due to lithium leaching from the NMC cathode,
making the water solution basic. Consequently, the corrosion of
aluminum would contaminate the NMC particles by forming Al
(OH)3 precipitates on the cathode surfaces.[11a] In addition to EG,
glycerol and diethylene glycol, both of which possess high
values of δH (29.3 and 20.7 MPa1/2, respectively), also work well
for delamination, further highlighting the significance of a
competitive inhibition mechanism.
Recycling aims to convert waste materials into new and
useful materials. It would be more sustainable if the recycling
process itself did not produce or yield waste materials,
ultimately leading to a closed-loop recycling process. Here, the
EG-based separation approach was demonstrated to be so, in
which the solvent EG can be directly recycled and reused.
Figure 7a shows the FTIR spectra of EG before and after the

delamination of cathode scraps. The spectra display no obvious
changes, suggesting that EG is stable during delamination. With
little to no waste creation, EG-based closed-loop recycling is
believed to substantially reduce the cost and environmental
impact, making it an economically and environmentally sustainable recycling process. Noteworthy, the spent cathode was
washed in dimethyl carbonate to rinse off electrolyte before the
EG-based delamination, ruling out the mixing effect of electrolyte with EG solvent. Several lab-scale studies have successfully
demonstrated the recovery and reuse of electrolyte from spent
LIBs, possibly avoiding the effect of electrolyte on the reuse of
EG solvent.[17] Nevertheless, the feasibility of reusing EG needs
to be further assessed if electrolytes are not extracted.
Figure 7b shows a spider chart for evaluating a solvent
regarding safety, health and environment criteria according to
the CHEM21 selection guide.[18] Both NMP and DMF are
reported as hazardous solvents, whereas EG is a recommended
solvent. Compared to NMP and DMF solvents, EG degrades
rapidly and is not persistent in air, surface water, soil, or
groundwater.[19] It is noteworthy that EG has a low toxicity
inherently but produces toxic metabolites, emphasizing the
importance of safe handling of this solvent in these
processes.[20] As a vital commodity chemical with an annual
consumption around 20 million tons, EG has been widely used
as antifreeze and as a polymer precursor, further highlighting
the advantages of the separation process in the economic and
environmental aspects. In addition to the delamination of
cathode materials from aluminum foils, the developed process
can also be applied to the separation of anode materials
(graphite, carbon black, and PVDF binder) from their current
collector, namely copper foil, which will substantially expand
the recycling revenue. The digital images in Figure 7c show that

Conclusions
An EG-based solvent separation process has been demonstrated to efficiently and rapidly delaminate electrode materials,
including a cathode and anode, from their current collectors
from spent Li-ion batteries. The electrode materials were
completely peeled off from the metal foils in several seconds,
which is so far the most efficient and rapid process. The
recovered electrode materials were intact in terms of their
crystal structure, morphology, and electrochemical performance. The current collectors, including aluminum foils and
copper foils, were fully recovered with no corrosion or
remaining residues. In addition to the rapid and damage-free
separation, the solvent was directly recycled and reused for
continuous delamination, which closes the recycling loop
without producing secondary waste. This environmentally
friendly and cost-effective separation process not only presents
a closed-loop recycling approach but also greatly advances
battery recycling into a new paradigm.
