The emergence and dominance of lithium-ion batteries in expanding markets such as consumer
electronics, electric vehicles, and renewable energy storage are driving enormous interests and
investments in the battery sector. The explosively growing demand is generating a huge number of
spent lithium-ion batteries, thereby urging the development of cost-effective and environmentally
sustainable recycling technologies to manage end-of-life batteries. Currently, the recycling of end-oflife batteries is still in its infancy, with many fundamental and technological hurdles to overcome.
Here, the authors provide an overview of the current state of battery recycling by outlining and
evaluating the incentives, key issues, and recycling strategies. The authors highlight a direct recycling
strategy through discussion of its beneﬁts, processes, and challenges. Perspectives on the future energy
and environmental science of this important ﬁeld is also discussed with respect to a new concept
introduced as the Battery Identity Global Passport (BIGP).
Keywords: Two-dimensional; Metallic nanocrystals; Renewable energy; Electrocatalysis

Introduction
The development of portable electronics such as cameras, laptops, and cell phones throughout the 1980s led to a growing
need for rechargeable batteries with high capacity and minimal
size and weight [1]. Rechargeable batteries such as lead-acid batteries and nickel–cadmium batteries, however, could not fulﬁl
the requirements because of their large size and heavy weight.
Concerted worldwide research efforts showed that lithium-ion
batteries (LIBs) stood out because of their lightweight, rechargeable, and powerful features [2–6]. Fig. 1 shows several major milestones in the development and application of LIBs. In the 1990 s,
Sony commercialized the ﬁrst rechargeable LIB, aiding the porta-

Demand for automotive battery has grown dramatically, and

Li-ion battery recycling value proposition
The recycling of LIBs offers many economic, environmental, and
social beneﬁts, which have been shown in several review articles

[12,13,19,20]. Moving from a linear economy to a circular one
can improve both the environmental and the economic footprint of batteries by harvesting end-of-life value from batteries.
Life-cycle analysis indicates that end-of-life batteries should not
be the end of the life cycle. Instead, recycled materials can serve
as raw materials for new battery manufacturing, offering a solution to overcome the supply chain issues of reliance on ﬁnite
resources and uneven distribution of metal reserves, which
improves energy security by reducing imports and lowering
dependence on foreign sources. Recovered materials could be a
stable and low-cost resource if cost-effective recycling technologies are developed, which could stabilize the material price and
potentially reduce the cost of batteries and EVs. A recent market
research report projects the LIB recycling market to grow from
$12.2 billion in 2025 to $18.1 billion by 2030, at a compound
annual growth rate of 8.2% from 2025 to 2030 [10]. Market
growth is attributed to the high output of spent batteries from
the automotive sector. The opportunity for generating sustainable and considerable jobs is associated with this vast market.
In addition to economic paybacks, recycling LIBs could reduce
the environmental harm from disposal in landﬁlls and lessen
the impacts of virgin material production. Life-cycle analysis
indicates that the use of recycled materials can substantially
reduce the energy use and emissions (e.g., CO2, SOx) through
the life cycle of LIBs [13,21–23]. For example, Dunn et al. estimated that the direct recycling process has the potential to
reduce greenhouse gas and SOx emissions from cathode material
production by 81–98% and 72% to nearly 100%, respectively
[21]. Thus, recycling end-of-life LIBs is critical to ensuring a
responsible, sustainable, and stable transition to a low-carbon
economy.
Although many economic and environmental advantages
from recycling exist, LIB recycling is still in its infancy because
of many impediments, including science and technology gaps,
economic barriers, collection, logistics and sorting issues, and
regulatory gaps [8,13]. Large ﬂuctuations in the price of raw battery materials and the ever-decreasing cost of batteries cast uncertainty on the economics of recycling, and they reduce the
incentive for recycling. Inefﬁcient collection, insufﬁcient collection infrastructures, high cost for transportation, and safety concerns during storage and shipping act as barriers to the increased
recycling of LIBs. The logistics of LIB transport and their storage
are reported to play a major role in mitigating the costs incurred
in recycling LIBs [10]. Lack of regulations and policies for spent

Lessons learned from lead-acid battery recycling
As one of the most widely used rechargeable batteries, lead-acid
batteries are found in a wide variety of small-medium scale storage applications such as automobile starting-lighting-ignition
(SLI) batteries and uninterruptible power supplies. About 99%
of lead-acid batteries are estimated to be recycled in the United
States, making this recycling one of the most successful recycling
programs [15]. Several factors contribute to the success of leadacid battery recycling, and lessons can be learned from this success to guide LIB recycling.
The characteristics of a single chemistry together with a standard design for all lead-acid cells result in a simple recycling process. Fig. 2 plots the cell composition in a NMC622 Li-ion cell
and a lead-acid battery. Lead-acid batteries have few components, making them easy to recycle. Additionally, almost 70%
of the mass of a lead-acid cell is lead or lead oxide, which is easily
recycled at a relatively low temperature. Recycling of lead-acid
batteries ﬂourishes because manufacturers seek the material as
a source to make new battery products, which are proﬁtable.
The battery chemistry of a lead-acid cell simpliﬁes its recycling
process, whereas that of a LIB complicates recycling. However,
lessons can still be learned from the success of lead-acid battery
recycling. Compared with lead-acid battery recycling, shortcomings in policy and infrastructure hinder LIB recycling. The LIB
sector should take a proactive approach to working with National
and International States and federal regulators on environmental

A typical Li-ion cell consists of four main components: cathode
or positive electrode, anode or negative electrode, separator,
and electrolyte. Additional materials are associated with each
component, making the cell composition complicated, as illustrated in Fig. 2. For example, a cathode is made up of active cathode material, conductive carbon, and binder, which are coated
onto a current collector, namely Al foil. As plotted in Fig. 2,
active cathode materials (e.g., NMC622) account for around
30% of the cell weight and are a substantial contributor to the
cell costs, representing the highest value in the LIB [13,23]. Most
efforts to improve recycling in an economically viable way have
focused on recovering valuable metals, especially cobalt and
nickel, from the active cathode materials. In addition to cathode
recovery, recycling other materials such as graphite, metal foils,
and electrolytes could expand recycling revenues and maximize
proﬁtability. Three different technologies, used alone or in combination, are employed for recycling LIBs: pyrometallurgical,
hydrometallurgical, and direct recycling processes. The ﬁrst two
recycling processes are commercialized by recovering valuable
metals and salts, whereas direct recycling recovers and reconditions electrode materials for use in new battery production under
development at the lab scale. The working principles for each
recycling strategy as well as recent advances and remaining challenges are reviewed and discussed in this Section. It is noteworthy that pre-treatment of spent LIB, which is discussed under
Section ‘Pre-treatment’, is an extremely important step that
ensures a concentrated feedstock for further hydrometallurgical
treatment as well as direct regeneration. The advantages and disadvantages for each technology are summarized in several review
papers and will be brieﬂy discussed in the following sections
[12,13,20].

Pyrometallurgy is a standard process that has garnered great traction in the area of LIB recycling owing to the advantage of its
straightforward adoption possibilities. A schematic process ﬂow
diagram illustrating this process is provided in Fig. 3. Usually,
pyrometallurgical and hydrometallurgical processes are either
used in combination with other pre-treatments or posttreatments or in sequential order with other processes. Typically,
in this recycling approach, spent LIBs are initially discharged, followed by being dismantled through a series of steps facilitating
the recovery of the electrolyte and separation of the electrodes.
After electrolyte recovery, the LIBs are crushed and screened to
separate the plastics, graphite, and metallic components (Al,
Cu, Fe, and others) [24–27]. Following this step, pyrometallurgy
involving high-temperature smelting is employed to recycle and
recover valuable metals such as Ni, Co, Cu. In certain cases, the
spent LIBs after discharging are directly introduced into a smelt-

These alloys can be further processed using hydrometallurgical

Notably, although the primary focus of most recycling strategies for LIBs has revolved around the recovery of expensive metals such as Co and Ni, the continually growing Li price makes Li
recovery an inevitable prospect. In this context, a major disadvantage of the direct pyrometallurgical smelting approach is
the loss of Li that occurs in high-temperature smelting furnaces
[28]. To overcome this challenge, several research approaches
have been explored in which a combination of the pyro- and
hydrometallurgical processes would create a possibility for
lithium recovery. Some of these efforts include lithium incorporation in slag [28,29], vacuum evaporation [30], and inert atmosphere roasting [31–35]. To a certain extent, these methods
prevent the otherwise lost Li by generating lithium compounds
that can be recovered at a later stage using additional hydrometallurgical processing techniques. Although pyrometallurgy is a
straightforward technique in recovering valuable metals in LIB
recycling, the drawback of effective Li recovery puts this process
at a disadvantage for achieving a closed-loop recycling approach
for LIBs. The process is also unfavourably viewed for the high
capital and operating costs, high energy cost of using hightemperature kilns, and associated detrimental generation of gaseous pollutants [36–39]. Nevertheless, because of the advantages
of process ﬂexibility, long-term proﬁtability, straightforward processing steps, and immediate commercial viability, several companies including Sumitomo-Sony [40], Accurec [41], Akkuser

(Finland) [42] and Umicore (Belgium) [43] have investigated
and in some cases employed pyrometallurgy as the go-to process
to recycle spent LIBs.

Hydrometallurgy
Hydrometallurgical processes employed in the LIB recycling sector are often preceded by initial physical/mechanical separation
or pyrometallurgical processes that prepare the spent LIBs for further processing. In this case, pre-treatment steps followed in the
case of pyrometallurgical processing would be used before beginning hydrometallurgical processes. If a standalone hydrometallurgical process is employed, it often involves several pretreatment processes, which will be discussed in the Section ‘Pre-treatment’. Usually, these processes begin with discharging the spent LIBs and then dismantling and separating,
after which the active materials are separated by using mechanical, and chemical methods [17,18,44]. Contrary to some
pyrometallurgical processes in which the spent LIBs without dismantling are directly subjected to high-temperature processing,
the hydrometallurgical approach is almost always preceded by
pre-treatment protocols [44]. A typical industrial-scale hydrometallurgical process primarily involves leaching, in which metallic
fractions from slags or alloys are dissolved or de-alloyed selectively to facilitate metal recovery from dissolved metal solutions
[18]. The metal solutions are then subjected to separation processes that yield the respective metals such as Co, Ni, Fe, Cu
and Al.
Leaching
Leaching is one of the most commonly used hydrometallurgical
technique for recovering valuable metals from spent LIBs. Most
conventional leaching methodologies involve inorganic acid,

one another. The process ﬂow schematic shown in Fig. 3 illustrates the hydrometallurgical step incorporated in sequence with

such as hydrochloric acid (HCl) [45–50], sulfuric acid (H2SO4)

respective slags/alloys is over 95% [65]. However, the challenges
in recovering low pH leachates and leachates of other metals
such as Al, Cu, and Fe coupled with the generation of detrimental by-products such as Cl2, SOx, and NOx, and the subsequent
process wastewater treatment raise serious concerns regarding
the environmental friendliness of this process [65]. A variant of
the inorganic leaching process involves the use of inorganic
bases such as sodium hydroxide (NaOH) [54,18] and ammonium
hydroxide (NH4OH) [37,66–70] for effective metal leaching from
pretreated metal alloys or metal slags from spent LIBs. Despite
selectivity advantages toward recovering certain metallic constituents, the alkaline leaching process suffers from similar disadvantages to that of the inorganic acid leaching process, in which
the waste processing is a cost-prohibitive step that challenges
safe and viable commercial adoption. Organic acid leaching, on
the other hand, employs low-strength acids such as succinic acid
[71], ascorbic acid [72], citric acid [73–76], aspartic acid [75],
malic acid [75,77], formic acid [78], maleic acid [79], acetic acid
[79], oxalic acid [65], and tartaric acid [80] with or without the
addition of hydrogen peroxide or organic compounds [81].
Although the organic acid leaching does not generate any detrimental by-products that affect the environment, their low
strength, high cost, low leaching rates, and low treatment capacity are some crucial disadvantages that hamper the industrial
prospects of this process [74]. An alternative to the organic acid
leaching is the environmentally friendly deep eutectic solvent
recently reported by Tran et al. to extract metal salts from spent
cathodes [82]. The green solvent made of choline chloride and
ethylene glycol was used to recover more than 90% of cobalt
and lithium based compounds from the LCO cathode.
Another hydrometallurgical leaching process that has garnered great attention is bioleaching, which uses metal-digesting
bacteria. This process has also been extensively explored in
recent years owing to its environmental friendliness, low treatment and recovery demands, and low operational costs when
compared with the inorganic and organic leaching processes
for spent LIBs. Metal-digesting bacteria such as Acidithiobacillus
ferrooxidans [83–87] and fungi such as Aspergillus niger [88–90]
are being extensively investigated in early reports for recycling
LIB metallic components. Despite the cost and environmental
advantages, challenges such as low throughput, low leaching
rates due to slow bio-reaction kinetics, and low operational vol-

Separation and puriﬁcation
The ﬁnal step in the metallurgical recycling process involves the
separation and puriﬁcation of the separated and leached metallic
constituents of the spent LIBs. Commonly used processes for separation involve solvent extraction, chemical precipitation, and
electrochemical deposition. The solvent extraction process is
widely used because of the ion selectivity advantages and high
extraction efﬁciencies (>95%) offered by the multitude of available extractants. One critical disadvantage of this process is the
high upfront cost of extractants and the cost of waste treatment
when considering large-volume operations [99–105].
The chemical precipitation technique is another widely used
technique that exploits the differences in solubility products
between the various metal ion species in the metal leachates.
Although the process involves relatively cost-effective hydroxides and carbonates to precipitate the valuable metals, the pH
sensitivity and operational costs are key factors that require careful attention prior to commercial implementation [106–109].
The electrochemical deposition technique is a widely applied
industrial technique that leverages the differences in electrode
potentials to achieve effective separation of the metallic constituents in metal leachates. Besides the ion selectivity and feasibility advantages offered by electrochemical deposition, the
adoption of this technique for effective separation of metallic
constituents from spent LIBs currently remains relatively unexplored [110–115]. The separated and recovered metallic constituents such as Ni, Co, Fe, Al, and Mn can be incorporated in
their respective salt formulations as raw materials for manufacturing new cathode materials [108,109,116]. Some recovered
metallic species containing Ni, Fe, Al, and Cu can also be converted to their respective metallic forms to be incorporated into
other LIB components such as electrode tabs and current collectors. Some companies that employ one or more of these
hydrometallurgical recycling processes for spent LIBs include
Duesenfeld (Germany) and Recupyl (France) [117].

Direct recycling
A typical direct recycling process is schematically illustrated in
Fig. 4. Direct recycling strategy involves discharging and dismantling, electrolyte recovery, separation of electrode materials from
metal foils and each other, and regeneration of electrode materials. Compared with pyro- and hydrometallurgical processes,
which recover chemical products like metal alloy and salts by
breaking apart the cathode thermally or in solution, direct recycling seeks to reclaim and regenerate the active cathode materials
without affecting their morphology and attempts to reuse them
in new battery production [13,14]. Because cathode production
is the major value-added step with substantial energy embodied
in the material, recovering cathode materials directly in an
energy-efﬁcient and environmentally sustainable manner by
avoiding energy-intensive reﬁning and resynthesis is desirable.
The NMC cathode material is estimated to be around 10 times
more valuable than equivalent amount of pure metals [12].
Life-cycle analysis and process-based cost models have revealed
that direct recycling potentially has both economic beneﬁts
and greenhouse gas emissions reductions over pyrometallurgical
and hydrometallurgical processes [21–23]. As a low-temperature
and low-energy process, direct recycling can be used for recovering manufacturing scraps. Additionally, direct recycling would
be especially beneﬁcial for battery manufacturers who recycle
their own batteries. Many challenges for the direct recycling
approach exist, keeping it at the lab scale. One key barrier for
direct recycling is from the multiplicity of cathode chemistries
because single-cathode input is preferred to recover high-purity
cathode powder. This barrier could somewhat be alleviated by
taking advantage of the limited battery chemistries in EVs to target the known cathode materials. However, reaching singlematerial chemistry requires laborious sorting and separation processes. The fact that the recovered cathode possibly consists of
obsolete formulation also limits its return to market. Another
technical obstacle for the recycling community is how to ensure
both the quality and consistency of the regenerated material to
meet the ever-increasing battery industry requirements in energy
density and cycle life.

Pre-treatment
As shown in Fig. 2, the cell composition of LIBs is complex,
requiring a pre-treatment step to obtain concentrated cathode
feedstocks for the subsequent leaching or direct regeneration
processes. Typically, the pre-treatment step includes discharging,
dismantling and electrolyte recovery. Those processes are important because they determine the recovery yield and chemical
purity of all recovered materials. While the cathode is the main
focus, recovery of other materials is also important as it can provide additional revenue and ensure higher proﬁt margins. A
recent review written by Sommerville et al. summarized the
physical processes used in the pre-treatment of LIBs [44].
Discharging is the critical ﬁrst step in recycling. Discharging
spent cells for stabilization and passivation to prevent thermal
runway can be achieved either through Ohmic discharge using
standard resistors or via immersion in brine solutions [12,44].
However, the state of charge and state of heath of the battery
are difﬁcult to determine without electrochemical charge and
discharge [44]. It is thus important to develop a safe and costeffective discharge platform as well as state of health detection
devices to ensure safety and reliability. Other stabilization methods include cryogenic pre-treatment in liquid nitrogen to cool
down the cells and in-process passivation under CO2 atmosphere
during comminution to limit risks [12,44].
After discharging, the cells are dismantled manually in
research scale or through automated comminution processes
including shredding and milling in pilot or industry scale. Current industrial comminution technologies usually end up with
material outputs which require a complex set of physical and
chemical processes to produce usable materials streams [12].
Besides, the comminution step can produce ﬁne particles of Al
and Cu, which are difﬁcult to separate from electrode coatings
[12]. Those metal contaminations greatly affect the downstream
direct cathode regeneration. It is worth noting that many separation processes are performed at the lab-scale for research purposes, where cell dismantling and electrodes separation and
cutting are all done manually. This raises a question of whether
those lab-scale separation processes can be scaled up under much

Electrolyte systems in LIBs are typically made of a lithium salt,
LiPF6, dissolved in a mixture of linear carbonate solvents (e.g.
dimethyl carbonate and ethyl methyl carbonate) and ethylene
carbonate [118]. Electrolyte additives for speciﬁc electrode passivation properties can also be present. Hence, the recycling of
electrolytes is not only important for augmenting proﬁt margins
but also necessary to separate liquids from solids in the cells.
Nevertheless, electrolytes and additives are ﬂammable and toxic
and can decompose into ﬂuorinated compounds and possibly
carcinogenic fumes [12,119]. In lab-scale research, wet electrodes
are typically immersed in dimethyl carbonate to wash off electrolytes [120,121]. On a larger scale, several techniques such as
vacuum pyrolysis and supercritical extraction have been used
[57,119]. Sloop et al. introduced the extraction of electrolytes
via supercritical ﬂuids among other studies by Nowak et al.
[118,119,122]. Liu et al. used supercritical CO2 extraction, resin,
and molecular sieve puriﬁcation which had led to the recovery of
electrolytes with decent ionic conductivity and electrochemical
performance [123]. While supercritical CO2 extraction has the
advantage of use either before or after shredding, the associated
high cost and low processing capacity limit its industrial application [119,122–124]. In general, contamination from degraded
products and proprietary additives have limited the potential of
electrolyte and salt recovery [14,125].
After extraction of electrolytes, multiple separation processes
including sieving, magnetic separation, and density separation
are exploited to remove steel casings, separators, and plastics,
producing a mixture of battery materials including cathode and
anode coatings on their current collectors. One major target for
the pre-treatment processes is to reclaim the degraded cathode
powder for the subsequent step of direct cathode regeneration.
Three general stages exist to achieve active cathode materials
with a single chemistry. The ﬁrst step is separation between electrode materials and metal foils because the active electrode materials are coated onto current collectors through organic binders
such as polyvinylidene ﬂuoride (PVDF) and carboxymethyl cellulose. The next step is set to separate cathode materials from the
anode part as they are mixed during the shredding process. Additionally, the cathode powders should be separated from the conductive additives like carbon black. Third, because of multiple
cathode chemistries from the input, a cathode/cathode separation process is necessary to reach single cathode chemistry. This
process may be avoided if the battery input in the shredding process is of single-cathode chemistry, which requires rigorous sorting and separation processes. Notably, those separation stages are
not isolated: the better separation in the earlier steps, the more
convenient for the following ones.
Cathode materials are tightly bonded to the Al foils by PVDF.
To separate electrode materials from Al foils, the polymeric binder
(PVDF) needs to be eliminated. Several approaches including the
use of a solvent—such as N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) or
dimethylformamide (DMF) to dissolve PVDF and thermal treatment to decompose PVDF—have been reported [126–128]. How310

the detachment of cathode coatings from Al foils in reaction
media such as molten salt, deep eutectic solvent, and ethylene glycol [121,131–133]. Ethylene glycol has been used to delaminate
cathode ﬁlms from Al foil in seconds without altering the crystal
structure and morphology of active cathode materials nor corroding the Al foil [121]. However, the PVDF binders were not
removed in those studies, which may not be an issue for the
hydrometallurgical process but remains problematic for direct
recycling. Another different pre-processing target between direct
recycling and hydrometallurgical processes is that the former
requires no or small damages to the active cathode materials to
save efforts on direct cathode regeneration. Thus, it requires a
cost-effective and environmentally friendly method to eliminate
the binder without signiﬁcantly affecting the active materials.
Moreover, recovering current collectors such as Cu and Al are critical because Cu smelting processes are the key contributors to
emissions of SOx and other greenhouse gases in the cradle-togate stages of the battery life cycle, suggesting that dissolving Al
in an alkaline solution is not a sustainable separation method [22].
In many reports, the separation of cathode and anode was
achieved manually after spent cells were disassembled by hand,
which is not applicable on the industrial level. Spent cells are
typically shredded, producing a mixture of battery materials.
Puriﬁcation of active cathode materials from the mixture is a critical step toward highly pure cathode powders. One puriﬁcation
methodology is froth ﬂotation, which can selectively separate
hydrophobic
materials
from
hydrophilic
materials
[124,134,135]. By choosing kerosene as the collector, researchers
demonstrated that over 90% of spent graphite, which is
hydrophobic, ﬂoated in froth layers and that a tailing product
having 88.2% active cathode materials was obtained at a total
recovery of 74.3%, suggesting the effectiveness of the froth ﬂotation process for puriﬁcation [134]. Notably, avoiding the puriﬁcation step is possible, yet challenging, by separating electrode
sheets of cathode and anode prior to cell shredding in an automated and scalable way.
The ideal scenario for easily and quickly reclaiming singlechemistry cathode materials is to ensure an input of spent cells
with a single-cathode chemistry. However, handling a massive
number of spent cells poses a barrier for efﬁcient sorting. Moreover, some battery manufacturers use mixed cathode materials
in their cells, further complicating the issue. Therefore, a cathode/cathode separation process seems requisite to achieve
single-cathode chemistry. However, so far, few reports face this
challenge. One potential separation approach seeks to use the
differences in magnetic properties to separate different cathode
materials [136].

Direct cathode regeneration
Cathode recycling is in the spotlight of battery recycling because
of the high value of the cathode materials. Direct cathode regeneration seeks to recover the functional cathode particles without
decomposition into substituent metals or salts. Although little
value exists for recycling cathode materials of LFP and LMO
through pyrometallurgical or hydrometallurgical processes,
direct recycling can recover those cathode materials. Both Li loss
due to thickening of the solid-electrolyte interface and phase
changes on the cathode particle surface contribute to cathode
capacity degradation. To regenerate or repair those degraded
cathode materials, several methods have been demonstrated to
restore Li back into the cathode structures, including solid-state
sintering, hydrothermal processes in combination with sintering, electrochemical and chemical processes [137]. Solid-state
relithiation is the most straightforward method to restore Li from
deﬁciency. After determining the amount of Li loss, one can calcine the degraded cathode powder with the addition of the stoichiometric amount of Li precursor (e.g., Li2CO3) to produce new
cathode powder [25,139]. For example, spent LCO was renovated
by thermal treatment with Li2CO3 at 900 °C for 12 h and the renovated cathode material delivered a discharge capacity of
152 mAh/g, which is close to commercial LCO [138]. LFP cathode was also directly regenerated by sintering it with Li2CO3 at
650 °C for 1 h under Ar/H2 ﬂow [139].
In addition to solid-state sintering, Li can also be restored in
an electrochemical way. Using Li metal or prelithiated graphite
as the counter electrode, the degraded cathode could be regenerated in a half-cell conﬁguration [140,141]. However, feasibility in
large-scale manufacturing is challenging because of the high
chemical reactivity of the counter electrode. Therefore, it is practical to develop a scalable roll-to-roll electrochemical bath for
direct relithiating degraded cathode materials. Hydrothermal
processing followed by heat treatment is another promising
approach for direct cathode regeneration [124,142–145]. In this
process, the degraded cathode powder is ﬁrst hydrothermally
treated in a Li aqueous solution in a pressure vessel, during
which the Li is reintroduced into the cathode structure. The following brief annealing process enhances the structure restoration by reconstructing the desired crystallinity of the material.
Compared with solid-state sintering, the hydrothermal process
does not require the stoichiometric addition of Li because this
solution-based healing process is self-limiting in the restoration
of Li. This process has been successfully demonstrated for several
cathode chemistries, including LCO, NMC532, NMC111, and
NMC622 [124,143–145]. Those regenerated cathode samples performed similarly to their baseline counterparts with high capacity, good cycling stability, and high rate performance. OnTo
Technology (Bend, Oregon, USA) recycles cathode materials
directly through a hydrothermal process and additional heat
treatment [124].

Design for recycling and prospects of Battery Identity
Global Passport
As discussed in the previous sections, many challenges complicate recycling LIBs. However, without batteries being intentionally designed for recycling, those challenges will remain.

Prescient product design could help increase recyclability of
future LIBs by addressing the separation of battery components
[146]. Designing batteries by considering their ultimate purpose
can improve recyclability. A simple design change may have the
potential to greatly affect the ease of separation and recovery.
To foster product design, ﬁnancial subsidies and nonﬁnancial
incentives could boost new inventions and quick adoption of better designs in favour of repurposing and recycling. In addition to
the regulatory policies, battery manufacturers need to be proactive by actively incorporating design-for-recycling principles into
their products and manufacturing processes. In Fig. 5, the authors
provide a few insights on the concept of design for recycling from
different levels to facilitate future battery recycling.
One challenge for LIB recycling is how to efﬁciently sort batteries of different chemistries to avoid or at least reduce the complicated separation processes. A simple yet effective design
principle would be appropriate labelling in many forms, such
as labels, QR codes, and RfID tags. The Battery Recycling Committee of the Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE) developed
a label to be placed on EV battery packs to enable separate processing of different battery types [13]. Another example is that
Battery Recycling Prize from United States Department of Energy
funded Everledger to trace the lifecycle of LIBs using blockchain
technology to ensure optimal management and responsible
recovery at end-of life [147]. As illustrated in Fig. 5a, scanning
those labels automatically would facilitate sorting batteries, circumventing the abovementioned challenging separation processes. Given as much information as possible, recyclers can
take less effort to identify the electrode chemistry [12,13]. A
recent report from the World Economic Forum and the Global
Battery Alliance suggests that battery diagnostic and shared data
systems or battery passports should be established, especially for
EV batteries [148]. The development of such a system embedded
within batteries or as separate tools could provide key information including battery chemistry, origin, the state of health of
the batteries, and the chain of custody that can be captured by
the battery recyclers at the recycling facilities. As illustrated in
Fig. 5a, Battery Identify Global Passports (BIGP) will help to efﬁciently unveil the identity of the components in the cells to support quick and automated sorting, and hence could lead to
streamlined separations. A second-life application sector could
use the BIGP information of a battery to enable effective selection and assessment of suitable batteries for reuse. Also, the BIGP
can make information on cathode compositions and electrolyte
formulations available to recyclers. This might be challenging
because manufacturers may not want to share their proprietary
formulations. It is thus important for all stakeholders and policymakers to collaborate and make agreements between battery
manufacturers and recyclers.
The economic efﬁciency of battery recycling could be boosted
through automation, which would also lead to enhanced separation of battery components [12]. However, current batteries are
not designed for easy disassembly. Designing products without
using permanent assembly methods would ease the dismantling
process. Manufacturers should be encouraged to consider standardizing their cell and pack construction, at least in limited
designs, allowing the adoption of simple and automated disassembly processes.

In addition to being explored on pack and module levels,

Research directions may pursue alternative binder developments

highly challenging. New designs for cells are being explored that
will enable them to be ﬂushed at end-of-life and reﬁlled for use as
reconstituted components in new batteries. To aid this effort,
new cell geometries can be designed and tested to enable the
ﬂush-and-ﬁll concept [121].

Conclusion and outlook
The rise of EVs, energy storage, and increasingly power-hungry
electronic devices poses challenges for end-of-life battery management. Recycling LIBs is necessary to support environmental,
resources and economics in general, and sustainability to close
the loop on battery materials and chart a sustainable circular
economy for LIBs. Many beneﬁts for recycling LIBs exist, but
technical, logistics, economic, and regulatory barriers must be
resolved. Lessons should be learned from the success of leadacid battery recycling. Three recycling methods—pyrometallurgi
cal, hydrometallurgical, and direct recycling processes—are being
developed and improved for recycling LIBs. Compared with the
312

former two processes, direct recycling provides the opportunity
to avoid energy-intensive reﬁning and reproduction of the cathode materials, which could further reduce the CO2 footprint of
LIBs. However, although direct recycling could have the lowest
environmental impacts, more research is still needed to validate
the results obtained from small bench scale experiments. With
more concerted engagement from the research community, the
Li-ion battery recycling technology readiness levels can move
on a faster pace towards pilot scale deployment and
commercialization:
1. Concerted efforts among academia—industries including battery manufacturers, original equipment manufacturers, and
battery recycling companies, governments, and end-users—
are critical to stimulate and enable economically viable processes for recycling LIBs, which require coordinated, immediate actions by companies, investors, and policymakers in
consultation with all stakeholders. Incentivizing LIB recycling
would foster innovation in industrial sectors as more groups
seek to make the process economically viable and environmentally friendly [152]. For example, the US Department of Energy
announced the Recycling Prize in 2019, aiming to motivate
innovators to develop and demonstrate proﬁtable business
and technology strategies to achieve a LIB recovery rate of 90%.
2. No one-size-ﬁts-all solution exists for the challenges of recycling LIBs. Each recycling technology targets different endproducts and has its own advantages and limitations. Combinations of different recycling methods can help move forward
the development of cost-effective recycling processes. For
example, the slag from a smelting process consists of Li, which
may be recycled through leaching and puriﬁcation processes.
Separation processes developed for the direct recycling
approach could also be used for the hydrometallurgical
strategy.

3. Automation in sorting and disassembling batteries is preferred
to protect human operators and reduce costs. Automation in
sorting batteries with the help of artiﬁcial intelligence
research could save time and efforts for down-stream separation processes. If the electrode sheets of cathode and anode
could be separated in an automated way, several separation
processes—including separating Al foils from Cu foils and
active cathode materials from anode materials—could be circumvented, which would reduce recycling costs and avoid
contaminations.
4. The direct recycling process is still at the lab scale and many
research activities are under way. One limitation for direct
recycling is that the regenerated cathode may be out-of-date
because of the rapid development of new cathode formulations. How to upgrade the cathode chemistry (e.g., from
NMC111 to NMC811) remains a challenging research opportunity. In addition to direct cathode recycling, more focus
needs to be centred on recovering other materials to expand
the recycling revenues and to support the circular economy
of LIBs. For example, direct regeneration of anode materials
could be a possible research direction to recover more values
from spent LIBs [153,154].
5. The lab-scale technologies often face challenges of scaling up
because of high capital costs, high energy consumption, and
environmental issues. Evaluating the recycling cost of a process and identifying its economic and environment viability
are critical. The new EverBatt model, as an example, has been
developed to evaluate cost and environmental effects for the
various lifecycle stages of LIBs [155]. The model can be used
to compare the effects of virgin batteries to those with recycled content and to compare processes.
6. Battery technologies are evolving at a rapid pace. Recycling
techniques should keep pace with the ever-evolving battery
developments. The rapid improvements in performance and
costs in existing technologies are insufﬁcient to meet the
requirements of the market, which means that nextgeneration innovative technologies such as solid-state batteries are required to deliver the change in performance. In this
regard, next-generation innovative recycling technologies
should be targeted for battery technology [20]. Additionally,
new technologies such as solid-state batteries need to be
designed for recycling [156].
7. As all solid-state batteries are believed to be the next endeavour in the realm of battery technologies, it is important to discuss the recycling prospects of such systems. Considering the
future scenario where there are more and more all solid-state
batteries nearing end of life, it is worth questioning whether
the existing strategies proposed for recycling current battery
systems would remain relevant. Moreover, in all solid-state
batteries, the choice of anode is lithium metal as opposed to
graphite in present day batteries. This poses serious safety
challenges if techniques such as pyro- or hydrometallurgy
are directly employed without new pre-treatment steps. Also,
the use of solid electrolytes and cathode-electrolyte architectures creates new challenges and barriers to present day recycling approaches. These challenges also offer new possibilities
and require new recycling strategies, for example; the lithium
metal anodes can possibly be recovered from end of life all

solid-state cells by implementing a pre-heating step to
200 °C, above the melting point of lithium metal. Such
approaches would have to be integrated with current recycling techniques such as pyro/hydrometallurgy or direct recycling strategies to effectively recycle all solid-state batteries of
the future.
8. All-in-all, the concept of Battery Identity Global Passport
(BIGP) could signiﬁcantly ease the pain and be materialized
through a digital chip comprising information that identiﬁes
the battery at the cradle (manufacturers) and at the gate (recyclers). Obviously, such a concept will have to be globally
implemented, otherwise, the global battery ﬂow, being so
complex, may jeopardize the chances to recycle Li-ion batteries in responsible and proﬁtable ways.
