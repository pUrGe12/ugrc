Tumor metastasis is considered the primary cause of therapeutic failure in cancer patients. Small interfering RNA
(siRNA) therapy, which has the ability to target and silence the aberrant expression of tumor oncogenes, holds
significant potential for improving therapeutic efficacy against metastatic cancer. Despite the advancement of
several siRNA-based therapeutics into clinical trials, their clinical efficacy remains suboptimal. This is partly
attributed to the multiple extracellular and intracellular barriers that hinder the efficient delivery and release of
siRNA drugs at tumor sites. Nanoparticle-based delivery systems have emerged as a promising strategy to
overcome these obstacles by enhancing siRNA stability, improving pharmacokinetics and biodistribution pro­
files, and facilitating targeted delivery and controlled release of siRNA within tumor tissues. Herein, this review
begins with a systematic discussion of the biological processes of tumor metastasis and the influence of the tumor
microenvironment (TME) on the in vivo fate of nanomedicines. It then summarizes the current clinical research
status of siRNA nanomedicines for cancer therapy. The discussion focused on description of siRNA delivery via
nanotechnology in treating tumor metastasis, emphasizing the targeted delivery and controlled release mecha­
nisms. At last, the challenges encountered in clinical translation are considered.

Overall, although lipid-based nanocarriers remain the mainstream
platform for systemic siRNA delivery, diversified strategies, such as
ligand conjugation and exosomes, are gradually expanding the clinical
translation pathways for siRNA therapy. Target selection is also broad­
ening from traditional oncogenes to emerging areas, including immune
checkpoints, metabolic enzymes, and transcription factors.
Among these, siG12D LODER, the first intratumoral siRNA nano­
therapeutic to enter clinical evaluation in 2011, utilizes a biodegradable
LODER polymer matrix to enable sustained local delivery of anti-KRAS
siRNA (siG12D). A Phase II trial initiated in 2020 enrolled 59 patients
with locally advanced pancreatic cancer (LAPC) across two cohorts:
Cohort 1 randomized patients to receive siG12D LODER plus
gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel (Group 1) and chemotherapy alone (Group
2), Cohort 2 received siG12D LODER plus standard chemotherapy
(mFOLFIRINOX or gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel). The primary endpoints
were overall survival (OS) for Cohort 1 and objective response rate
(ORR) for Cohort 2. In Cohort 1, the median OS in the modified
intention-to-treat (mITT) population without KRAS status screening was
7 months for Group 1 and 21.9 months for Group 2. Among patients with
KRAS G12D/V mutations, OS was 22.7 months and 13.4 months,
respectively. In Cohort 2, the ORR was 31.6% in the mITT population
and reached 57.1% in the KRAS G12D/V subgroup. These findings
warrant further investigation of siG12D LODER in LAPC patients
harboring KRAS G12D/V mutations [10].
Several early-stage drug candidates had their trials terminated due to
efficacy and toxicity issues. For example, DCR-MYC, the first MYCtargeted, LNP-encapsulated siRNA drug to enter clinical trials, under­
went two Phase I trials (DCR-MYC-101 and DCR-MYC-102) that pre­
liminarily validated its mechanism of action and demonstrated
acceptable safety. However, clinical development of the drug was offi­
cially terminated in September 2016 for two main reasons: First, the
reduction in MYC expression was insufficient to produce meaningful
clinical effects, and no significant antitumor activity was observed in
hepatocellular carcinoma or other solid tumors. Second, a 70-year-old
patient with metastatic breast cancer developed progressive renal fail­
ure five months after starting treatment. A renal biopsy confirmed
thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Renal function partially recovered
following discontinuation of the drug. The clinical and pathological
changes were highly correlated with the initiation and discontinuation
of DCR-MYC treatment. Investigators concluded that the most likely
cause of the progressive renal failure was the anti-MYC siRNA therapy
[11]. Collectively, these cases suggest that efficacy and off-target
toxicity are the two key factors in the clinical translation of antitumor
siRNA nanomedicines.

Although these siRNA-based anticancer nanomedicines remain in the
early stages of clinical trials, nanocarrier-mediated siRNA delivery has
achieved landmark breakthroughs in non-oncology indications. In 2018,
Patisiran (Onpattro®) obtained FDA approval, becoming the first siRNA
therapy to reach the market and also the first LNP drug approved by the
FDA [12]. The success of Patisiran validated the clinical feasibility of
LNPs as a platform for systemic siRNA delivery.
3. Tumor metastasis and nanomedicine
3.1. The cascade of tumor metastasis
Metastasis refers to the spread of tumor cells from one organ to
another and is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths. The biological
process of tumor metastasis, illustrated in Fig. 1, primarily involves five
critical steps: local invasion at the primary tumor site, intravasation into
the circulatory system, survival in circulation, extravasation into distant
organs, and colonization through adaptation to the new microenviron­
ment [13].
Tumor cells acquire stem cell characteristics and invasive abilities
through the process of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), which
enable their detachment from the primary site [14]. The vascular
structures formed by cancer cells are often incomplete, highly tortuous,
and prone to leakage [15]. These features provide the necessary con­
ditions for CTCs to extravasate into tissues. CTCs protect themselves by
binding to platelets to form tumor emboli. This strategy not only helps
them resist hemodynamic shear forces but also allows them to utilize
platelet-derived growth factors and transforming growth factor-beta
(TGF-β) [16]. These factors promote the EMT process, maintain inva­
siveness, and protect tumor cells from immune surveillance by natural
killer (NK) cells [17]. Extravasation is the process by which CTCs
penetrate the vascular walls of distant organs and enter the tissue pa­
renchyma. This enables direct contact with the target organ and pre­
pares for distant colonization. The majority of tumor cells that
successfully cross the vessel wall enter a dormant state, evading immune
elimination by suppressing immune ligands. Reactivation of these
dormant cells occurs only when the distant microenvironment un­
dergoes remodeling, often mediated by inflammatory signals or
myeloid-derived suppressor cells, triggering active proliferation and
forming lethal macro-metastases [18].
3.2. The tumor microenvironment affects the efficacy of nanomedicines
The TME is a complex ecosystem composed of tumor cells, stromal
cells, immune cells, the extracellular matrix (ECM), and various soluble
factors (cytokines, chemokines, growth factors) and metabolites. Ab­
normalities in its structure and function not only drive tumor progres­
sion and metastasis but also serve as significant barriers that limit the
effectiveness of nanomedicine delivery.
3.2.1. Physical barrier
The tumor metastasis microenvironment is the local tissue environ­
ment that facilitates the spread of tumor cells and their distant coloni­
zation. The multilayered physical barriers formed by the tumor
metastasis microenvironment severely hinder the effective delivery of
nanodrugs. The dense ECM is its central component. The ECM,
composed of large amounts of collagen, fibronectin, hyaluronic acid
(HA), and other components secreted by cancer-associated fibroblasts
(CAFs), forms a dense fibrotic matrix that increases tissue stiffness and
severely impedes the physical diffusion of nanodrugs and immune cells
[19]. High interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) is another key physical bar­
rier. Abnormally elevated IFP in solid tumors generates outward
convective forces that severely restrict the deep penetration of nano­
particles [20]. In addition, tumor blood vessels exhibit morphological
characteristics such as disordered branching and unstable endothelial
junctions [21]. Although these vessels are permeable, their permeability

3.2.2. Immune evasion
The immunosuppressive barrier is another major barrier of the TME.
The most fundamental characteristic of the tumor immune microenvi­
ronment (TIME) is the formation of a network of immunosuppressive
cells. The TIME contains various immune cells, such as tumor-associated
macrophages (TAMs), NK cells, dendritic cells (DCs), tumor-associated
neutrophils (TANs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and T
cells [23]. TAMs typically exhibit M2-type polarization. Through the
secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β) and various
growth factors, they synergistically promote tumor proliferation,
angiogenesis, and immune evasion [24]. In contrast, T cells and NK cells,
which possess intrinsic cytotoxicity under physiological conditions,
become functionally impaired within the TME due to immunosuppres­
sive signals such as the PD-L1/PD-1 axis, resulting in decreased anti­
tumor activity [25,26]. In the TIME, DCs often exhibit reduced antigen
uptake and processing capacity, and are disrupted by inhibitory cyto­
kines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, as well as metabolic stress, thereby
diminishing the efficacy of immunotherapy [27]. TANs promote tumor
metastasis by releasing a network-like structure that captures circulating
tumor cells, promotes TIME, and reactivates dormant cancer cells [28].
MDSCs proliferate rapidly during tumorigenesis and promote regulatory
T cell (Treg) development by secreting TGF-β, IL-10, and immunosup­
pressive enzymes such as ARG1, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and
inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS). They also enhance the survival of
CTCs, promote EMT and the expression of matrix metalloproteinases
(MMPs), thereby creating a metastasis-promoting microenvironment
[29]. These multifaceted mechanisms enable tumor cells to escape im­
mune detection, which in turn promotes tumor progression and recur­
rence, presenting a major challenge for nanomedicine.

3.2.4. Chronic inflammation
Chronic inflammation within the TME acts as a double-edged sword,
exerting dual regulatory effects on tumor development. On the protumor
side, inflammatory cells produce cytokines and chemokines, including
interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), which stimu­
late tumor proliferation, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix remod­
eling [32]. On the antitumor side, certain inflammatory mediators are
theoretically capable of activating antitumor immune responses. How­
ever, within the actual TME, inflammation generally skews toward a
protumor direction [33]. For example, TAMs and neutrophils promote
tumor invasion and immunosuppression by secreting pro-tumor in­
flammatory mediators [34]. Collectively, chronic inflammation not only
fuels tumor initiation and progression but also constitutes a critical
barrier to effective cancer treatment.
In summary, TME-associated physical barrier, immune, metabolic,
and inflammatory abnormalities collectively foster tumor progression
and therapeutic resistance, underscoring the need to develop rationally
designed nanocarrier systems.
3.3. Nanomedicine for the regulation of tumor metastasis
3.3.1. Intervention strategies targeting the metastasis process
Tumor metastasis is a complex, multi-step process, and nano­
medicines can be rationally designed to intervene at specific stages
along this cascade. They can also inhibit cancer cell proliferation,
migration, and invasion by blocking EMT [35]. During the local invasion
phase, nanomedicines are able to be engineered to suppress tumor cell
migration through the blockade of key signaling pathways such as TGFβ/Smad, PI3K/AKT, and Wnt/β-catenin [36]. Nanomedicines have the
ability to inhibit the adhesion and activation of platelets and CTCs,
thereby disrupting the survival-promoting protection that platelets
provide to CTCs and weakening their ability to resist anaplastic
apoptosis [37]. After extravasation, nanomedicines with active targeting
capabilities can precisely eliminate disseminated cancer stem cells
(CSCs), effectively eradicating established CSCs and suppressing
metastasis [38]. Furthermore, nanomedicines are capable of disrupting

rapid proliferation of tumor cells exacerbates local oxygen consumption,
thereby inducing a hypoxic microenvironment. In response, tumor cells
upregulate hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), which subsequently pro­
mote angiogenesis and metabolic reprogramming [30]. Even under
aerobic conditions, tumor cells preferentially undergo glycolysis, pro­
ducing large amounts of lactate. Hypoxia further exacerbates proton
accumulation, lowering the extracellular pH to 6.5–6.8 [31]. This acidic

3.3.2. Targeting the tumor microenvironment
In addition to directly affecting the metastasis process, nano­
medicines can also indirectly inhibit metastasis by reshaping the TME. A
dense ECM and abnormal vascular structures constitute the primary
physical barriers that limit the penetration of nanomedicines. Designing
nanomedicines capable of degrading the ECM can reduce interstitial
pressure and open pathways for the penetration of drugs and immune
cells [40]. To improve therapeutic precision, these nanomedicines can
be designed as a multi-stimulus-responsive system to pH, MMPs, and
ROS. This enables localized ECM degradation and the time-controlled
release of nanodrugs, thereby enhancing therapeutic accuracy [41].
Nanomedicines can also indirectly lower IFP via vascular normalization.
By modulating angiogenesis, they restore vascular integrity, improve
perfusion, reduce IFP, and consequently boost nanomedicine delivery
efficiency [42].
The inhibitory polarization or functional exhaustion of various im­
mune cells within the TIME constitutes a core immunological barrier
that impedes antitumor immune responses. Through precise design,
nanomedicines can achieve targeted regulation of key immune cell
subsets within the TIME. Nanomedicines can regulate TAMs by blocking
monocyte recruitment and reprogramming TAMs phenotypes, thereby
improving intratumoral drug accumulation [43]. As multifunctional
delivery platforms, nanoparticles can achieve subpopulation-specific
targeting of immune cells through surface modification, they can also
enhance antigen presentation and T-cell activation capabilities through
co-delivery strategies [44]. By coating nanoparticles with cell mem­
branes to form biomimetic nanoplatforms, researchers can realize spe­
cific tumor recognition [45,46]. In addition, by leveraging the high
migratory capacity, chemotaxis, and ability of neutrophils to cross the
blood-brain barrier, these cells can serve as living cell carriers for anti­
tumor nanoparticles, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy and
reducing off-target toxicity [47].
Traditional therapies are often hindered in their ability to effectively,
specifically, and deeply combat tumor metastasis due to tumor hetero­
geneity, phenotypic plasticity, the various barrier, and the presence of
dormant cells. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop rationally
designed nanocarrier systems, particularly focusing on targeted delivery
and responsive release. These nanocarriers should enable synergistic
multi-drug delivery, microenvironment-responsive release, and active
targeting ability. This strategy endows drugs with selective delivery
capabilities and controlled release properties, and provides an inte­
grated solution for various therapeutic modalities.

4.1.1. Lipid-based nanoparticles
Lipid-based nanoparticles serve as a primary delivery system for
RNA-based therapeutics, including siRNA, mRNA, and CRISPR/Cas
genome-editing components [51]. These nanoparticles are broadly
classified as either liposomes or LNPs. In both, cationic or ionizable
lipids electrostatically complex with negatively charged siRNA, fol­
lowed by co-assembly with phospholipids to yield stable nanocarriers.
These nanoparticles protect inherently unstable RNA molecules from
rapid nuclease degradation, prolong circulation time, enhance cellular
uptake, and enable efficient intracellular release [52]. For example,
Zhang et al. [53] developed a cationic liposome delivery system to
deliver siRNA targeting the methyl-CpG-binding domain protein 2
(MBD2) gene to inhibit tumor metastasis. MBD2 is a “reader” protein
that interprets DNA methylation information [54], which plays a crucial
role in tumor metastasis. The liposome achieves passive targeted de­

cationic adsorption to the tumor cell membrane. In vitro, this liposome

the downstream tumor suppressor gene damage specific DNA binding
protein 2 (DDB2), and effectively inhibits the EMT process. Conse­

4.1.3. Extracellular vesicles
Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are nanoscale particles actively secreted
by cells. As important mediators of intercellular communication, they
play a critical role in maintaining physiological homeostasis and
participating in pathological processes [77]. Compared to traditional
nanodrug delivery systems, EVs offer unique advantages due to their
inherent biocompatibility, stability, barrier-penetrating ability, and
homing properties [78]. An increasing body of research confirms that
EVs can efficiently deliver a variety of cargoes, including microRNAs,
siRNAs, messenger RNAs, plasmids, and antisense oligonucleotides, for
the targeted treatment of various diseases [79].
Fu et al. [80] induced the liver to continuously produce and secrete
native sEVs loaded with VEGFR2 and mTOR siRNA by intravenously
injecting naked DNA plasmids. These siRNA-loaded sEVs are delivered
via the bloodstream to the primary tumor and lung metastases, reducing
primary tumor size and lung metastases while prolonging survival. In a
ccRCC model, the sEVs exhibit a 71% reduction in lung metastasis
fluorescence intensity, and median survival is extended from 23 to 62
days. In a post-nephrectomy model, the incidence of lung metastasis is
reduced by 99%, significantly outperforming conventional combination
therapy (sunitinib + everolimus). Wen et al. [81] used low-frequency
ultrasound to stimulate tumor cells, producing ultrasound-engineered
tumor-derived exosomes (US-EV) for the co-delivery of hematoporphy­
rin monomethyl ether (HMME) and YTHDF1 siRNA (EV-H/S), which
increased yield approximately 3.4-fold. In a bilateral tumor model, EVH/S reduces primary tumor size by 64.8% and distant metastases by
77.4%. In a CRC liver metastasis model, it almost completely suppresses
liver metastasis and nearly doubles median survival time. Additionally,
Chen et al. [82] encapsulated siTEAD4-loaded MOF nanozymes into
exhausted CD8+ T cell-derived EVs (siT/MOF@EVs). Surface PD-1/TIM3/TIGIT on these EVs competitively binds PD-L1/CD155/PtdSer on
tumor cells, blocking three immune checkpoints. In 4T1 models, siT/
MOF@EVs reduces lung metastases, CSCs, and exhausted T cells, while
increasing CD8+ T cells and the M1/M2 macrophage ratio, thereby
remodeling the immune microenvironment.
Exosomes, a type of EVs, are small extracellular vesicles with a
phospholipid bilayer, approximately 30–150 nm in diameter, actively
secreted by both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells [83]. They function as
natural extracellular carriers involved in intercellular communication
and signal transduction. After release from donor cells, these vesicles
can be internalized by neighboring or distant recipient cells through
endocytosis, thereby facilitating the effective transmission of critical
biological information [84]. Furthermore, exosomes exhibit low
toxicity, favorable biocompatibility and excellent membrane penetra­
tion capabilities [85]. Collectively, these properties highlight the
promising potential of exosomes for the passive targeted delivery of
siRNA.
Liu et al. [86] constructed a HEK293T cells-derived exosome-based
carrier loaded with DARS-AS1 siRNA, termed siDARS-AS1-EXOs.

chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS), to inhibit breast cancer liver
metastasis (BCLM). The exosomes leverage their natural biocompati­

targeted delivery to primary triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) le­

up by TNBC cells and significantly inhibited cell migration, reducing it

4.1.2. Polymeric nanoparticles
Polymeric nanoparticles have attracted widespread attention in
siRNA delivery, primarily due to their low toxicity, tunable chemical
composition, high stability, long circulation time, and controlled-release
characteristics [65]. Cationic polymers are widely used in nucleic acid
delivery. They have the ability to form stable complexes with the
negatively charged phosphate groups of siRNA through electrostatic
interactions [66]. And, their unique “proton sponge effect” promotes
endosomal escape to enhance anti-tumor efficacy [67].
Sun et al. [68] constructed polymeric nanoparticles loaded with
siSLITRK4 (termed siSLITRK4-NPs) to target SLIT- and NTRK-like family
member 4 (SLITRK4), a key driver gene in colorectal cancer (CRC) liver
metastasis. The nanocarrier is formed through a one-step nano­
precipitation process, in which siSLITRK4 self-assembled with mPEG5KPLGA15K and the cationic lipid G0-C14, achieving an encapsulation
efficiency of 48%. The PEG shell prolongs the blood circulation time,
enabling passive targeting of the tumor site through the EPR effect.
Overexpression of SLITRK4 promotes the infiltration of CD206+
CD11b+ TAMs in CRC liver metastases by activating the PI3K/AKT/NFκB signaling pathway. The siSLITRK4-NPs efficiently silence SLITRK4
expression in tumor tissues and significantly suppress subcutaneous
colorectal tumor growth in vivo in a dose-dependent manner. Unlike the
approach used by Sun et al., who employed a single-step nano­
precipitation method, Wang et al. [69] prepared inhalable core-shell
hybrid nanoparticles (HNPs) using a two-step microfluidic method.
DOTAP/DOPE cationic liposomes are first combined with siRNA to form
a lipid core, which is then coated with a rigid PLGA shell. In a lung
cancer model, the siKRAS + siTGF HNPs group demonstrates significant
anti-metastatic effects: the number of metastatic nodules in the right
lung decreases by 69.8%; the diameter of metastatic lesions decreases by
71.8%; extrapulmonary metastases to the thoracic wall and medias­
tinum decline by 86.0%; and the number of nodules on the tumor sur­
face decreases by 78.6%.
Polymer nanoparticles offer several inherent advantages over other
non-viral carriers. Their molecular-level structural tunability enables
high drug-loading capacity, protects siRNA from nuclease degradation,
and allows the incorporation of stimulus-responsive groups to achieve
controlled release at the target site [70,71]. Furthermore, surface
functionalization with targeting ligands can promote active tumor
homing and cell-specific uptake [70]. However, several limitations
hinder their clinical translation. Although LNPs can be produced using
relatively straightforward microfluidic mixing, some polymeric nano­
particles require more extensive and complex fabrication techniques,
such as emulsion solvent evaporation or electro spraying [72]. Secondly,
the preparation of polymeric nanoparticles may involve emulsifiers such
as polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), which could raise toxicity concerns [73].
Third, the formation of a protein coat in biological fluids compromises
targeting accuracy and alters biodistribution [74]. The complex rela­
tionship between the physicochemical properties of polymers (molecu­
lar weight, charge, hydrophobicity) and their biological interactions in
vivo further complicates rational design and batch-to-batch

In summary, compared to synthetically produced lipid nanoparticles,
EVs possess the inherent advantages of low immunogenicity and high
biocompatibility. They can be derived from a variety of cell types, and
their lipid bilayer structure effectively protects nucleic acid drugs from
degradation [87]. Furthermore, EVs can be engineered using various
strategies to encapsulate siRNA, thereby enabling highly efficient, tar­
geted gene silencing in both primary tumors and metastatic sites.
However, the clinical translation of EVs as siRNA carriers still faces
numerous challenges. First, insufficient drug-loading efficiency.
Although existing methods such as electroporation and ultrasonic
treatment can increase drug loading capacity, they may compromise the
integrity of the vesicle membrane [78]. Second, there is a lack of stan­
dardized production and purification methods. Current separation
techniques struggle to balance recovery rate and specificity, and no
unified protocol exists for scaling up production from laboratory to
clinical-scale manufacturing [88]. A more fundamental issue lies in
potential in vivo stability and safety issues. Natural EVs have a short
half-life and are easily cleared by the immune system, the endogenous
bioactive molecules they carry may pose unknown risks of immunoge­
nicity and off-target effects [89]. These bottlenecks collectively hinder
the clinical translation of the EVs-siRNA delivery system.

MOFs possess considerable potential for efficient drug delivery by
virtue of their self-assembly characteristics, favorable biocompatibility,
and tunable porous structures [100]. Utilizing the EPR effect, MOFs
preferentially accumulate at tumor sites. Their robust structural stability
protects payloads, such as siRNA, from degradation during delivery,
particularly when targeted toward the specific TME [101]. Moreover,
the intrinsic porous structure of MOFs facilitates the design of multi­
functional nanoplatforms capable of co-delivering multiple therapeutic
agents within a single carrier, thereby achieving synergistic antitumormetastasis effects with reduced adverse outcomes [102]. Lv et al.
[103] engineered a Cis@MOF-siVEGF nanocarrier targeting VEGF and
modulating MUC4 and ACE2 expression. The nanocarrier is constructed
based on ZIF-8, which self-assembles with THPP to create the MOF
framework, and is then loaded with cisplatin (DDP) and siVEGF.
Benefiting from the EPR effect, the MOF facilitates accumulation at
tumor sites. The cisplatin loading efficiency reaches 43.41% ± 11.12%,
effectively protecting and delivering siVEGF and THPP. In both cellular
and animal models, Cis@MOF-siVEGF achieved potent VEGF silencing,
significantly inhibits tumor angiogenesis, and, in conjunction with ACE2
upregulation, MUC4 downregulation, and chemotherapy, suppressed
tumor growth and metastasis.
Overall, metal nanoparticles serve as effective siRNA delivery vehi­
cles due to their high specific surface area, which enables efficient drug
loading. Additionally, their surfaces can be easily functionalized to
confer active targeting or imaging capabilities [104]. Their size is
adjustable, and ultra-small particles can be excreted from the body via
renal clearance, reducing the risk of long-term accumulation [105].
These advantages make them an ideal siRNA delivery platform that
combines delivery efficiency, multifunctionality, and safety. However,
their clinical translation faces challenges in multiple areas, including
safety, preparation processes, and regulatory issues. The long-term
accumulation of non-degradable metals (such as gold, silver, and cop­
per) in the body may lead to oxidative stress, membrane disruption, and
mitochondrial dysfunction at high doses or after prolonged exposure
[106]. Furthermore, the formation of protein coronas and immunoge­
nicity increase the unpredictability of their in vivo behavior. Therefore,
most metal nanoparticle platforms remain in the preclinical stage, with
only a few gold nanoparticle formulations having advanced to clinical
trials [107,108].

4.1.4. Metal nanoparticles
Metal nanoparticles, derived from metal-containing compounds,
constitute a class of nanomaterials that incorporate metal ions and are
often conjugated with various inorganic or organic molecules [90].
Compared with other nanoparticle systems, metal nanoparticles possess
unique physicochemical properties, including magnetic, optical, ther­
mal, catalytic, and electrical characteristics [91]. In addition, metal
nanoparticles can prevent burst drug release and avoid off-target effects,
thereby improving pharmacokinetic profiles and enabling flexible con­
trol of drug release [92]. Leveraging these properties, metal nano­
particles have been widely applied in the treatment of primary tumors
and tumor metastasis [93]. A broad range of metal nanoparticles has
been developed, such as gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), iron oxide and
metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), have become important nanoplat­
forms for the passive targeted delivery of siRNA owing to their
controllable size, stable structure, and diverse functionalities [94].
AuNPs have attracted significant attention as metal nanocarriers
owing to their favorable physicochemical properties. As reported by He
et al. [95], the authors constructed a delivery system in which thiolmodified TUBB2B siRNA was directly assembled onto the surface of
AuNPs and passivated with PEG-SH. Due to their nanoscale size, the
siRNA-AuNPs conjugate passively accumulates in tumor tissues via the
EPR effect. They can also effectively cross the blood-brain barrier,
leading to significant silencing of TUBB2B expression. Following intra­
venous administration in vivo, the siRNA-AuNPs preferentially localize
within primary tumors and brain metastases, markedly inhibiting tumor
growth and metastatic colonization in the brain.
Nevertheless, the risks of long-term accumulation of nanocarriers
based on Au in the body cannot be overlooked [96,97]. In contrast,
superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) can be degraded
and cleared through iron metabolism pathways, resulting in a low risk of
long-term accumulation [98]. SPIONs exhibit superparamagnetic
properties within specific size ranges and have been utilized as siRNA
delivery vehicles. For example, Hu et al. [99] developed PEN-coated
SPIONs (CNP@siSnail2) targeting Snail2. Upon intravenous injection,
CNP@siSnail2 passively extravasates and accumulates within tumor
tissues through the EPR effect, facilitating efficient delivery of siSnail2
to tumor cells. Both in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrate that
CNP@siSnail2 significantly downregulates Snail2 expression, thereby
inhibiting EMT as well as tumor cell migration and invasion.

4.2. Active targeting
Nanoparticle delivery systems that rely on the EPR effect exhibit
limited targeting specificity. Given the wide variation in pore sizes
within the tumor stroma and the difficulty in predicting its permeability,
passive targeting alone is often insufficient to achieve efficient and
precise delivery of anticancer drugs [109]. Furthermore, the presence of
complex physiological barriers, along with the potential toxicity arising
from the inability to discriminate between healthy and tumor tissues
further impede the effectiveness of tumor therapies [110]. To address
these challenges, researchers have developed active targeting strategies
to enhance drug accumulation at tumor sites, and minimize off-target
side effects. By modifying affinity ligands on nanocarriers, active tar­
geting not only leverages the EPR effect to localize nanoparticles within
the tumor region but also enables the recognition of overexpressed re­
ceptors on cellular or vascular surfaces through specific ligands to
enhance tissue retention and promote cellular uptake [111]. To better
illustrate these design strategies, the key strategies of active targeting
and stimuli-responsive delivery are summarized in Fig. 3.

Although proteins are highly biodegradable, inexpensive, structur­

porting various molecules [119], protein-based nanoparticles used for
nucleic acid delivery face inherent limitations. Among published
albumin-based nanocarriers, more than half use bovine serum albumin
(BSA) as a building block. However, in clinical applications, BSA can
trigger a variety of immune reactions, from mild allergies to lifethreatening anaphylactic shock. Furthermore, BSA poses a small but
unacceptable risk of prion transmission, leading regulatory agencies to
restrict its use in injectable formulations. Even with human-derived
proteins, repeated dosing may lead to the development of anti-carrier
antibodies [120]. Another major obstacle in the development of
protein-based nanocarriers arises from the intrinsic instability and
delicate nature of protein conformations. Proteins rely on weak non­
covalent interactions to maintain their tertiary structure, such as
hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and salt bridges [121].

Environmental stressors encountered during production and adminis­
tration, including fluctuations in temperature, pH, ionic strength, and
the presence of organic solvents, can lead to protein denaturation and
aggregation [122]. Common nanoparticle preparation steps, such as
desolvation, emulsification, self-assembly, and cross-linking, are
particularly susceptible to these instabilities, which adversely affect
both particle uniformity and functional efficacy [123]. Consequently,
these challenges present significant obstacles to developing reproduc­
ible formulations with potential for clinical translation.
4.2.2. Antibody
Antibody-targeted nanoparticles are regarded as one of the most
promising approaches for active targeted drug delivery because of their
high specificity and affinity for tumor-specific antigens, as along with
their well-established development platforms [124].
Numerous studies have confirmed that the epidermal growth factor
receptor (EGFR) is overexpressed in various solid tumors [125]. Owing
to its overexpression in these malignancies and the extensive clinical
research, EGFR has become a prominent target for the design of
antibody-targeted nanoparticles. Based on this characteristic, thera­
peutic strategies employing EGFR antibody-modified nanoplatforms for
tumor-targeted delivery have demonstrated significant efficacy in mul­
tiple studies. Jiang et al. [126] constructed an engineered exosome
functionalized with an EGFR single-chain antibody (scFv) for targeted
therapy of lung cancer brain metastases. By fusing scFv with the exo­
some membrane protein Lamp2b, active targeted recognition of EGFRpositive lung cancer cells is achieved. The engineered exosome is effi­
ciently loaded with siLPCAT1 via electroporation, achieving an encap­
sulation efficiency of approximately 85%. It effectively silences LPCAT1

Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) is overexpressed on the surface
of various tumor cells and serves as a key target for immunotherapy
[127]. Based on this, integrating anti-PD-L1 antibodies onto the surface
of exosomes not only endows the nanocarriers with active targeting

tumor cells, enabling active targeting, and silencing metastasispromoting pathways via STAT3 siRNA. In combination with pacli­

high stability, strong tissue penetration, and low immunogenicity,
antibody-modified nanoparticles have emerged as a highly promising
new generation of targeting ligands in tumor-targeted delivery systems
[129]. However, non-specific uptake, loss of activity resulting from nonspecific conjugation, and uncertainties regarding conjugation efficiency
collectively constitute main barriers to clinical translation [130]. A
second major obstacle to the clinical translation of antibody-modified
nanoparticles is the significant manufacturing challenges they present.
Unlike conventional drugs, the production of these antibody-modified
nanoparticles demands mastery of two interconnected engineering
challenges: first, the reproducible fabrication of nanoparticle cores with
well-defined and consistent physicochemical attributes, including size,
morphology, and surface charge. Second, the precise, oriented conju­
gation of antibodies that preserves their integrity and functionality
[131]. Current synthetic methods often fail to address these complex­
ities, resulting in heterogeneous batches with high polydispersity. All of
these factors severely hinder the process of translating such nanoplat­
forms from laboratory research to clinical application.

M2 type TAMs exert immunosuppressive and metastasis promoting
effects in various cancers and are key targets for reshaping the tumor
immune microenvironment. Given the high expression of receptors such
as CD206 on M2 type TAMs, the M2pep peptide can be incorporated
onto the surface of LNPs to enhance targeting specificity [142]. For
instance, Huang et al. [143] designed DSPE-PEG-M2pep-modified lipo­

type tumor associated macrophages via the surface bound M2pep pep­

reduce lung bioluminescence signals, and H&E staining reveals mark­
edly more necrosis regions in the M2pep-modified group, indicating
significant inhibition of metastasis.
Peptide modification can endow nanoparticles with multi­
functionality. By actively targeting tumors, penetrating deeply into tis­
sues, releasing drugs in response to the microenvironment, and
activating antitumor immunity, these nanoparticles significantly
enhance therapeutic efficacy and reduce toxic side effects, making them
powerful tools for precision therapy of tumors and tumor metastases
[144]. However, the in vivo application of peptide-based nanocarriers is
substantially constrained by inherent stability issues. Peptides are
intrinsically labile and highly susceptible to enzymatic degradation and
rapid systemic clearance, both of which can significantly compromise
their therapeutic efficacy. A range of stabilization approaches, such as
chemical modification, physical encapsulation, and the adoption of
peptidomimetic scaffolds, have been explored to overcome this limita­
tion. Nevertheless, these interventions may come at a cost, potentially
altering bioactivity or increasing formulation complexity [145]. Spe­
cifically, any structural modification to the peptide can affect its targetbinding affinity, solution-phase stability, and overall pharmacokinetic
behavior. Although the prospects for related research are promising,
numerous challenges must still be overcome before these technologies
can be brought to the clinical stage, including insufficient receptor
density, the need for systematic optimization of peptide density, species
differences, and validation of clinical translation [146].

4.2.3. Peptides
Peptides can mediate transmembrane transport, achieve targeted
delivery, and perform various fundamental cellular functions [132].
Therefore, they are considered ideal candidate molecules for targeted
therapy. Tumor-targeting peptides typically consist of fewer than 50
amino acids and can be either naturally occurring or synthetically pro­
duced [133].
The integrin αvβ3 is significantly overexpressed in malignant tumor
cells and tumor-related vasculature, with its expression level is posi­
tively correlated with disease progression, making it an ideal target for
active targeting [134]. Peptides containing the arginine-glycine-aspartic
acid (RGD) motif are a typical tumor-targeting peptide, which target
integrin αvβ3 and widely used targeting functional moieties in non-viral
delivery systems [135]. Extensive efforts have been devoted to designing
nano-delivery systems functionalized with RGD peptide, enabling pre­
cise targeting of tumor cells and integrin receptors that are highly
expressed in the vasculature [136]. Alzamely et al. [137] covalently
attached the cRGDfK peptide to the surface of chitosan-lactate (CL)
using the SPDP crosslinker. After mixing CD73 siRNA and ZEB-1 siRNA
with tripolyphosphate (TPP), the mixture is added to the CL solution to
self-assemble into nanoparticles, forming RGD cyclic peptide-modified
CL nanoparticles (RGD-CL NPs). ZEB-1 regulates EMT and the expres­
sion of E-cadherin, knockdown of ZEB-1 can reverse EMT and inhibit
tumor metastasis [138]. CD73 promotes tumor cell migration, invasion,
and distant metastasis through both enzymatic and non-enzymatic
mechanisms, forming a positive feedback loop with EMT [139]. The
RGD-CL NPs specifically bind to αvβ3 integrins, which are overexpressed
on the surface of tumor cells and tumor vascular endothelial cells, via
RGD peptides. This interaction significantly enhances cellular uptake
efficiency from 20% to 62%, thereby achieving active targeted delivery.

4.2.4. Aptamers
Aptamers are short, single-stranded DNA or RNA oligonucleotides

them powerful tools for targeted therapeutic delivery [147]. Due to their
characters of small size, low immunogenicity, and ease of chemical
modification, aptamers offer advantages in safety, toxicity profile,
specificity, biodistribution, and tissue accumulation [148].

The advantage of small molecule ligand modification strategies lies
in enhancing targeting specificity and therapeutic efficacy. FA modifi­
cation can significantly increase the area under the curve (AUC), halflife, and tumor accumulation of drugs, while reducing distribution to
off-target organs such as the heart and kidneys, thereby lowering sys­
temic toxicity [159]. PBA modified nanoparticles offer advantages such
as low immunogenicity, simple synthesis, and ATP responsiveness, and
in vitro and in vivo experiments have confirmed their excellent
biocompatibility [160,161]. However, the small molecule ligand
modification strategy is not without its limitations. FA receptors (FR) are
also expressed at low levels on normal cells, which may lead to off-target
effects [162]. More seriously, once FA-modified nanoparticles enter the
bloodstream, they readily bind to endogenous IgM antibodies, which not
only block their binding to tumor FR but also accelerate clearance by the
liver and spleen and enhance immunogenicity [163]. This phenomenon

variability [164]. Furthermore, the safety of FA has also been called into
question. In animal studies, FA has been shown to induce abnormal
mammary gland hyperplasia [165]. PBA modification strategies also
face unique limitations. The binding of PBA to SA is pH-dependent. For
PBA to bind to SA on the surface of tumor cells, it must first form an
active tetrahedral borate anion. The pKa value of standard PBA is
approximately 8.8, which is much higher than physiological pH, this
means that in the bloodstream, most PBA exists in a planar, hydrophobic
form that cannot effectively bind to SA [166]. Design parameters that
urgently need to be optimized through precision molecular engineering
are systematically addressing these issues.

4.3. Stimuli-responsive delivery
Stimuli-responsive nanocarriers represent promising nanoplatforms
for drug delivery, capable of precisely responding to both endogenous
and exogenous triggers to facilitate targeted nucleic acid delivery [187].
Endogenous stimuli, including acidic pH, ROS, glutathione (GSH) and
enzymes, can be harnessed to construct tumor-specific gene release
systems. These carriers remain stable under physiological conditions yet
undergo defined structural or physicochemical changes when exposed to
specific stimuli, thereby enabling intelligent regulation of in vivo
behavior and enhancing siRNA delivery efficiency [188]. Notably,
endogenous stimulus-responsive systems, which exploit the intrinsic
pathological features of the TME as triggering signals, are particularly
advantageous for siRNA drug delivery and release [189]. They obviate
the need for external apparatus, rendering them more feasible for clin­
ical translation.
4.3.1. pH-responsive nanocarriers
Aberrant pH values are a hallmark of tumor tissues and closely
correlate with disease progression [190]. Normal physiological pH
ranges from approximately 7.0–7.4. In contrast, due to the Warburg
effect, tumor tissues rely primarily on glycolysis even under aerobic
conditions, produced substantial lactic acid and led to acidification of
the TME [191]. Notably, a decreasing pH gradient exists along the
endocytic pathway, from early endosomes to late endosomes and lyso­
somes, with pH values ranging from 4.5 to 6.5 [187]. These pH varia­
tions at both the tissue and cellular levels offer critical advantages for
designing pH-responsive nanocarriers.
pH-responsive nanocarriers can specifically improve payloads
release within tumor tissues and reduce systemic side effects [192].
These carriers remain structurally stable at physiological pH, and
effectively protect siRNA from nuclease degradation. Upon entry into
the acidic TME or endosome, they undergo structural or physicochem­
ical changes that promote cellular uptake and endosomal escape [193].
It is important to facilitate siRNA escape from endosomes, and pHresponsive nanoparticles markedly enhance the cytoplasmic delivery
efficiency and gene silencing efficacy of siRNA.
Recent research has begun to extend the use of such nanoparticles to
the field of tumor metastasis. For example, Yang et al. [194] constructed
a pH-responsive nanoparticle carrier targeting lncBCMA. Eukaryotic
translation elongation factor 1A1 (eEF1A1) is a key GTP binding protein
that is highly expressed across diverse cancer types [195]. LncBCMA
directly binds to eEF1A1 and antagonizes its ubiquitination, thereby
stabilizing eEF1A1 protein expression. This mechanism enhances breast
cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, ultimately promoting
tumor metastasis. Employing a nanoprecipitation method, the re­
searchers dissolve Meo-PEG-b-PDPA in dimethylformamide (DMF) and
encapsulate the siRNA/G0-C14 complex via electrostatic interactions.
This nanocarrier remains stable at physiological pH 7.4. After entering
the endosome, it escapes via the proton sponge effect and releases siRNA
upon degradation. At a dose of 30 nM, it downregulates lncBCMA
expression in TNBC cells by approximately 80%. Furthermore, tumor
volume reduction exceeds 83% in the orthotopic tumor model and
surpasses 90% in the patient derived xenograft (PDX) model, ultimately
suppressing TNBC tumor growth and lung metastasis with high speci­
ficity and no significant effects on healthy tissues.
In addition, Sun et al. [196] designed a biomimetic nanoplatform
(BSA-OPC/siMDR1@CCM) based on BSA and encapsulated within a

4.3.3. GSH-responsive nanocarriers
GSH is a tripeptide containing a thiol group and a γ-amide bond
(γ-glutamyl-cysteinyl-glycine) [209]. As the most abundant non-protein
thiol in mammalian cells, GSH plays a critical role in maintaining
membrane integrity, redox balance, thiol enzyme stability, and cellular
detoxification [210]. The concentration of GSH in the ECM or blood is
approximately 2–20 μM. In contrast, due to rapid proliferation, tumor
cells exhibit a markedly elevated intracellular GSH concentration of
2–10 mM. This is approximately 100 to 1000-fold higher than extra­
cellular levels and 4 to 10-fold higher than that of healthy tissues [200].
Notably, exploiting this substantial intracellular versus extracellular
GSH concentration gradient, GSH responsive nanocarriers can release
siRNA drugs specifically within tumor cells, thereby enhancing thera­
peutic effect and reducing toxicity of healthy tissue [211]. A represen­
tative study demonstrated [212] the clinical challenges of EGFRtargeted drug resistance and lymph node metastasis in oral squamous
cell carcinoma (OSCC). The investigators developed a GSH-responsive
hybrid nanocarrier composed of a mPEG-SS-PLGA polymer and G0C14 cationic lipids. Through self-assembly, the hybrid polymers
encapsulate siLCN2, achieving an exceeding 80% encapsulation effi­
ciency. Notably, under the high intracellular GSH concentrations, the
disulfide bonds undergo reductive cleavage, leading to the rapid release
of siLCN2 and subsequent efficient silencing of LCN2 expression. The
characteristic results in a reduction of protein expression by over 60% in
vitro and more than 50% suppression in vivo, disrupting the binding of
LCN2 to EGFR and EGFR recycling, thereby inhibiting activation of the
EGFR-MEK-ERK signaling pathway. Ultimately, this redox responsive
strategy significantly suppresses tumor growth and metastasis in both
OSCC tongue orthotopic xenograft models and EGFR positive PDX
models. Moreover, the nanocarrier prolongs the in vivo circulation time
of siRNA and reduces off target toxicity, thereby providing a novel
strategy for the treatment of drug resistant and metastasis of OSCC. In
addition, the PFCPD nanoplatform developed by Huang et al. [213] is a
GSH-responsive siGPX4 delivery system. In a bilateral 4T1 tumor model,
PFCPD not only significantly inhibits primary tumor growth but, more
importantly, synergistically induces potent ICD through ferroptosis
combined with PDT. This process activates dendritic cell maturation,
promotes CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and reshapes the pro-inflammatory
cytokine microenvironment by increasing IFN-γ and TNF-α levels,
while decreasing TGF-β, thereby effectively suppressing tumor
metastasis.

5. Efficient combination of siRNA therapy with current
therapeutic modalities
In the context of tumor heterogeneity, monotherapy often fails to
achieve the desired clinical outcomes. To address this problem, combi­
nation regimens based on nanotechnology have emerged as a pivotal
strategy. As illustrated in Fig. 4 and detailed in Table 3, the co-delivery
of siRNA alongside established therapeutic modalities via nanotech­

5.1. siRNA with chemotherapy
Chemotherapy remains a first-line treatment for various cancers. It
induces DNA damage and activates complex signaling networks, thereby
leading to cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [224]. However, tumor
metastasis is usually associated with mutations in several core proteins,
reflecting pronounced heterogeneity and complexity of advanced cancer
[225]. Consequently, monotherapy using a single chemotherapeutic
agent often results in suboptimal therapeutic efficacy, severe adverse
effect, and acquired recurrence and metastasis. In contrast, combining
chemotherapy with siRNA therapy can achieve multi-target synergistic
effects, wherein siRNA can specifically silence metastasis-related genes
to overcome tumor heterogeneity and complexity and inhibit tumor
growth and metastasis [226].
Active Targeting: Researchers developed [227] a trastuzumabconjugated mesoporous silica-based nanocarrier that facilitated codelivery of docetaxel and HER2 siRNA. This nanocarrier is engineered
with trastuzumab for active targeting, thereby demonstrating superior
efficacy and favorable safety profiles in both orthotopic breast cancer
and brain metastasis models. For the more challenging pulmonary
metastatic melanoma, Gu et al. [228] developed an exosome-liposome
hybrid nanoplatform, designated TP-siRC@tHyNPs, which comprised
exosomes functionalized with DR5-scFv and liposomes loaded with
triptolide and CYP3A4-siRNA. DR5-scFv is decorated to specifically
recognize the overexpressed DR5 receptor on tumor cells, thereby
enabling active targeted siRNA delivery and enhancing drug accumu­
lation at metastatic lesions. In vitro, TP-siRC@tHyNPs significantly
inhibit the invasion and migration of A375M cells, achieving inhibition
rates of 21.78 ± 3.16% for invasion and 13.83 ± 5.41% for migration. In
both patient-derived melanoma (PMM) and PDX models, TPsiRC@tHyNPs achieve tumor inhibition rates exceeding 90%.
In the treatment of pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors, Wang et al.
[229] used LNPs surface-modified with octreotide to co-deliver temo­
zolomide (TMZ) and siRNA targeting the DNA repair enzyme MGMT
(LOTR). In a liver metastasis model, LOTR significantly suppresses the
growth of metastatic lesions while markedly alleviating bone marrow
suppression and organ toxicity associated with high-dose TMZ. This
combination strategy offers a novel therapeutic approach for pancreatic
neuroendocrine tumors, particularly in patients with metastatic disease.
Stimuli-Responsive delivery: Zhang et al. [230] achieved the codelivery of Xkr8 siRNA and annexin A5 (ANX5) mRNA by converting
cisplatin into a lipid prodrug and incorporating it into lipid nano­
particles (PtLNPs). In a lung metastasis model, PtLNPs effectively sup­
press lung metastatic nodules. PtLNPs offer a new strategy for
immunosenitization and metastasis suppression in platinum-based
chemotherapy.
Huang et al. [231] developed a pH-responsive nanoparticle
composed of PEG-Dlinkm-PLGA and G0-C14 for the systemic delivery of

4.3.4. Enzyme-responsive nanocarriers
Enzymes are indispensable biological catalysts that participate in
nearly all life processes and are essential for maintaining normal phys­
iological functions. In tumor tissues, various enzymes, including MMPs,
cathepsin B (CTSB), prostate-specific antigen (PSA), HAase, and ester­
ases, are aberrantly overexpressed. The enzymatic profile evolves
dynamically during tumor progress [217]. Leveraging the differences in
enzyme expression between healthy and malignant tissues, researchers
have designed enzyme-responsive nanocarriers. These carriers exploit
the substrate specific cleavage activity of enzymes to enable controlled
dissociation, trigger drugs release, and enhance cellular uptake, thereby
improving siRNA delivery efficiency.
MMPs are aberrantly overexpressed across a broad spectrum of
malignancies and have been implicated in tumor progression, local in­
vasion, and distant metastasis [218]. Leveraging this hallmark, Yi et al.
[219] engineered an MMP-2-responsive micelle complex (PA7R@siPDL1) designed to convert “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors by inte­
grating vascular normalization with PD-L1 gene silencing. The complex
incorporates an MMP-2-cleavable GPLGVRG linker, which undergoes
site-specific dissociation within the TME, thereby exposing R9 cellpenetrating peptides to augment cellular uptake. Concomitantly, the
anti-angiogenic peptide A7R is released to promote tumor vascular
normalization, alleviate hypoxia, and facilitate immune cell infiltration.
Upon near-infrared laser irradiation, ROS is generated by the photo­
sensitizer Ce6, triggering ICD and disrupting lysosomal membranes,
further promoting endosomal escape and cytosolic release of siPD-L1.
This multimodal immune reprogramming strategy not only potentiates
antitumor efficacy in primary tumors, but also effectively suppresses
metastatic dissemination.
Although enzyme-responsive nanocarriers show potential for various
applications, they face several challenges and limitations, which prevent
their widespread clinical translation. Commonly used proteases, such as
MMPs, are not specific to cancer cells, they are also expressed during
normal physiological processes [220]. This lack of absolute specificity
can lead to off-target effects [221]. Therefore, striking a balance be­
tween the sensitivity and specificity of enzyme-based assays remains a
key challenge in translating research into clinical practice. Additionally,
enzyme regulatory abnormalities vary significantly across different
types of cancer, various stages of the same cancer, and different patient
populations [222]. Thus, it is particularly important to conduct an indepth analysis of the spatiotemporal distribution of enzymes to guide
the development of more precise and effective nanomedicines.
In conclusion, endogenous stimulus-responsive delivery systems
exploit physiological discrepancies between the TME and healthy tis­
sues, thereby providing an efficient and safe technological nanoplatform
for the precise delivery of siRNA. These systems enhance tumor specific
targeting, minimize nonspecific release and off-target effects of siRNA in
healthy tissues, and facilitate endosomal escape [223]. Collectively, the
targeting and responsive nanoplatform hold considerable promise for
siRNA-based therapy against tumor metastasis.

associated with aberrant expression of multiple pivotal genes, such as
HIF-1α, and Trop2 [247,248]. The overexpression of these genes
together constitutes the molecular mechanisms driving tumor resistance
to radiation, ultimately promoting tumor cell migration and distant
metastasis. Consequently, combination of RT and siRNA therapy,
downregulating the expression of these key genes via gene silencing, has
been proved to enhance the therapeutic effect of RT and suppresses

mediate light-triggered generation of ROS, which subsequently induces
tumor cell apoptosis or necrosis [238]. Nevertheless, PDT has substan­
tial limitations against tumor metastasis. Although it can effectively
ablate primary tumors, PDT exhibits limited efficacy against dissemi­

6. Clinical translation and future perspectives

5.5. siRNA with immunotherapy

Tumor heterogeneity and the complex TME present significant ob­
stacles in the design and development of nanomedicines. The TME not
only contributes to tumor metastasis and drug resistance but also serves
as a major barrier to the targeted delivery of nanocarriers and the
effective release of siRNA therapeutics. Consequently, it is essential for
researchers to develop a comprehensive understanding of tumor initia­
tion and metastatic progression, as well as an in-depth analysis of the
interactions between the TME and nanomedicines. Currently, emerging
analytical techniques such as single-cell sequencing, spatial tran­
scriptomics, and proteomics, along with artificial intelligence-driven
data analysis and predictive models, as well as chip-based organ
models, are employed to analyze, predict, and assess the complex in­
teractions between the TME and siRNA nanomedicines [261–263].
These advanced approaches aim to facilitate the design and develop­
ment of nanocarriers, that enable active targeting and stimuliresponsive release of siRNA drugs, thereby optimizing and refining the
design principles and parameters of nanomedicines to achieve precise
tumor therapy.
To address the delivery challenges posed by tumor heterogeneity and
the complex TME, the nanocarrier is specifically designed with three key
considerations: material safety, precise targeting, and efficient delivery.
In terms of material selection, a “safe-by-design” approach is adopted.
Biodegradable materials, such as PLGA, lipids, or peptides, are priori­
tized over inorganic nanocarriers to prevent ion leakage and reduce risks
associated with chronic accumulation. For precise targeting, a dual
strategy combining passive and active targeting is employed. This
approach is further enhanced by PEGylation and cell membrane cam­
ouflage to reduce clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system
(MPS) and to prevent ligand masking by protein coronas [264]. By
modulating particle size, shape, and surface charge, the ability of
nanocarriers to traverse tumor vascular barriers and overcome inter­
stitial hypertension is enhanced, thereby addressing delivery barriers
imposed by tumor heterogeneity [109]. Multi-stage responsive release
systems, including pH/enzyme dual-responsive platforms, are engi­
neered to achieve temporally and spatially controlled drug release at the
target site while preventing premature leakage [188].

Tumor metastasis contributes to the leading cause of treatment
failure in cancer patients. However, siRNA-based therapies remain
confronted with multiple obstacles in the treatment of tumor metastasis,
including degradation by RNases, clearance via the reticuloendothelial
system (RES) in the liver, renal excretion, poor tissue permeability, and
inefficient endosomal escape [259]. Nanotechnology has improved the
inherent pharmacokinetic and biodistribution limitations that have
hindered siRNA therapeutic efficacy, enabling targeted delivery and
controlled release of siRNA agents to both primary tumors and meta­
static lesions [260]. However, the clinical translation of siRNA therapy
for tumor metastasis continues to face significant challenges.
6.1. Design of nanocarriers for siRNA

Tumor metastasis is a multi-stage process involving numerous im­
mune cells that exert either pro-metastatic or anti-metastatic functions
[252]. Immunotherapy can eliminate tumor cells and inhibit metastasis
by activating immune cells and modulating the TME [253]. Currently,
immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy inhibits immune check­
points such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4, thereby enhancing anti-tumor
immunotherapy and suppressing metastatic dissemination [254].
However, metastatic lesions often establish a more robust immunosup­
pressive microenvironment, which restricts the activation of systemic
anti-tumor immune responses, making it difficult to effectively control
disseminated tumors [255].
Passive Targeting: Chen et al. [256] developed vesicle-like nano­
particles (VNPs) composed of lipids and polymers for the co-delivery of
siPD-L1 and siCD47, thereby effectively silencing both PD-L1 and CD47.
In B16F10 melanoma cells, the CD47 silencing efficiency reached
82.4%. In a murine melanoma model, this strategy achieves an 81.4%
tumor growth inhibition by activating antitumor immune responses
involving both macrophages and CD8+ T cells.
Active Targeting: Liang et al. [257] developed mannose-modified
SORT lipid nanoparticles (siSPP1-mIFNγ@mLNPs) with 40% DOTAP
to enable lung-selective accumulation and co-delivery of SPP1-siRNA
and IFN-γ mRNA. siSPP1-mIFNγ@mLNPs can precisely target M2 mac­
rophages through mannose-CD206 conjugation. In models of lung me­
tastases, combining siRNA with anti-PD-1 immune checkpoint inhibitors
reduces lung metastases, increases OVA-specific CD8+ T cells (9-fold),
effector memory CD8+ T cells (3.24-fold), and M1 macrophages (3-fold).
This effectively converts “cold” to “hot” tumors.
Active Targeting and Stimuli-Responsive delivery: Active targeting
combined with stimuli-responsive release represents a promising strat­
egy for precision siRNA delivery. Researchers engineered a HA deco­
rated core-shell nanocarrier (HA-LSL/siTGF-β) exhibiting dual
responsiveness to HAase and GSH as well as dual CD44-targeting
capability [258]. This nanocarrier achieves precise cellular delivery
through CD44-mediated active targeting. HAase-mediated shell degra­
dation and the LPEI proton sponge effect enable efficient endosomal
escape, while GSH-triggered cleavage of disulfide bonds allows rapid
intracellular release of siTGF-β. The silencing efficiency of TGF-β mRNA
reaches 53.0% in CAFs and 43.5% in 4T1 cells. In vivo, TGF-β protein
levels in tumor tissue are downregulated by over 85%. By silencing TGFβ, this nanocarrier induces tumor vascular normalization, matrix
degradation, and hypoxia alleviation, thereby remodeling the TME.
When combined with anti-PD-L1 therapy, this system synergistically
and effectively inhibits lung metastasis of TNBC, increases primary
tumor growth inhibition to 79.1%, suppresses distant tumor volume by
86.7%, and significantly prolongs the survival in tumor-bearing mice.

efficacy during preclinical studies. To address these limitations, several
innovative technologies have recently emerged. One notable example is

6.3. Safety issues

6.5. Scale-up production

The safety profile is one of the primary challenges in the clinical
translation of nanomedicines. There are several concerns for develop­
ment of nanocarriers, including chronic toxicity risks due to prolonged
retention in the body; accelerated clearance triggered by immune sys­
tem recognition; non-specific distribution caused by insufficient tar­
geting; and issues of low delivery efficiency [268,269]. These problems
do not occur in isolation and contribute to toxicity of siRNA nano­
medicines. For instance, inadequate targeting often exacerbates nonspecific uptake, which in turn provokes immunotoxicity [270], while
low delivery efficiency necessitates higher doses, resulting in long-term
nanocarrier accumulation [271]. Therefore, addressing the in vivo
safety of nanocarriers has become an urgent priority for clinical trans­
lation of siRNA therapy.
And another safety issue is the off-target effects and immunogenicity
of siRNA molecules. These non-targeting effects may cause adverse ef­
fects and affect the efficacy of siRNA therapies. siRNA can bind to the 3’
UTR of non-target mRNAs, similar to endogenous miRNAs, and resulting
in the suppression of non-target mRNA [272]. Moreover, as exogenous
molecules, siRNA drugs can activate the innate immune response, pro­
mote the production of type I interferons and pro-inflammatory cyto­
kines, which potentially cause the inflammation toxicity [273].
Addressing these challenges requires a dual strategy involving chemical
modifications and carrier design. Chemical modifications can improve
therapeutic efficiency, while reducing the inherent off-target risks and
immunogenicity of siRNA in vivo. Additionally, actively targeted and
stimulus-responsive carriers minimize accumulation in normal tissues
by enhancing tumor-specific delivery and controlled release, ultimately
achieving the dual goals of precise treatment with low-toxicity safety.

Emerging complex nanomedicines still remains lack of extensive
experience in large-scale production [279]. Consequently, clinical
translation must overcome the challenges associated with scaling up
from laboratory preparation to industrial manufacturing. Reliable
formulation design and preparation methods are essential to ensure
product uniformity during large-scale production, thereby guaranteeing
the safety and efficacy of nanomedicines [280]. Therefore, the devel­
opment of novel siRNA nanomedicines requires controllable and
reproducible preparation processes. Notably, siRNA LNPs and mRNA
LNPs produced via microfluidic technology have already demonstrated
the reliability of nucleic acid drug chemistry, manufacturing, and con­
trol processes, thereby providing an important reference for the largescale production of multicomponent nucleic acid nanomedicines
[281]. Moreover, standardized quality control criteria should be estab­
lished, and detection methods for key parameters, such as particle size
distribution and surface modification, must be clearly defined [282].
6.6. Reproducibility in product quality control
Quality control for nanomedicines faces significant challenges, and
strict process control and analytical methods are urgently needed to
ensure product quality. Pacria's decision to discontinue its DepoCyt
(liposomal cytarabine) business due to persistent issues with product
quality, sterility, and reproducibility highlights the serious conse­
quences of inadequate quality control [280]. Therefore, comprehensive
inter-batch characterization of Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs) is
necessary to understand product variability and heterogeneity and
ensure process robustness. CQAs generally encompass properties that
can predict therapeutic outcome, covering physicochemical attributes
(like size, surface charge, shape, and morphology), drug-related char­
acteristics (such as loading capacity and release kinetics), and formu­
lation aspcets (e.g., sterility and biocompatibility) [283]. These
characteristics are critical to ensuring the quality of pharmaceuticals,
biotechnology products, or other regulated products. The identification
of these attributes requires thorough scrutiny of both the product and its
production workflow, generally undertaken during development and
validation stages. These attributes underpin the quality standards
required to guarantee batch-to-batch consistency and compliance with
regulatory expectations.

is essential for maintaining consistent product quality and performance.

6.4. Limitations of targeting efficiency
Nucleic acid delivery mediated by nanocarriers faces significant in­
efficiency issues in tumor targeting. Both preclinical and clinical studies
have shown that, regardless of whether the targeting is passive or active,
the actual tumor accumulation is far lower than expected.
The efficacy of ligand-modified systems is highly dependent on
tumor-specific receptor expression. For instance, FA-conjugated miR34a is effective in FOLR1-high breast and ovarian cancers but ineffective
in FOLR1-low prostate cancers [274]. Moreover, ligand conjugation
may trigger immune clearance rather than enhance tumor selectivity.
This limitation is exemplified by RGD-modified AuNPs, which show
enhanced uptake in 2D culture but are rapidly cleared by the mono­
nuclear phagocyte system in vivo, reducing their performance by more
than tenfold [275]. Even when these biological barriers are overcome,

7. Conclusion
Tumor metastasis is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths.
Engineered siRNA nanoparticles can block metastasis-related signaling
pathways via specific gene silencing. Leveraging TME characteristics
and metastatic pathology, nanodelivery systems achieve selective
accumulation at metastatic sites through EPR effects and active target­
ing. In addition, multi-responsive release mechanisms enable
microenvironment-triggered siRNA delivery, enhancing gene silencing
and reducing off-target toxicity. However, single-target approaches
cannot reverse the complex metastatic cascade. Combining siRNA with
chemotherapy, PTT, PDT, RT, or immunotherapy can synergistically
disrupt the metastatic niche at genetic, cellular, and microenviron­
mental levels. Despite these promising prospects, clinical translation of
siRNA nanomedicines remains limited, with only a few products
approved to date.
In summary, the siRNA delivery nanotechnologies exhibit great
promise for the personalized therapies of cancer metastasis. Although
various siRNA nanomedicines have shown efficacy to treat tumor
metastasis in preclinical models, several challenges remain to be
addressed for clinical translation. Therefore, the rational design and
development of novel nanomaterials are critically important for clinical
translation of siRNA-based therapeutics in the treatment against tumor
metastasis.
