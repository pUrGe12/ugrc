In recent years, cobalt has become a critical constraint on the supply chain
of the Li-ion battery industry. With the ever-increasing projections for electric
vehicles, the dependency of current Li-ion batteries on the ever-fluctuating
cobalt prices poses serious environmental and sustainability issues. To
address these challenges, a new class of cobalt-free materials with general
formula of LiNixFeyAlzO2 (x + y + z = 1), termed as the lithium iron aluminum
nickelate (NFA) class of cathodes, is introduced. These cobalt-free materials
are synthesized using the sol–gel process to explore their compositional landscape by varying aluminum and iron. These NFA variants are characterized
using electron microscopy, neutron and X-ray diffraction, and Mössbauer and
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy to investigate their morphological, physical,
and crystal-structure properties. Operando experiments by X-ray diffraction,
Mössbauer spectroscopy, and galvanostatic intermittent titration have been
also used to study the crystallographic transitions, electrochemical activity,
and Li-ion diffusivity upon lithium removal and uptake in the NFA cathodes.
NFA compositions yield specific capacities of ≈200 mAh g−1, demonstrating
reasonable rate capability and cycling stability with ≈80% capacity retention
after 100 charge/discharge cycles. While this is an early stage of research, the
potential that these cathodes could have as viable candidates in next-generation cobalt-free lithium-ion batteries is highlighted here.

electrochemical stability advantages.[24–26] However, the fundamental challenge of inexpensive cobalt availability leads to the

batteries.[28] To develop new cobalt-free cathode materials that
are cost-effective, a key point of consideration is that these
materials should have similar or better electrochemical characteristics to that of widely used, mainstream NCA and NCM
materials, which have had decades of continuous research and
development aimed at fine-tuning their processing parameters.
This similarity should be considered because new cobalt-free
materials should seamlessly integrate into existing global battery manufacturing infrastructures without the need for a complete overhaul of the entire battery industry.
To simultaneously solve the battery industry’s cobalt problem
and address the challenges of seamless industrial integration,
this work focuses on eliminating cobalt in the battery cathodes
entirely. One cathode candidate of choice is the high-nickel-­
containing LiNiO2[23,29–31] material; however, this material
­suffers from structural and electrochemical performance instabilities. By replacing some of the nickel with cost-effective, sustainable, and environmentally friendly alternatives such as iron
and a­ luminum, significant improvements to structural stability,
cationic disorder, and further cost savings can be achieved.
­
Incorporating small amounts of trivalent aluminum and iron,
which have similar ionic radii to Ni3+ (0.54 Å for Al3+ and 0.55 Å
for Fe3+ compared to 0.56 Å for Ni3+), would enable cycling stability and improve power capabilities, respectively.[32,33]
Here, we introduce a novel class of nickel rich, cobalt-free Li-ion
battery cathode material with general formula LiNixFeyAlzO2
(x ≥ 0.8, x + y + z = 1), termed as the NFA class of cathodes,
as a sustainable alternative for the development of nextgeneration Li-ion batteries. These novel cobalt-free NFA
cathodes retain the structural stability and safety advantages
provided by iron and aluminum and the high capacity offered
by nickel, while lowering the cost and increasing the capacity
(≈200 mAh g−1). In doing so, the advantages of NCA and NMC
cathodes can be transferred to the NFA class without a recourse
to cobalt. The capacities and voltage window of NFA materials
are like NCM 811 and NCA, but the material cost would be significantly lower because of the substitution of expensive cobalt

with inexpensive iron and aluminum. Moreover, the similarities in material and electrochemical characteristics between
the cobalt-containing NCA, NCM, and the cobalt-free NFA
class would enable an unconstrained transition toward nextgeneration lithium-ion batteries that are completely cobalt-free.
A schematic representation of the NFA class of cathodes
with nickel, iron, and aluminum incorporated into the lattice
structure for use in a next-generation cobalt-free lithium-ion
battery is shown in Figure 1A. Similar to several conventional
cobalt-containing cathode materials such as the NCM and NCA
classes, the NFA class of cathodes can be seamlessly integrated
into existing global battery manufacturing infrastructures
owing to their similar characteristics. To further highlight the
material and cost advantages when moving to the cobalt-free
NFA class when compared to the cobalt containing NCM and

the amount of cobalt required for each of these cathode classes

As an example, the scenario of an electric vehicle with a range
of 260 miles (battery pack energy ≈76.47 kWh) using the NCM
(111, 532, 622, 811), NCA, and NFA classes of cathodes was considered. Scaling this up in accordance with the projected estimates of about 1 000 000 EVs on the road by 2030, the amount
of cobalt required for reaching this target number with each of
these cathode classes was determined. The calculations indicate
that in such a scenario if the current mainstream, NMC 111
(33.3% Co), was the cathode of choice, then the total amount of
cobalt required would be ≈2500 tons. Even the next-generation
cathodes, such as NMC 811 and NCA which are poised for a
broadband commercial deployment in the coming years, would
still require ≈700 tons of cobalt to realize this scenario. These
numbers though disturbing are still only a conservative estimate because global EV projections for the next half-century are
constantly increasing at a burgeoning rate. Our approach, on
the other hand, replaces the expensive cobalt with low cost iron
and aluminum thus providing a viable solution to this cobalt
conundrum. In this scenario, if the NFA cathodes are deployed
for the same number of EVs with the same range and pack energies, no cobalt would be required. These comparisons categorically substantiate the viability of cobalt-free cathode materials
like our NFA class for induction in next-generation batteries.
To demonstrate this potential, we synthesized NFA cathode
materials using the sol-gel process in three different nickel rich
compositions: LiNi0.8Fe0.1Al0.1O2 (NFA 811), LiNi0.8Fe0.15Al0.05O2
(NFA-F), and LiNi0.8Fe0.05Al0.15O2 (NFA-A). The sol–gel process was employed because of the intimate mixing of the constituent elements that can be achieved through this process,
thereby enabling good compositional control and purity.[35–39]

operando Mössbauer spectroscopy was performed on the NFA
811 cathode material during the charge/discharge process. A
custom-made pouch cell and holder were used with 68 mg cm−2
of NFA 811 as the cathode and Li metal as the anode (Figure S6,
Supporting Information). Three-hour rest periods at specific
voltage intervals were incorporated to the charge/­
discharge
measurements (Figure 4A) between 3.0–4.5 V at C/20 to allow
for the collection of the spectra. Data was collected both during
charge/discharge and rest times. The predominant redox reaction in the NFA cathode material is the nickel redox reactions,
Ni3+ ↔ Ni4+ centered around 3.75, 4.0, and 4.25 V, c­ orresponding

to the delithiation/lithiation process of the cathode material.
The collected Mössbauer spectra revealed that in the beginning
of the charge process at 3.3 V (open circuit voltage, OCV), the
oxidation state of iron in the NFA material is +3, which is similar to the spectra obtained for calcined powders. Upon delith­
iation, no change in iron oxidation state is observed until a
voltage of 3.75 V. However, a second Fe 3+ component, denoted
Fe(III)B, appears at the expense of the original component,
denoted Fe(III)A. The isomer shift of Fe(III)B is slightly larger
than for Fe(III)A, but still clearly indicative of trivalent iron.
The quadrupole splitting of Fe(III)B is significantly larger than
for Fe(III)A indicating a more distorted local environment. The
probable cause is the disorder appearing in the near-neighbor
environment during the gradual oxidation of Ni. Above 3.75 V,
further delithiation causes changes in the Mössbauer spectra
corresponding to an oxidation state change of the iron species
in the cathode (Figure 4A; inset, surface plot). The spectra can
no longer be fit using only Fe 3+ components as part of the
iron is oxidized to Fe 4+. This Fe 4+ component is characterized
by an isomer shift[57] of −0.08 mm s−1, which we kept constant
after verifying that this is warranted in preliminary fits. Moreover, a quite large quadrupole splitting of ≈1.2 mm s−1 indicates
that the local coordination of Fe 4+ likely gets highly distorted
by Jahn–Teller splitting. Note that the iron that oxidizes from
3+ to 4+ seems to be specifically those 60% that exhibit the initial distortion from Fe(III)A to Fe(III)B, whereas about 40% of
the iron retains the Fe(III)A spectral parameter throughout the
whole cycle (Figure 4B). Upon lithiation during the discharge
process, the Fe4+ species completely disappear and only the

The electrochemical performance metrics such as rate capability and cycling stability are critical figures of merit for battery cathode materials. The rate capability of the NFA variants
were assessed through galvanostatic charge/discharge tests at

capacities are shown in Figure 7A. Considerable literature
reports that the layered nickel containing cathode materials
such as NCMs and NCAs have good cycling stability and
deliver stable performances between 3 and 4.3 V.[1,4,63] However, they suffer from accelerated failure owing to detrimental

structural transitions when the voltage window is increased
to 4.5 V.[3,64] In this research, a 3 to 4.5 V voltage window was
chosen to push these materials to their stability limits. The
NFA-A (LiNi0.8Fe0.05Al0.15O2) and NFA 811 (LiNi0.8Fe0.1Al0.1O2)

compared with the NFA-F variant (≈81 mAh g−1) at the same
rate. Finally, upon lowering the C-rate from 1C to 0.1C, the
NFA-A variant achieved a capacity of ≈170 mAh g−1, which
was similar (≈5%) to the initial capacity observed at 0.1C
(≈184 mAh g−1) prior to rate capability tests. Comparatively, the
NFA-F variant delivered a low capacity of ≈138 mAh g−1 when
compared with the initial capacity of ≈190 mAh g−1, resulting
in ≈27% loss. This observation indicates that the NFA-A variant
was the most stable in these test conditions when compared
with the NFA 811 and NFA-F variants.

Experimental Section
Sol–Gel Method for the Synthesis of NFA Materials: The cathode
materials were synthesized using the sol–gel process. Appropriate molar
quantities of nickel acetate (Sigma-Aldrich), iron acetate (Sigma-Aldrich),
aluminum acetate (Sigma-Aldrich), and lithium acetate (Sigma-Aldrich)
were dissolved in a solution of citric acid (Sigma-Aldrich) and deionized
water with the ratio of citric acid to transition metals maintained at
1:1. The solution was continuously stirred and evaporated at 60 °C
until a solid gel was formed. The resulting product was dried at 100 °C
overnight followed by drying in air at 400 °C for 4 h. The final cathode
material was obtained by grinding and calcining the preheated powders
in an oxygen atmosphere at 750 °C for 20 h. Specific compositional
variants of the NFA system were synthesized using the same method by
varying the amount of the constituent elements.
Material and Electrochemical Characterizations: The microstructural
analysis of the synthesized NFA compositional variants was performed
using optical microscopes as well as a scanning electron microscope
(Zeiss Merlin). Compositional analysis of the compositional variants
was performed using energy-dispersive spectroscopy and XPS. XPS was
performed with a Thermo Scientific Model K-Alpha XPS instrument. The
K-Alpha XPS instrument uses monochromated Al Kα X-rays (1486.6 eV)
focused to spot sizes ranging from 30 to 400 µm. For this study, the
largest X-ray spot size (400 µm) was used for maximum signal over the
largest possible area. Photoelectrons were energy analyzed and detected
with a hemispherical analyzer equipped with a 128 multi-channel
detector system. NFA cathode powders were dispersed onto doublesided tape fixed to clean glass slides which were in turn attached to
the XPS sample holder with metal clips. All survey spectra (0–1350 eV)
were acquired using a pass energy setting on the analyzer of 200 eV,
while all core level spectra were acquired with a pass energy setting of
50 eV. Data were collected and processed using the Thermo Scientific
Avantage XPS software package (v 4.61). When necessary, spectra were
charge corrected by setting the main. C 1s core level peak to 284.8 eV.
The base pressure in the analysis chamber is typically 3 × 10−10 mbar.
Crystallographic assessment through XRD and neutron diffraction was
performed using the Malvern Panalytical X’Pert Pro and the POWGEN
powder diffractometer (which is at Oak Ridge National Laboratory’s
Spallation Neutron Source), respectively. For powder neutron diffraction,
powder samples (1–2 g) were loaded into 6 mm vanadium cans. Fourhour scans were collected for each powder pattern. The diffraction
data were normalized against a data from a vanadium rod and
calibrated using a powder diamond standard. The data were collected
using a center wavelength of 0.8 Å with a bandwidth of about 1 Å.
Standard NIST Si 640d was used as external calibrants for the

Electrochemical Testing and Evaluations: The prepared cathode
powders were made into a slurry with N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (SigmaAldrich) containing 10% carbon black (Denka), 10% PVDF (Solvay),
and 10% active material, and was cast onto aluminum foils (MTI)
using a doctor blade. The cast electrodes were dried, calendared, and
assembled in coin cells with a lithium foil anode (Sigma-Aldrich)
and Gen 2 electrolyte (1.2 m LiPF6 in 3/7 EC/EMC by weight).
The assembled coin cells were electrochemically evaluated through
cyclic voltammetry, galvanostatic charge/discharge tests, potentiostatic
EIS measurements, and GITT using a BioLogic testing station between
the voltage range of 3 to 4.5 V.
Galvanostatic Intermittent Titration Technique: GITT measurements
with in situ EIS were performed to determine the ionic diffusivity and
interfacial charge transfer resistance as a function of lithium content
within the cathode. In this case, the cell was charged and discharged
at 0.1C to form a stable solid electrolyte interphase layer, and then a
charging/discharging current equivalent to the C/10 rate was applied
using the BioLogic electrochemical test station. The current was applied
for 1 h to attain a certain state of charge/discharge, followed by a 2 h rest
to reach the steady-state cell voltage, and the procedure was repeated
stepwise to cover all states of charge/discharge. After partial lithiation/
delithiation, the cell was relaxed under the OCV condition to reach the
steady state where the voltage decay is less than 2 mV h−1 at the end of
the time interval. Thereafter, EIS was performed in the frequency range
of 2 MHz and 5 mHz using a sinusoidal voltage amplitude of 10 mV. The
obtained EIS spectra were fitted using an equivalent circuit model built
in ZView software. Numerical values were extracted using a complex
nonlinear least-squares regression analysis.
