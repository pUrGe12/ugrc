Accurate and rapid bacterial identification in serum is critical for the early diagnosis of sepsis. However, con­
ventional identification methods are often hindered by time-consuming procedures and complex operational
requirements. In this work, a novel intrinsic chiroptical structure-encoded single-probe sensor array is developed
based on 4-mercaptophenylboronic acid-functionalized L-type intrinsically helical gold nanorods (BA@LHGNRs). The probe specifically binds to bacterial surfaces through synergistic electrostatic attraction and co­
valent coupling, thereby modulating their NaCl-induced aggregation behavior and generating distinct circular
dichroism (CD) as chiroptical signatures with red/green/blue (RGB) signals serving as complementary colori­
metric readouts. Importantly, the CD signals exhibit significantly enhanced resolution compared to UV–vis,
enabling highly discriminative identification of seven representative pathogenic bacteria without the need for
additional energy-dissipation pathways. The sensing performance of the proposed system was systematically
validated across a wide range of bacterial concentrations and diverse bacterial species. Furthermore, the
developed probe also allows for rapid preliminary assessment of bacterial viability and successfully differentiates
clinical sepsis serum samples associated with bacterial infections. These results highlight the potential of this
sensor array for early sepsis diagnosis and the evaluation of persistent infectious pathogens in septic conditions.

1. Introduction
Sepsis caused by bacterial infections remains a chronic threat to
human health, leading to sustained pressure on healthcare systems [1].
It is estimated that several million people die from sepsis worldwide
each year [2], underscoring substantial challenges for early and precise
diagnosis [3]. Consequently, accurate identification and classification of
pathogenic bacteria are crucial for reliable clinical diagnosis and
effective treatment of sepsis [4]. Currently, bacterial culture [5] and
polymerase chain reaction [6] are widely employed for pathogen iden­
tification; nevertheless, these techniques are often time-consuming and
require specialized expertise, which limits their practicality in many
settings. Therefore, there is an urgent demand for the development of
simple, rapid, and accurate strategies for bacterial pathogen
identification.

Sensor arrays serve as a versatile analytical platform that converts
chemically diverse inputs into multidimensional response profiles,
enabling analyte discrimination through the decoding of these response
patterns [7]. In recent years, benefiting from their capability to extract
holistic information from complex chemical systems, such arrays have
attracted significant attention in the field of bacterial discrimination
[8–10]. Among them, gold nanoparticle-based colorimetric sensor ar­
rays have been emerged as a prominent platform, owing to advantages,
such as operational simplicity, tunable surface modification, rapid
response, and visual readout [11–13]. However, these conventional
colorimetric sensor arrays remain constrained by their reliance on
additional recognition elements to enhance discriminative performance
across diverse bacterial species [14–16]. To address this limitation,
single-probe sensor arrays have been developed [17]. These approaches
have somewhat diversified signal representation and increased feature

dimensionality, yet their pattern recognition generally relies either on
integrated signal elements [18] or on additional energy-dissipationderived outputs [19]. Therefore, it is of crucial importance to design
single-probe sensing systems capable of unlocking latent information
from a single physicochemical interaction and generating highresolution discriminative fingerprints through structurally sensitive
signal transduction without introducing additional energy dissipation
pathways.
Circular dichroism (CD) refers to the differential absorption of left
and right circularly polarized light, arising from the asymmetric inter­
action between the electromagnetic field and chiral geometry [20]. As a
direct consequence of the structurally encoded asymmetry, the CD signal
is tightly coupled to the stereochemical arrangement and structural
dissymmetry, endowing it with remarkable sensitivity to subtle varia­
tions in three-dimensional chiral architecture. Owing to such structuredependent optical activity, CD-based analytical strategies have been
increasingly explored for biosensing applications, especially in systems
based on inherently chiral nanomaterials [21–23]. Significantly, Kuang
and colleagues have demonstrated that inherently chiral nanoprobes not
only exhibit stereochemistry-dependent behavior during the sensing
process, but also function as a CD signal for response readout, which
demonstrate higher sensitivity compared to other readout methods
throughout the generation of the response signal [24–26]. Moreover,
our recent works about intrinsically chiral helical gold nanorods
(HGNRs) further illustrate that CD responses are more sensitive to
structural variations than conventional UV–vis absorption, providing
enhanced signal contrast for analyte discrimination within the same
reaction system [27]. Such structure-sensitive signal transduction could
amplify subtle differences among analytes, providing a promising basis
for extracting high-resolution discriminative information from singleprobe responses.
Herein, an intrinsic chiroptical structure-encoded single-probe
sensor array is developed based on L-type helical gold nanorods (LHGNRs) to extract high-resolution discriminative information for the

effective differentiation of multiple pathogenic bacteria (Scheme 1).
Positively charged L-HGNRs are functionalized with 4-mercaptophenyl­
boronic acid (4-MPBA, BA) to form BA@L-HGNRs recognition probes
capable of discriminating bacteria. These probes exhibit NaCl-induced
aggregation behavior. But in the presence of bacteria, BA@L-HGNRs
become stably anchored to the bacterial surface through a synergistic
combination of electrostatic interactions and boronic acid–cis-diol dy­
namic covalent binding, which effectively suppresses their NaCltriggered aggregation behavior [28]. The amounts of probes immobi­
lized on the bacterial surface primarily vary with the type and abun­
dance of polysaccharide molecules in the bacterial cell wall and
bacterial surface charge. Similarly, when bacterial mixtures contain
different concentrations (1:1, 1:4, etc.) and compositions (binary,
ternary mixtures), the total number of probes immobilized on the sur­
face also differs, which depends on the mixing ratio and bacterial spe­
cies, leading to CD signals accompanied by discernible red/green/blue
(RGB) chromaticity variation observed in the BA@L-HGNRs@bacteria
composites. Notably, CD responses exhibit high sensitivity to subtle
structural variations in the resulting composites, providing the pre­
dominant discriminatory information and enabling significantly
enhanced discrimination compared with conventional extinction-based
methods, while the RGB signals provide complementary colorimetric
readouts. Effective differentiation of seven representative pathogenic
bacteria is achieved with the aid of the statistical method of linear
discriminant analysis (LDA). Moreover, this single-probe sensor array
not only successfully discriminates bacterial samples across varying
concentrations and species, but also demonstrates significant potential
for bacterial differentiation within human serum matrices. Notably, the
probe enables rapid preliminary assessment of bacterial viability and
effectively distinguishes clinical sepsis samples (E. coli and E. faecium),
offering a novel and valuable approach for the early diagnosis of sepsis.

2.1. Reagents and instruments

3.1. Characterization of HGNRs and BA@HGNRs

The detailed reagents and instruments are displayed in the Sup­
porting Information.

The gold nanorods (AuNRs) displayed a smooth surface and an
average length of 90 nm (Fig. S1). In contrast, the as-prepared HGNRs
presented a rod-like morphology with a well-defined helical shell
structure and an average length of 126 nm (Fig. 1a, S2). Given that Land D-HGNRs possess identical elemental compositions, L-HGNRs, one
of the enantiomeric forms, were selected for elemental composition
characterization using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) sur­
face scanning analysis, confirming that the primary constituent elements
of the HGNRs were Au, Ag, and S (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, CD spectra
reveal that both L- and D-HGNRs exhibited mirror-image chiral re­
sponses with two distinct chiroptical peaks at 585 nm and 790 nm
(Fig. 1c), and the corresponding g-factors were − 0.0151 and 0.0150,
respectively (Fig. 1d). Additionally, AuNRs showed a longitudinal
localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) absorption at about 800
nm, while HGNRs displayed a blue-shifted LSPR absorption (Fig. S3).
These observations validated the successful construction of HGNRs with
a distinct helical configuration and inherent chiral properties. After­
wards, the appearance of a B 1 s peak in the X-ray photoelectron spec­
troscopy (XPS, Fig. 1e), together with the corresponding changes in the
CD and UV–vis spectra and zeta potential (Fig. 1f–h), provide comple­
mentary evidence supporting the incorporation of BA onto the HGNRs
surface.

2.2. Synthesis of HGNRs
HGNRs were synthesized based on the previously established
bottom-up regrowth approach [29], which is presented in the Support­
ing Information.
2.3. Preparation of BA@HGNRs probe
The obtained HGNRs were subsequently subjected to surface modi­
fication with 4-MPBA as detailed below. Initially, cetyl­
trimethylammonium bromide (CTAB)-stabilized HGNRs were
centrifuged (6800 rpm, 10 min) and redispersed in an equal volume of
KOH solution (pH 11). Subsequently, 320 μL of the above solution was
mixed with 6.4 μL of 4-MPBA solution (10 mM in ethanol solvent) in a
1.5 mL tube, which was incubated at 25 ◦ C for 12 h under slow stirring
(300 rpm) in the dark. After this, the mixture was centrifuged at 3600
rpm for 10 min, followed by resuspension and concentration in 160 μL of
10 mM NH4HCO3 to yield BA@HGNRs to act as probes, which were
stored at 4 ◦ C for subsequent bacterial detection.

3.2. Effect of constituents on the chiroptical development of BA@LHGNRs

2.4. Single-probe intrinsic chiroptical structure-encoded sensor array for
bacterial identification

Given that the intrinsic CD signal of BA@L-HGNRs is inherently
derived from the chiral plasmonic properties of L-HGNRs, the dynamic
chiroptical evolution occurring within BA@L-HGNRs can be reliably
represented by that of the pristine L-HGNRs, in consistent with the
widely reported findings in existing works [29]. In the absence of any
additional functional ligands and under strictly standardized reaction
conditions, the cysteine concentration and the proportion of Ag in the
Au/Ag growth solution demonstrate a dominant modulatory effect on
the chiroptical property evolution of HGNRs. Furthermore, systematic
experimental validation reveal that the volume of the Au/Ag growth
solution remains an independent critical parameter governing the ulti­
mate chiroptical properties of HGNRs, even when the Ag fraction in the
growth system is held at the previously optimized level. With the Cys
concentration increasing across the range of 45 to 120 μM (Fig. S4a), the
CD intensity of L-HGNRs reaches its maximum at 75 μM, resulting from
the prominent modulatory action of Cys loading on the construction of
spiral channels [29]. Notably, once the volume of the Au/Ag growth
solution is adjusted to the pre-determined optimum (80 μL), the CD
intensity of L-HGNRs manifest a clear oscillatory evolution profile
(Fig. S4b). This counterintuitive experimental phenomenon originates
from the finely tuned dynamic equilibrium between the AuNR seed
system and the Au/Ag growth medium [30], which enable precise
modulation of chiral nanostructures within L-HGNRs and further
develop their chiroptical activity. Therefore, a cysteine concentration of
75 μM and a growth solution volume of 80 μL are adopted for the sub­
sequent preparation of BA@L-HGNRs (Fig. S4 c).

Each of the seven pathogenic bacterial strains (P. aeruginosa, E. coli,
E. sakazakii, S. typhimurium, E. faecium, L. monocytogenes, S. aureus) was
cultured at 37 ◦ C for 24 h. The bacterial biomass was collected by
centrifugation, washed sequentially with sterile 0.9% NaCl solution and
ultrapure sterile water to remove extracellular metabolites and residual
nutrients, and resuspended to form uniform bacterial suspensions. After
adjusting the optical density at 600 nm to 0.1, the actual viable cell
concentration for each strain was determined via the standard plate
count method, with values recorded as: 9.2 × 107 CFU/mL
(P. aeruginosa), 8.5 × 107 CFU/mL (E. coli), 7.6 × 107 CFU/mL
(E. sakazakii), 6.4 × 107 CFU/mL (S. typhimurium), 7.4 × 107 CFU/mL
(E. faecium), 7.1 × 107 CFU/mL (L. monocytogenes), and 8.2 × 107 CFU/
mL (S. aureus).
Thereafter, discrimination work was conducted as outlined below.
Initially, 160 μL of each bacterial suspension was mixed with an equal
volume of BA@HGNRs solution in a 1.5 mL tube at 37 ◦ C for 15 min.
Following incubation, 30 μL of NaCl solution was supplemented to the
reaction vessels until the concentration eventually at 65 mM. After color
evaluation and RGB analysis with a smartphone running a color picker
App, the solution was performed with CD spectral analysis. Finally, LDA
was conducted on the obtained response data using Origin 2026 and
SPSS v27.0 software.
2.5. Analysis under practical scenarios
To evaluate the practical applicability of the proposed single-probe
sensor array, its performance in distinguishing live and dead bacteria
and analyzing serum samples was further investigated. Bacterial sus­
pensions before and after ethanol-inactivated and autoclaving were used
as representative live and dead samples, respectively. The serum sample
analysis was conducted using bacteria-spiked human serum and clinical
sepsis serum to assess the sensor array's bacterial discrimination capa­
bility in a serum matrix. Detailed experimental procedures are provided
in the Supporting Information.

3.4. Mechanism of bacterial identification and chiroptical response
behavior of BA@HGNRs
It has been well documented that BA-functionalized gold nano­
materials are prone to salt-triggered aggregation at high NaCl concen­
trations when no bacteria are present [28]. Notably, the presence of
bacteria can efficiently inhibit such aggregation process, through a
mechanism that anchors the nanoparticles onto bacterial surfaces via
the dynamic covalent interaction formed between boronic acid moieties
and cis-diol groups [33–35]. Moreover, electrostatic interactions
[36–38] between probes and bacterial surfaces could also produce

characteristic response profiles toward multiple bacterial species. These
findings enable the construction of chiroptical single-probe sensor array
based on the suppression of salt-induced aggregation. To further verify
the roles of electrostatic interactions and boronic acid–cis-diol dynamic
covalent binding in bacterial recognition, this work performed zeta
potential measurements to characterize bacterial surface charge prop­
erties and conducted free diol-mediated competitive inhibition assays to
verify boronic acid–cis-diol covalent binding. Zeta potential measure­
ments reveal the opposite surface charges between positively charged
BA@L-HGNRs and negatively charged seven bacterial strains, support­
ing electrostatic interactions as a plausible intermolecular driving force
(Fig. S8). Meanwhile, concentration-dependent free-fructose competi­
tive inhibition assays confirmed boronic acid–cis-diol covalent coupling
between the probe and bacteria (Fig. S9). These results indicate that
bacterial recognition by the constructed single-probe system is governed
by the synergistic interplay of electrostatic attraction and boronic
acid–cis-diol covalent bonding, which collectively promote favorable

attachment of the chiral probes to bacterial cell surfaces (Fig. S10).
Given the potential influence of chiral materials with distinct configu­
rations [39], the impact of probe stereochemistry on bacterial responses
is assessed. Probes with distinct stereochemical configurations exhibited
nearly identical CD response amplitudes toward the same bacterial
strain, as quantified by the |ΔCD| values plotted in Fig. 2a. While the
corresponding original CD spectra of L- and D-HGNRs-based probes
displayed opposite CD signal signs (Fig. S11), their CD response mag­
nitudes remained closely comparable. These results collectively
demonstrate that the bacterial recognition responses of the asconstructed chiral single-probe are largely independent of its inherent
stereochemical configuration. Thus, BA@L-HGNRs were employed as
the probe for subsequent work.

3.6. Concentration-dependent bacterial identification
To estimate the quantitative identification performance of the sensor
array for bacterial analysis, these bacteria are tested across a concen­
tration gradient (OD600 = 0.02–0.2). In the case of P. aeruginosa, E. coli,
E. sakazakii, and L. monocytogenes, the signal channels of the singleprobe exhibit consistent evolution as their respective concentrations
varied. This trend is also reflected in the cluster heat maps (Fig. 3a-d).
Furthermore, in all LDA score plots, the first principal component
dominated by CD signals contribute over 95% of the total variance. It
therefore derives concentration-dependent calibration relationships
from the CD signals, enabling quantitative evaluation of bacterial
detection. Moreover, CD consistently achieved lower limit of detection
(LOD) than UV–vis. P. aeruginosa had an LOD of OD600 = 0.0034 (CD,
Fig. S17a) versus 0.0066 (UV–vis, Fig. S17b), while L. monocytogenes
presented an LOD of 0.0123 (CD, Fig. S17c) versus 0.0125 (UV–vis,
Fig. S17d). These data confirm the analytical advantage of chiroptical
quantification.
Next, the response matrix from all tested bacterial strains was pro­
cessed via LDA. The resulting score plots fell into two groups: complete
discrimination with no cluster overlap, and incomplete discrimination
with partial overlap. Five strains, including P. aeruginosa, E. coli,
E. sakazakii, L. monocytogenes, and E. faecium, were fully distinguishable
(Fig. 3e-h, Fig. S18a, Tables S4-S8), while only partial discrimination
was achieved for S. typhimurium and S. aureus (Fig. S18b-c). Blind tests
were performed on four strains at unknown concentrations, which
delivered 100% classification accuracy (Tables S9-S12).
