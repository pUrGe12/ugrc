Serratia marcescens, a Gram-negative facultative anaerobe widely distributed across diverse ecological niches, is
increasingly explored as a non-model microbial chassis for industrial biotechnology. As expanding bio­
manufacturing demands require complex products and diverse feedstocks, broadening host repertoires beyond
traditional model organisms has become essential. Against this backdrop, this review evaluates the potential of
S. marcescens, synthesizing recent advances in its fundamental biology, secondary metabolism, and synthetic
biology tools. We highlight key metabolic engineering research, particularly the high-yield production of
endogenous metabolites such as prodigiosin, acetoin, and 2,3-butanediol. Furthermore, the multi-stress-tolerant
chassis HBQA7 has enabled the heterologous biosynthesis of diverse terpenoids, with several achieving recordhigh microbial titers, while its intrinsic polyamine tolerance has facilitated value-added spermidine derivative
production. While these achievements highlight its metabolic potential, translating this organism from a
promising candidate into a mature industrial platform requires systematically addressing biosafety, process
scalability, and economic viability. Ultimately, S. marcescens is positioned not to replace traditional model hosts,
but to serve as a specialized, ecologically robust platform, complementing the existing toolkit for next-generation
sustainable biomanufacturing.

1. Introduction
Metabolic engineering, through the rational design and systematic
rewiring of cellular metabolic networks, aims to enhance the biosyn­
thetic efficiency of target compounds and has emerged as one of the core
technologies in modern biotechnology [1,2]. Microbial cell factories
constructed from engineered microorganisms have demonstrated broad
application prospects in chemical production, pharmaceutical
manufacturing, and environmental remediation, providing a feasible
route toward reducing reliance on traditional petrochemical resources
and enabling green and sustainable biomanufacturing [3]. In metabolic
engineering research, the choice of chassis organism largely influences
the upper limit of pathway engineering and overall development effi­
ciency [4]. To date, metabolic engineering has primarily relied on model
microorganisms such as Escherichia coli and Saccharomyces cerevisiae.

These hosts are widely used due to their well-characterized genetics and
mature genetic manipulation systems. However, they still exhibit
several inherent limitations in practical applications, including
restricted substrate utilization spectra, insufficient adaptability to
complex environmental conditions, and the lack of native pathways
required for the biosynthesis of certain complex natural products. These
constraints often hinder their performance in the biosynthesis of com­
plex natural products or non-canonical compounds [5].
To overcome these limitations, the exploration of non-model mi­
croorganisms with unique physiological and metabolic traits has
become a pivotal trend in metabolic engineering [6]. Through long-term
ecological adaptation, these microorganisms have evolved specialized
metabolic advantages, such as versatile substrate utilization spectra,
exceptional environmental stress tolerance, or the intrinsic ability to
efficiently produce specific high-value metabolites [6]. For example,

Halomonas exhibit high salt and alkali tolerance and strong resistance to

With the rapid advancement of genome sequencing, systems biology,
and cutting-edge genome editing technologies, the genetic tractability of
non-model microorganisms has been significantly improved, opening
new horizons for constructing diversified microbial cell factories [4].
Among various non-model microorganisms, Serratia marcescens has
attracted increasing attention due to its distinctive physiological and
metabolic features. Compared with conventional model organisms, this
bacterium exhibits strong tolerance to diverse physicochemical stresses,
including exceptional resistance to organic solvents in some strains. This
property provides inherent advantages for the production of hydro­
phobic compounds such as terpenoids and for biphasic fermentation
processes [8,9]. In addition, its flexible carbon utilization capacity and
efficient chitin degradation system enable the conversion of low-cost
renewable biomass resources into value-added products [10]. In addi­
tion, it naturally produces prodigiosin and other bioactive secondary
metabolites with important biological functions [11].
With the development of genetic manipulation technologies, the
genetic tractability of S. marcescens has progressively improved. Early
engineering relied mainly on homologous recombination and counterselection systems such as sacB, followed by the development of λ-Red
recombineering and CRISPR-Cas systems [12,13], enabling gene dele­
tion, site-specific integration, and genome reconstruction.
Beyond genome editing, plasmid-based expression systems and ge­
netic elements, including origins of replication and promoters, have also
been developed. Plasmids with different origins of replication provide
flexible platforms for heterologous gene expression, while endogenous
promoter screening has generated resources with diverse expression
strengths [14,15]. Chromosomal integration sites further enable stable
expression of heterologous genes and pathways [16]. However, sys­
tematic RBS libraries remain lacking, and higher-order synthetic biology
tools such as RNA-level regulation and modular genetic circuits are still
underdeveloped. Together with the limited portability of genetic tools
across strains, these gaps constrain the development of S. marcescens as a
standardized and broadly applicable microbial chassis.
With these genetic tools in place, metabolic engineering has
expanded from modification of native pathways to the construction of
heterologous pathways. Native products such as prodigiosin, acetoin,
and 2,3-butanediol have been produced at high levels through gene
deletion or overexpression, precursor supply enhancement, regulatory
rewiring, cofactor engineering, and enzyme engineering, with repre­
sentative titers reaching the 10 g L− 1 scale [17,18]. For heterologous
production, S. marcescens HBQA7 has shown particular potential for
terpenoid biosynthesis. Introduction of different MVA pathways com­
bined with precursor optimization, competing-pathway deletion, and
enzyme engineering has enabled high-level production of various
monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes [19–21]. Maximum titers of 70.50,
60.32, and 102.3 g L− 1 have been reported for (E)-nerolidol, β-car­
yophyllene, and (− )-α-bisabolol, respectively [22–24]. Heterologous
pathway construction, including methanol assimilation, has also
expanded its substrate utilization and biosynthetic capabilities [25].
Despite these advances, metabolic engineering remains concentrated
in a limited number of advantageous strains, and strain-specific genetic
and regulatory differences constrain the portability of engineering tools
and strategies. Although recent reviews have covered the pathogenicity,
virulence, antimicrobial resistance, and selected biotechnological ap­
plications of S. marcescens, a systematic evaluation of its genetic engi­
neering tools, synthetic biology resources, and metabolic engineering
from the perspective of a non-model microbial chassis remains lacking
[26–28]. This review therefore summarizes the development of
S. marcescens from genetic manipulation and synthetic biology tools to
native and heterologous product biosynthesis, identifies key techno­
logical gaps, and discusses its prospects and challenges as a broadly
applicable microbial chassis. Finally, we consider biosafety, process

compatibility, and product positioning to outline future directions for its
industrial development.
2. General characteristics of S. marcescens
2.1. Taxonomy and ecological distribution
As the type species of the genus Serratia [29], S. marcescens is clas­
sified within the class Gammaproteobacteria, order Enterobacterales,
and family Yersiniaceae (Fig. 1) [30]. First isolated in 1819, its
nomenclature evolved through Monas prodigiosus and Bacillus prodigiosus
before the valid name Serratia marcescens was reinstated [31]. Its taxo­
nomic identification has transitioned from phenotype-based ap­
proaches, relying on traits such as Gram-negative rod morphology,
facultative anaerobic metabolism, and broad carbon utilization [32], to
molecular approaches based on 16S rRNA and multilocus sequence
analysis [29]. Additionally, comparative genomics has reclassified it
from the family Enterobacteriaceae to Yersiniaceae [33].
S. marcescens exhibits a broad ecological distribution, inhabiting
diverse natural environments including soil, water, plant rhizospheres,
and animal intestinal tracts [26]. It also demonstrates remarkable
adaptability in anthropogenic settings, surviving physicochemical
stresses in industrial, food-processing, and clinical environments
[34,35], and has even been isolated from the International Space Sta­
tion, highlighting its resilience in closed artificial systems [36].
This broad distribution provides a rich genetic reservoir for chassis
development, enabling the screening of isolates with desirable traits like
stress tolerance or high productivity.
2.2. Phenotypic and genomic characteristics
S. marcescens is a Gram-negative, short rod-shaped bacillus typically
observed as single cells [37]. It forms smooth, mucoid colonies and
possesses peritrichous flagella conferring strong motility [38]. Its bio­
film formation and adhesion capabilities are closely linked to environ­
mental adaptation and pathogenic potential [39].
Intracellular prodigiosin accumulation yields characteristic red/pink
colonies, serving as a key physiological marker for laboratory identifi­
cation (Fig. 2A) [40]. Production is strongly temperature-dependent,
peaking at 25–28 ◦ C and suppressed at ≥37 ◦ C [41]. However, not all
strains produce prodigiosin; the biosynthetic gene cluster exists only in a
subset of biogroups, causing substantial pigmentation variation (Fig. 2B)
[42].
The genome ranges from 4.5 to 6.1 Mb (~59% GC) and exhibits high
plasticity (Fig. 2C, D) [42,43]. A pan-genome analysis of 45 isolates
revealed 19,469 total genes, of which only 3155 (~16.2%) are core
genes, highlighting extensive accessory and strain-specific diversity
[44]. Additional studies have similarly reported extensive genomic di­
versity within the species (Fig. 2E) [45,46]. Plasmids further drive this
plasticity, with S. marcescens harboring ~73% of plasmids identified in
Serratia; ~72% are conjugative or mobilizable, facilitating horizontal
gene transfer and cross-genus gene flow (Fig. 2F) [42].
For chassis engineering, this plasticity is a double-edged sword. The
accessory genome provides a wealth of harnessable metabolic functions,
yet extensive strain variability and mobile genetic elements challenge
engineering standardization and genetic stability. A key strategy is
therefore to identify a genetically stable strain background through
comparative genomics and to remove dispensable mobile or strainspecific regions that compromise stability, thereby reducing genomic
variability while retaining desirable metabolic traits. Managing this
plasticity is essential for developing S. marcescens as a reliable industrial
chassis.

exploiting individual endogenous traits rather than systematic meta­
bolic reprogramming. Bridging this gap, from utilizing what
S. marcescens naturally provides to engineering its potential as a true
chassis, is the central theme of subsequent sections.

3. Potential of S. marcescens as a chassis
An effective industrial chassis must satisfy four key criteria: (i) robust
growth and stress tolerance (e.g., temperature, pH, osmotic pressure,
product toxicity) under industrial conditions; (ii) utilization of inex­
pensive, renewable feedstocks; (iii) metabolic versatility for native or
heterologous biosynthesis; and (iv) a mature genetic toolbox for precise
engineering [60]. Accordingly, this section evaluates the functional
advantages of S. marcescens regarding environmental adaptability,
substrate utilization, and secondary metabolism, followed by a review of
recent advances in its genetic manipulation and synthetic biology tools.
3.1. Environmental adaptability
S. marcescens exhibits strong environmental adaptability, enabling
sustained growth across diverse extreme niches, from marine sediments
to glacier soils [61,62]. Strains isolated from different environments
demonstrate remarkable tolerance to individual physicochemical
stresses. For example, marine strain MBBTBC9 survives temperatures of
4–50 ◦ C, 0.5–7% NaCl, pH 7–13, and heavy metals (up to 500 ppm) [61].
Himalayan glacier strain MTCC 4822 grows at 4–45 ◦ C and pH 3–14,
producing laccase under low-temperature, nutrient-limited conditions
[62].
Beyond single stresses, the species adapts to complex, multi-stressor
environments, such as strongly alkaline olive-processing waste [63],
high-salinity leather wastewater [64], and thermogenic composting
systems [65].
Certain strains exhibit remarkable tolerance to organic solvents and
hydrophobic compounds. Strain IBBPo15 survives high concentrations
(>40%) of solvents like cyclohexane and n-hexane [9,66]. Strain
HBQA7 maintains robust growth in 1.0 g L− 1 geraniol (which strongly
inhibits E. coli) and tolerates up to 2 M methanol, surpassing many

selection and targeted improvement of strains for specific biotechno­
logical applications.

3.2. Substrate utilization capacity
The central carbon metabolism of S. marcescens comprises a
conserved core framework consisting of the EMP, PPP, TCA cycle, and
ED pathway [72], which provides key regulatory nodes for carbon flux
distribution and nutritional adaptation [73]. Consequently, research has
not focused on structural network differences, but rather on carbon
source transport, substrate assimilation routes, and intracellular flux
allocation strategies.
3.2.1. Carbon source transport and metabolism
S. marcescens exhibits broadly conserved substrate utilization pat­
terns (Table 1). Commonly utilized substrates include simple sugars (e.
g., glucose, fructose, GlcNAc), oxidized sugar derivatives (e.g., gluco­
nate), TCA cycle-associated organic acids (e.g., citrate, malate), polyols
(e.g., glycerol, mannitol), and amino acids (e.g., aspartate, glutamate).
These substrates are readily assimilated into the EMP, PPP, ED, or TCA
pathways.

The uptake of common carbon sources relies on specialized transport
systems (Fig. 4). Sugars such as glucose, fructose, sucrose, and GlcNAc
are primarily transported via the phosphoenolpyruvate-dependent
phosphotransferase system (PTS), which couples uptake with simulta­
neous phosphorylation. This allows direct entry into the EMP pathway
as key metabolites like Glc6P and Frc6P [74]. When preferred carbon
sources become limiting, alternative uptake systems activate: ABC
transporters utilize ATP hydrolysis to import D-galactose, D-ribose, and
maltose; MFS transporters exploit ion gradients to import D-gluconate
and D-galacturonate; and glycerol enters via MIP channels for rapid
transmembrane diffusion.
Carbon flux distribution varies substantially depending on the car­
bon source. With glucose as the primary carbon source, metabolic flux is
predominantly directed through the EMP pathway and the TCA cycle for
oxidative metabolism and energy generation. Conversely, when GlcNAc
serves as the sole carbon source, a larger proportion of flux is redirected

toward the PPP, accompanied by reduced TCA cycle activity. This shift
increases the supply of NADPH and biosynthetic precursors like ribose5-phosphate, shifting carbon allocation from energy generation toward
anabolic metabolism [72].

3.4. Genetic accessibility and emerging synthetic biology tools
Early genetic manipulation of S. marcescens relied on homologous
recombination with counter-selection systems (e.g., sacB-based strate­
gies), which were lengthy and inefficient. To improve editing efficiency,
Chen et al. developed a rapid λ-Red recombination platform requiring
no prior host engineering [13]. This system uses short ~39 bp homol­
ogous arms for efficient gene replacement and supports large-fragment
deletions up to ~21 kb. By integrating an AraC/PBAD controlled kill
counter-selection system with ssDNA-mediated recombineering, it ach­
ieved the first scarless genome modifications in S. marcescens.
Recently, CRISPR-Cas technologies have substantially expanded en­
gineering capabilities. Gou et al. established a dual-plasmid CRISPRCas9 system using a stationary-phase promoter to drive Cas9 expression
without external inducers [12]. This system enables highly efficient
gene deletion, targeted integration, and single-nucleotide mutagenesis,
with overall efficiencies exceeding 80% across multiple strains.
The development of regulatory elements is equally essential. Pan
et al. identified 32 endogenous promoters from strain JNB5–1, estab­
lishing the first constitutive promoter library for this species [14]. Liu
et al. screened endogenous stationary-phase promoters in HBQA7,
generating a library spanning a ~ 43-fold expression range that enables
fine-tuned, inducer-free gene expression [15]. Additionally, Wang et al.

3.3. Secondary metabolite biosynthesis potential
S. marcescens possesses a remarkable capacity for natively synthe­
sizing diverse secondary metabolites without metabolic engineering.
These include tripyrrole pigments (prodigiosin), lipopeptide bio­
surfactants (serrawettins, via NRPS pathways encoded by swrW and
swrA), polyketide antibiotics (oocydin A), hybrid NRPS-PKS antibiotics
(althiomycin), and alkaloids (serratin) [87–90].
Among these, prodigiosin is the highest-producing representative,
with substantial accumulation achievable in wild-type strains solely
through fermentation optimization. For example, in solid-state
fermentation, strain UCP 1549 produced 119.8 g kg− 1 dry substrate
using wheat bran and waste soybean oil [91]. In submerged fermenta­
tion, strain FZSF02 achieved 15,420.9 mg L− 1 with olive oil supple­
mentation [92]. These studies demonstrate a strong inherent
biosynthetic capacity unlockable via process engineering.
This potential is underpinned by a highly integrated precursor supply
network. Different metabolite classes draw precursors from distinct
central metabolic branches: prodigiosin requires pyruvate and specific
amino acids; serrawettins depend on fatty acid and amino acid

Plasmid backbones and their replication systems provide another
important basis for genetic engineering in S. marcescens. Several estab­
lished plasmid backbones carrying broad-host-range origins, including
pBBR1, RK2, and pRO1600 in pBBR1MCS, pRK415, and pUCP18,
respectively, as well as p15A- and pMB1-based plasmids such as
pACYC184, pSU36, pBAD33, and pBAD18-Kan, have been reported to
be maintained in S. marcescens [12,96,97]. These diverse replication
systems provide a broad reference for plasmid backbone selection.
However, most studies have focused on individual strains and specific
plasmids, and the host compatibility, copy number, genetic stability,
and expression burden of different replicons across S. marcescens strains
remain poorly characterized. Systematic comparison and optimization
of replication systems will therefore be important for more rational
plasmid design.
Despite these advances, the genetic toolbox still lags behind model
chasses, slowing the design–build–test–learn cycle. A major obstacle is
the endogenous nuclease NucA, which degrades exogenous DNA and
recombination templates, reducing transformation and recombination
efficiencies [68,98]. Mitigation strategies include nucA deletion, adding
EDTA to PCR reactions, and cloning homologous arms into plasmid
vectors [12,68,99]. Broader systemic limitations remain: simultaneous
multi-locus editing and large-fragment deletions are unreported; tools
like promoter libraries and recombineering protocols are validated in
only a few isolates, limiting portability; regulatory tools such as CRISPRi
and CRISPRa are lacking; and standardized modular cloning frameworks
and broad-host-range vectors remain bottlenecks [81].
Overall, these continuously refined editing and regulatory toolkits
have markedly improved the genetic accessibility of S. marcescens

4.1. Prodigiosin production
Prodigiosin biosynthesis is directed by the pigA–pigN gene cluster,
following a convergent modular logic (Fig. 7) [27]. Two intermediates,
MAP (from 2-octenal and pyruvate) and MBC (from L-proline, L-serine,
L-methionine, and malonyl-CoA), are independently synthesized and
condensed by PigC [93]. This modular architecture engages multiple
metabolic branches (fatty acid, glycolysis, amino acid, one‑carbon
metabolism), providing multiple intervention points for engineering.
In S. marcescens, research on metabolic regulation, particularly
transcriptional control, is predominantly centered on the pig cluster.
Consequently, summarizing these mechanisms is essential not only for
directly guiding prodigiosin overproduction, but also for understanding
the species' global regulatory networks, which can inform the engi­
neering of other value-added products. Transcription of the pig cluster is
governed by a multilayered regulatory network integrating physiolog­
ical and environmental signals (Table 3). Positive regulators (PigP, PsrA,
OxyR) activate pig expression [111–113], while negative regulators
(HexS, RcsB, MetR, CRP) suppress it [114–116]. Two-component sys­
tems further modulate production: EnvZ/OmpR and CpxA/CpxR pro­
mote expression [117,118], whereas RssA/RssB and BarA/UvrY exert
repressive effects [119,120]. Quorum sensing (QS) circuits (SpnI/SpnR,
LuxS/AI-2) and alternative σ factors (RpoS, RpoH) contribute to celldensity- and growth-phase-dependent regulation, respectively
[121–123]. Notably, regulatory architecture varies significantly among
strains [124]. Relieving transcriptional repression, amplifying positive
signals, and rewiring environmental sensing circuits are effective stra­
tegies for redirecting flux toward prodigiosin.

the pig cluster; the engineered strain PG yielded 5.83 g L− 1 prodigiosin
(~42% higher than wild-type) [101,117]. Pan et al. used the strong

Prodigiosin production is temperature-dependent due to the
decreased expression and stability of PigF and PigN at higher tempera­

Regulating cell membrane permeability effectively relieves feedback
inhibition. Through transposon mutagenesis, Pan et al. identified
peptidoglycan synthase DacA as a negative regulator. Knocking out dacA
in strain SK4–72 altered cell morphology and increased membrane
permeability, facilitating intracellular prodigiosin release and upregu­
lating pigA, resulting in a 2.46-fold yield increase over wild-type JNB5–1
[100].

tolerance and prodigiosin production. Wang et al. evolved strain SDSPY136 for 120 generations under high osmotic pressure, yielding the
tolerant strain R82. In 5 L fed-batch fermentation, R82 reached 11.4 g
L− 1 prodigiosin (~double the original strain), with transcriptomic

precursor supply and product release [18].
4.2. Acetoin and 2,3-butanediol production
Acetoin and 2,3-butanediol (2,3-BD) are important bio-based plat­
form chemicals. Acetoin is widely used in foods, cosmetics, agriculture,
and the chemical industry, serving as a precursor for 2,3-BD, liquid
hydrocarbon fuels, and heterocyclic compounds [130]. 2,3-BD can be
converted into butadiene and methyl ethyl ketone, and has broad ap­
plications in printing inks, perfumes, plasticizers, and pharmaceuticals
[131].
Their biosynthesis shares a common pathway: pyruvate from
glycolysis is converted to α-acetolactate by α-acetolactate synthase
(ALS), decarboxylated by α-acetolactate decarboxylase (ALSD) to form
acetoin, and reduced to 2,3-BD by 2,3-butanediol dehydrogenase (BDH)
in an NADH/NAD+-dependent reaction. Thus, acetoin is both the direct
precursor of 2,3-BD and an independent target product, tightly coupling
their biosynthesis and allowing accumulation via metabolic flux redis­
tribution and cofactor balancing.

S. marcescens HBQA7 has emerged as a potent non-model chassis for
heterologous biosynthesis, particularly for terpenoids. Wang et al.
established a universal terpenoid platform via site-specific multicopy
integration of Mevalonate (MVA) pathway and terpene synthase genes

methylotrophic pathway for methanol assimilation [25]. Using meth­

1256.41 mg L− 1 (− )-α-bisabolol, demonstrating feasibility for reducing
costs via non-sugar C1 feedstocks.
The heterologous capability of HBQA7 extends beyond terpenoids.
Exploiting its spermidine tolerance, Gou et al. established a carbox­
yaminopropylagmatine (CAPA) pathway [110]. Through enzyme
screening, deleting spermidine degradation gene speG, attenuating
competing pathways, and optimizing transport, the strain produced 5.8
g L− 1 spermidine in a 5-L bioreactor, outperforming E. coli BL21(DE3)
(1.16 g L− 1) [133].
Overall, HBQA7 has been successfully engineered for diverse valueadded compounds, achieving state-of-the-art titers, particularly in
mono- and sesquiterpene production. Despite these promising examples,
the repertoire of heterologous compounds currently producible in
S. marcescens remains relatively limited. Considering its strong precursor
supply capacity and tolerance to hydrophobic products, there is un­
tapped potential for fatty acid derivatives (e.g., fatty alcohols, fatty acid
ethyl esters, alkanes) [134], polyketides (e.g., emodin-type aromatic
polyketides) [135], and longer-chain terpenoids (e.g., taxadiene, squa­
lene, β-carotene).
It must be acknowledged, however, that a critical challenge lies in
transferring these strategies to other S. marcescens isolates. Strains like
HBQA7 and JNB5–1 were rationally selected for distinct phenotypic
advantages, not as random vessels. Consequently, while top-down
metabolic “methodologies” (e.g., enhancing acetyl-CoA supply, block­
ing by-products) rely on conserved biochemistry and are highly
portable, strain-specific genetic “techniques” face significant uncer­
tainty. Due to strain-level heterogeneity, such as divergent transcrip­
tional regulation, chromosomal variations, and restriction-modification
barriers, specific promoters, vectors, or regulatory elements often
behave unpredictably in new host backgrounds. This dichotomy be­
tween conserved metabolic logic and strain-specific tool efficacy re­
mains a core bottleneck for broader non-model microorganism
application. Crucially, overcoming this bottleneck requires decoding the
deep genetic mechanisms underlying these specialized phenotypes,
thereby enabling the transition from empirical strain mining to rational,
universally transferable chassis engineering.

RG2–RG4, the NIH does not automatically classify them as RG1, which
refers to biological agents that are not associated with disease in healthy
adult humans; rather, their risk should be evaluated based on their
known and potential biological characteristics [138]. Therefore, the
biosafety risk of S. marcescens should not be uniformly assigned on the
basis of species identity alone, but should instead be evaluated
comprehensively according to the genetic background, known virulence
determinants, other relevant functional traits, and the intended appli­

particularly important for strains intended for industrial production.
Several functional modules associated with the potential pathoge­
nicity of S. marcescens have been systematically investigated, providing
a foundation for engineering approaches aimed at reducing the potential
risks of chassis strains. Important reported virulence-associated func­
tions include adhesion and motility systems, biofilm formation, extra­
cellular toxins and proteases, secretion systems, iron acquisition
systems, and genetic elements associated with antibiotic resistance [26].
Because these functions are mediated by genes or gene clusters with
defined genetic bases, they provide identifiable targets for chassis safety
engineering. Multiple pathogenicity-associated phenotypes of
S. marcescens can be substantially attenuated or eliminated through the
inactivation or deletion of specific genes, including those encoding
extracellular virulence factors, proteases, and secretion-associated
components. Even complex functional systems involved in adhesion,
colonization, and motility, such as fimbriae and flagella, can be dis­
rupted through deletion of key structural or assembly genes, thereby
abolishing the corresponding phenotypes [139]. In addition, plasmids
carrying antibiotic resistance genes and other non-essential resistanceassociated genetic elements can be directly eliminated through genetic
engineering, thereby removing the corresponding resistance phenotypes
and reducing the potential for horizontal transfer of resistance de­
terminants [140].

the use of a microorganism in all industrial settings. Production strains
carrying certain biosafety risks may still be employed under appropriate
containment facilities, process controls, and biosafety management
systems. Their practical feasibility depends on multiple factors,
including strain-specific risk, production scale, intended product use,
and applicable regulatory requirements [141]. Indeed, several micro­

5.2. Process compatibility and scale-up challenges
Despite the successful laboratory-scale fermentation production of
various products using S. marcescens, the scale-up of production with
this species still faces substantial challenges [147]. Fermentation scaleup involves complex changes beyond simple volume expansion,
impacting oxygen transfer, nutrient distribution, shear stress and
cellular physiology [148,149]. Current industrial fermentation research
for S. marcescens is mostly limited to small-scale systems, with few sys­
tematic investigations into high-cell-density cultivation and large-scale
bioreactor operation. Though its facultative-anaerobic trait confers ox­
ygen tolerance, high-density cultures still suffer from multiple con­
straints. Scale-up induces spatial heterogeneity of oxygen, nutrients and
byproducts [149], which triggers metabolic heterogeneity and impairs
product yield and batch-to-batch reproducibility. Since different target
products demand specific dissolved‑oxygen and feeding regimes,
establishing standardized, scalable fermentation protocols constitutes a
key bottleneck for industrialization.
Beyond fermentation scale-up, the complex native metabolism of
S. marcescens further complicates process control. It naturally produces
an array of secondary metabolites that act as metabolic burdens during
heterologous biosynthesis. Endogenous pathways compete with syn­
thetic circuits for precursors and energy, diverting carbon flux toward
off-target metabolites. More importantly, these native metabolites can
also directly interfere with downstream processing, an effect that is
often underestimated during chassis development. Therefore, fermen­
tation optimization alone is insufficient; future efforts must integrate
systematic metabolic engineering with precise gene editing to delete,
attenuate, or dynamically regulate competing pathways, ensuring the
rational allocation of cellular resources.
Furthermore, robust physiological traits like motility and biofilm
formation hinder industrial scalability by causing wall adhesion,
reduced mass transfer, and culture heterogeneity. Under long-term highdensity cultivation, metabolic burdens also drive adaptive mutations
that degrade production phenotypes [150]. Future chassis development
must prioritize genetic stability through chromosomal integration,
genome stabilization, and dynamic expression regulation.
In addition to upstream fermentation processes, downstream sepa­
ration, purification, and processing operations constitute critical de­
terminants of the industrial viability of S. marcescens [151]. Its native
12

L. Wang et al.

Chemical Engineering Journal 549 (2026) 182355

production of pigments, extracellular proteins, and biosurfactants
complicates the broth composition and significantly hinders product
recovery. In particular, serrawettins and other endogenous bio­
surfactants promote broth emulsification, severely impeding organicsolvent extraction and biphasic separation of hydrophobic compounds
such as terpenoids [152]. Meanwhile, prodigiosin and other pigments
complicate decolorization and subsequent refining, increasing purifi­
cation complexity and potentially compromising overall process eco­
nomics [153]. These interference effects highlight that downstream
processability should be treated as an intrinsic property of the produc­
tion chassis rather than as a problem addressed only after fermentation.
Accordingly, the development of S. marcescens as an industrial chassis
for terpenoid biosynthesis should integrate strain engineering with
downstream-process considerations from the outset, including the tar­
geted knockout or attenuation of endogenous secondary-metabolite and
biosurfactant pathways when appropriate. Such a strategy could
simultaneously reduce metabolic competition, improve broth charac­
teristics, facilitate two-phase extraction, and lower downstream pro­
cessing costs, thereby enhancing the overall economic feasibility of
S. marcescens-based biomanufacturing [154].

Overall, S. marcescens offers distinct opportunities for bio­
manufacturing through its native metabolism, environmental adapt­
ability, and versatile substrate utilization. Continued advances in chassis
safety, fermentation optimization, and systematic metabolic engineer­
ing will further expand its potential for specialized biomanufacturing
applications.
6. Conclusions and perspective
The development of S. marcescens as a typical non-model microbial
chassis exemplifies the future trajectory of industrial biomanufacturing:
a shift from a reliance on “a few standardized hosts” toward a “diver­
sified, function-specific chassis ecosystem.” Although mature industrial
microorganisms like E. coli and S. cerevisiae remain dominant, their
limitations in complex natural product synthesis, specialized substrate
utilization, and high-stress production environments have driven the
exploration of novel chassis resources featuring unique ecological ad­
aptations [4]. As synthesized from current research, the application
value of S. marcescens lies in leveraging its specialized metabolic capa­
bilities shaped by long-term ecological adaptation, its rich potential for
natural product synthesis, and its increasingly sophisticated engineering
toolkit to address specific biomanufacturing challenges.
Currently, research surrounding S. marcescens has progressively
expanded from traditional ecological function and natural product dis­
covery to metabolic engineering and synthetic biology applications. Its
capacity for native product synthesis (e.g., prodigiosin), complex sub­
strate utilization systems, potential for high-value compound production
(e.g., terpenoids), and expanding repertoire of gene editing and regu­
latory tools have laid a solid foundation for constructing novel engi­
neered strains. However, compared to mature industrial chassis,
S. marcescens remains in the early stages of development; its industrial
application is collectively constrained by biosafety concerns, scalability
and fermentation compatibility, and product economics.
Moving forward, the further development of S. marcescens requires a
transition from single-product optimization to systematic chassis con­
struction. On one hand, more controllable, low-risk engineered chassis
must be established through genome-level safety modifications, regu­
lation of virulence-related functions, and genetic biocontainment stra­
tegies [156]. On the other hand, strain design and optimization
efficiency must be enhanced through the integration of systematic
metabolic engineering, AI-assisted design, and high-throughput engi­
neering technologies. Furthermore, establishing application scenarios
aligned with its unique advantages will be critical for commercializa­
tion, including the bioconversion of renewable resources like chitin, the
development of high-value natural products that are challenging for
traditional hosts, and the production of complex chemicals leveraging its
exceptional tolerance [4,60]. Importantly, fully realizing this potential
requires a shift from empirical phenotypic utilization to decoding their
underlying genetic mechanisms, enabling the rational customization
and even cross-species transfer of these complex traits.
In summary, the future of S. marcescens lies in developing its
distinctive physiological and metabolic properties into practical ad­
vantages for targeted biomanufacturing applications. By embracing the
diversity of microbial life and matching specific strains to targeted
biomanufacturing challenges, S. marcescens and similar non-model hosts
will play an indispensable role in driving the next generation of sus­
tainable and resilient bioeconomies.

5.3. Product suitability and competitive positioning
Because the need for strain-specific biosafety risk assessment and the
challenges associated with production scale-up may increase R&D and
regulatory costs, the commercialization of S. marcescens requires clear
economic justification. Rather than directly competing with highly
optimized traditional chassis like E. coli and S. cerevisiae, S. marcescens
must leverage its unique physiological traits to establish differentiated
competitiveness. Target product selection should therefore integrate
native metabolic advantages, economic value, host compatibility, and
sustainable feedstock utilization.
First, leveraging native specialty metabolites remains the most direct
application path [6]. Compounds like prodigiosin possess evolutionarily
refined biosynthetic networks, offering higher metabolic compatibility
and development efficiency than de novo pathway reconstruction in
model hosts. Furthermore, genome mining of its underexplored sec­
ondary metabolite clusters holds substantial potential for discovering
novel bioactive molecules. For products where the host natively pos­
sesses synthetic capabilities or precursor networks, S. marcescens holds a
distinct advantage.
Second, target selection must be dictated by market economics, not
just technical feasibility [154]. Competing on bulk chemicals against
mature chemical processes or established strains is economically unvi­
able. Instead, S. marcescens is better suited for high-value-added prod­
ucts, such as natural products, pharmaceutical intermediates, fine
chemicals, and complex stereoisomers, whose high unit values can offset
the additional costs of non-model chassis development and biosafety
containment [155].
Additionally, host physiology determines product suitability. Longterm environmental adaptation has endowed S. marcescens with robust
stress-response mechanisms, enabling tolerance to compounds that
severely inhibit model hosts. For instance, hydrophobic terpenoids often
cause membrane toxicity in E. coli and S. cerevisiae, whereas
S. marcescens exhibits superior host-product compatibility. Thus, it is
ideally positioned as a supplementary platform for specialty natural
products, high-value terpenoids (e.g., (S)-limonene and nerolidol), and
functional metabolites (e.g., spermidine).
Finally, its highly efficient chitin degradation system offers unique
opportunities in sustainable biomanufacturing [10]. Utilizing low-cost,
chitin-rich biomass (e.g., crustacean waste) as feedstock not only
broadens raw material availability but also enables marine waste valo­
rization. Coupled with metabolic engineering, this establishes a novel
production paradigm that integrates resource recycling with green
manufacturing, distinguishing S. marcescens from most traditional
chassis.
