the increasing applied voltage gave a positive eﬀect in improving performance. Moreover, the Ni loading

Introduction

The International Energy Agency (IEA) has reported that
global energy-related carbon dioxide emissions, one of the
main components of greenhouse gases, increased to 37.8
billion tons in 2024 due to high energy demand and extremely
rapid economic recovery aer a disruption.1 Furthermore, the
most recent IEA study found that overall energy-related
carbon dioxide emissions increased by 0.8% in 2024 and
reached a new record high of 37.8 Gt.1 For this reason,
numerous attempts have been devoted to reducing these
pollution problems, and researchers are developing new
routes to convert them into useful and valuable products. The
reforming of methane (CH4) with carbon dioxide (CO2),
known as dry reforming of methane (DRM) (eqn (1)), has
attracted signicant interest as a sustainable alternative due
to its ability to reduce CH4 and CO2 greenhouse gases and
produce a valuable synthesis gas (H2 + CO), which is used as

including electrons and radicals with high energies. DBD
(dielectric discharge barrier), a type of non-thermal plasma,
has been intensively investigated for plasma-catalytic DRM
reactions.4–13 The integration of low-temperature plasma with
heterogeneous catalysis is regarded as synergistically
enhancing the CH4 and CO2 conversions, and improving
product selectivity, stability and sintering tolerance.
Ni-based catalysts are the most common ones employed in
catalytic DRM reaction, due to their promising activity and
selectivity, inexpensive price and widespread availability.2 Many
researchers have been conducted to improve catalyst deactivation and increase catalytic performance on Ni-based catalysts3
and SiO2-supported Ni catalyst.14–18 SiO2 ber (SF) is an attractive support material due to its non-porous structure, contributing to easy accessibility and exposed active sites. Fiber-like
structure supports e.g. SiO2 ber,18–21 CeO2 ber22 and ZSM-5
ber23 were studied in previous researches. Wen et al.18 reported the SiO2 nanobre conned Ni catalyst can improve Ni
dispersion, leading to slightly higher initial activity and much
better stability than Ni on commercial SiO2 powder support for
CO2 reforming of methane process. Mhadmhan et al.20 reported
that Ni/SF catalyst gave higher catalytic performance, hydrogen
production and stability than the conventional Ni on SiO2
porous (Ni/SP) catalyst in ethanol steam reforming. Ni/SiO2
ber catalyst has much strong metal-support interactions
(SMSI) and more abundant basic sites, leading to improved
coke resistance property. Herminio et al.22 reported that Ni/
CeO2 brous catalyst for CO2 reforming of methane showed
high resistance against carbon formation and enhanced catalyst
stability aer 30 hours at 700 °C. Besides, Harnsinudom et al.11
demonstrated, for the rst time, the full and partial packing of
nickel catalyst on commercial glass wool support in a planar
plate DBD plasma reactor for simulated biogas reforming. The
result revealed that the DBD plasma system with partially
catalyst packing obviously better performed than the ones with
fully catalyst packing in the plasma zone-occurring at a gap
between two planar plate stainless steel electrodes.
In this research, the syngas production from dry reforming
of methane was investigated in a planar plate DBD plasma
reactor packed with Ni/SiO2 ber catalyst at room temperature
and atmospheric pressure, with no additional heating. The

2 Experimental
2.1

SiO2 ber (SF) preparation

SiO2 ber was synthesized by sol–gel assisted electrospinning
technique using tetraethyl orthosilicate (99.0% TEOS,
Si(OC2H5)4) purchased from Sigma-Aldrich as a Si source. TEOS
was dissolved in deionized water (DI water) with a 1 : 2 ratio
followed by 37% HCl and 99.9% ethanol with a 0.01 : 2 ratio
under stirring at room temperature. Aer that, the solution was
continuously stirred and heated at 55 °C to form the gel. Finally,
the mixed gel was transferred into a syringe with a 0.55 mm
diameter needle for SF synthesis by electrospinning technique.
The electrospinning method was carried out by controlling
a distance of 15 cm between the needle tip and electrode, an
injection rate of 10 mL h−1 and an applied electric voltage of 15
kV. The obtained SF support was dried overnight at 110 °C and
calcined at 500 °C for 2 hours, as shown in Fig. 1.20
2.2

Catalyst preparation

Ni catalysts on SiO2 ber (Ni/SF) and SiO2 porous (Ni/SP)
supports with Ni loading of 5, 10 and 15 wt% were prepared
by the incipient wetness impregnation method. SP support was
a commercially obtained material (Fuji Silysia) with a diameter
range of 0.85–1.70 mm. SF support was synthesized by sol–gel
and electrospinning techniques. Nickel nitrate hexahydrate
(Ni(NO3)2$6H2O) purchased from Sigma-Aldrich as a Ni
precursor was dissolved in DI water, and then the solution was
impregnated into the SF and SP supports. The sample was dried
overnight at 110 °C, and then calcined at 500 °C for 2 hours. The
obtained Ni/SF catalysts were labeled as 5%Ni/SF, 10%Ni/SF
and 15%Ni/SF, and Ni/SP catalysts were marked as 5%Ni/SP,
10%Ni/SP and 15%Ni/SP.

2.4 Plasma-catalytic performance in dry reforming of
methane
The experimental set-up of the DBD plasma-catalytic system for
dry reforming of methane reaction is shown in Fig. 2. A planar
plate DBD plasma reactor is made of acrylic material with
a 21.5 cm length, a 9.5 cm width and a 3.85 cm thickness. The
two planar-shaped electrodes were fabricated from stainless
steel sheets with a 3 mm thickness, and a glass plate with
a 2 mm thickness serves as the dielectric material. The electrode
gap distance of 3 mm was xed. The simulated feed gas of
methane and carbon dioxide with a CH4/CO2 ratio of 70 : 30, was
specially manufactured for this research by Linde (Thailand)

Public Co., Ltd with a total feed ow rate of 35 cm3 min−1
controlled by a digital mass ow controller (New-Flow, TLFC).

frequency of 300 Hz to operate the discharge of plasma generation. The voltage and frequency were manipulated by a function generator (GW Instek, GFG-802OH). The power, current
and output voltage at the low-side voltage of power source were
measured and monitored by a power analyzer (Lutron,
DW6090). The gaseous products was analyzed by a gas chromatograph (Shimadzu, GC-14B) equipped with a Unibeads C

3 Results and discussion
3.1

Catalyst characterization

The textural properties of the fresh catalysts are presented in
Table 1. The specic surface area and pore volume of Ni/SF
catalysts were signicantly smaller than Ni/SP catalysts, as
clearly shown on SF without Ni loading. The SF support had the
lowest specic surface area of 2.892 m2 g−1 and pore volume of
0.011 cm3 g−1, indicating that the obtained SF was a non-porous
structure. The increase in Ni loading on SF support resulted in
an increase surface area and pore volume while decrease pore
radius. The highest surface area of 28.104 m2 g−1 and pore
volume of 0.068 cm3 g−1 were obtained at Ni loading of 15 wt%
on SF, owing to the characteristics of non-porous materials.
Thus, increasing the Ni loading enhances the specic surface
area of catalyst.6 However, increasing Ni loading on SP support
were signicantly decrease the textural property of Ni/SP,
certainly caused by the deposition of nickel oxide (NiO) within
SP pores. Furthermore, the N2 adsorption–desorption isotherm

Fig. 4 shows the XRD patterns of fresh Ni-based catalysts

Fig. 6 shows the XPS survey spectra of all fresh catalysts. For
the Ni/SF catalysts (5%, 10%, and 15%Ni/SF), the survey spectra
revealed the characteristic photoelectron peaks corresponding
to Si 2p (∼100–105 eV), C 1s (∼285 eV), O 13s (∼530 eV), and Ni
2p (∼855 eV), conrming NiO impregnated onto the SF support.
The presence of C 1s signal in the Ni/SF catalysts is attributed to
adventitious carbon contamination, which is commonly
observed in XPS measurements and serves as an internal
binding energy reference. Notably, the Ni 2p peaks in the Ni/SF
catalysts are clearly observable and exhibit increasing relative
intensity with higher Ni loading on the ber surface. The XPS
survey spectra of the Ni/SP catalysts (5%, 10%, and 15%Ni/SP)
show the prominent peaks for Si 2p and O 1s, but the Ni 2p
signals are absent. This observation implies that Ni species in
the Ni/SP catalysts are likely incorporated within the internal
pore channels of the porous SiO2 structure, so limiting the
detection of Ni by the surface-sensitive XPS technique, which
typically probes only the outermost 5–10 nm of the sample
surface.

of catalyst into the discharge gap results in an excellent

catalyst. However, the generation of microdischarge is signicantly reduced in the fully packed catalyst. Moreover, the electrical signals of plasma discharge suggest that partially catalyst

Characterization of spent catalyst

Fig. 11 showed the TGA proles of spent 10 wt% Ni/SF and
5 wt% Ni/SP catalysts under an applied voltage of 13 kV. From
room temperature to 100 °C, the weight of 5 wt% Ni/SP
catalyst decreased by almost 1% due to the weight loss of
moisture adsorbed on the porous catalyst surface. Meanwhile,
the 10%Ni/SF catalyst observed no weight loss in this region,
indicating that moisture adsorption did not occur on the nonporous SiO2 ber surface. In addition, heating both catalysts
to 1000 °C resulted in no signicant eﬀect on weight loss. It
can be inferred that the Ni catalyst on SiO2 support in this
study did not have coke deposited on the catalyst surface. In
general, the carbon deposits can be clearly observed on the
catalyst surface, which may contribute to the blocking of
active sites. The Raman spectra of the spent 15%Ni/SF catalyst
are presented in Fig. 12. Two prominent bands at approximately 1350 cm−1 (D band) and 1580 cm−1 (G band) are
detected at both measurement points of a and b. The D band
is associated with defective or disordered graphitic carbon,
while the G band corresponds to the stretching vibration of
sp2-hybridized carbon in ordered graphitic structures. The
relatively sharp and well-resolved D and G bands indicate the
formation of well-dened graphitic-type carbon on the Ni/SF
surface during reaction. Similarly, the XRD patterns of the
spent catalyst (Fig. 13) did not show a peak at 25°, which
referred the crystalline structure of carbon. However, the
intensity of the structural NiO crystal planes of the spent
catalyst decreased as compared to the fresh catalysts. It can be
expected that the spent catalyst has a reduced amount of Ni
particles or sintering of the catalyst. Moreover, the particle
size of NiO crystals were presented in Table 2. The NiO
particle size of the spent catalyst increased due to an aggregation of metal particle on the catalyst, which can cause to the
deactivation of catalyst. TEM-EDS analysis also revealed that
the C species were detected in the two selected regions on the

4 Conclusions
The comprehensive study on the planar plate DBD plasmaassisted dry reforming of methane over Ni/SiO2 ber catalyst
claried the factors aﬀecting overall system performance.
Notably, catalysts supported on SiO2 ber exhibited superior
catalytic performance compared to those on porous SiO2 pellets.
Furthermore, the packing conguration within the plasma
reactor was highly inuential, with a partial packing of the
catalyst in the plasma zone demonstrating a signicant
enhancement in system performance. This improvement is
attributed to superior discharge distribution and stable plasma
behavior. The applied electrical voltage strongly impacted both
system eﬃciency and plasma discharge stability. Post-reaction
characterization via TGA, Raman spectroscopy, XRD, and
TEM-EDS conrmed the negligibly small level of coking on the
catalyst surface and the structural integrity of the silica ber
support under plasma discharge conditions. The optimum
operational conditions for syngas production—achieving
maximum CH4 and CO2 conversions, syngas yield, H2/CO molar
ratio, and energy eﬃciency—were identied as 10% Ni/SF
catalyst partially packed within the plasma reactor zone at an
applied voltage of 15 kV.
