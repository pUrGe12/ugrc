ABSTRACT: To investigate the morphological occurrence of
carbon and ash in coal gasification coarse slag (CS) and their
impacts on resource utilization, this study focused on Jinhua CS
after grinding for 40 s. Samples with progressively decreasing
particle sizes, ranging from CS60 (coarse fraction) to CS200 (fine
fraction), were obtained through sieving. Combined with proximate
analysis, LOI, XRF, FTIR, XRD, SEM-EDS, Raman, BET, and TGDTG, the physicochemical properties of the samples were
systematically analyzed. Results indicated that the particle size
distribution was predominantly composed of intermediate fractions
(CS60−200), with a total yield exceeding 90%. As the particle size
decreased, the ash content reduced from 95.79% (CS60) to 82.72%
(CS200), while the fixed carbon content correspondingly increased from 2.29 to 14.71%, illustrating the distribution pattern of
″residual carbon enrichment in fine fractions and inorganic mineral dominance in coarse fractions.″ CS200 exhibited a greater
diversity of crystalline phases, with carbon and ash primarily existing as ″ash-encapsulated carbon,″ ″carbon-encapsulated ash,″ and
cross-linked structures. This fraction demonstrated a surface carbon content of 54.74%, predominantly comprising stable C−C/C−
H bonds (68.00%), along with a high specific surface area (104.35 m2/g), well-developed mesopores, abundant carbon defect sites,
and excellent combustion activity (DTGmax = −4.69%·min−1), making it suitable for preparing adsorbent materials or energy
recovery as fuel. Conversely, CS60 was characterized by discrete and ″carbon-encapsulated ash″ distributions of carbon and ash,
enriched with inorganic elements such as O, Si, and Al, exhibiting low combustion activity, and could be utilized for the preparation
of geopolymers, construction aggregates, etc.

1. INTRODUCTION
Coal gasification technology represents a pivotal method for
the clean and efficient utilization of coal, enabling the
conversion of coal to high-value-added products such as
syngas and fuel gas. This technology is extensively applied in
critical areas, including power generation and chemical
synthesis,1 providing essential support for the low-carbon
utilization of coal resources. However, the process generates a
significant amount of solid waste, specifically coal gasification
coarse slag (CS), characterized by its complex composition.2
CS primarily consists of incompletely gasified residual carbon
(a combustible phase) and aluminosilicate minerals (inorganic
ash).3,4 The direct stockpiling or inefficient disposal practices
of CS not only occupy valuable land resources but also pose
risks of soil and water pollution from heavy metal leaching and
the leaching of soluble salts. This issue has become a major
bottleneck constraining the large-scale deployment of coal
gasification technology.5 Consequently, enhancing the resource
utilization of coal gasification coarse slag has emerged as a

utilization.6 The structure and composition characteristics of

including coal type, gasifier type, reaction temperature, and
pressure.7,8 Among these factors, particle size, as a key

attracted extensive attention owing to its inherent correlation

demonstrated that coal particle size significantly affects the
mechanical properties, pore structure, and permeability of coalbased materials, modulating the utilization efficiency of the
materials. Chen et al.11 highlighted that the particle size of coal
gasification slag is a primary determinant of the compressive
strength of geopolymers, and the finer the slag particles, the
higher the compressive strength of the resulting geopolymers.
Miao et al.12 investigated coal gasification slag and found that
samples with different particle sizes exhibit distinct crystalline
structures, and the crystalline order of carbon exhibits a
specific trend with particle size variation. Pan et al.13 further
revealed that major elements such as Si, Al, and Ca are
preferentially enriched in coarse slag with larger particle sizes,
while elements such as Fe, S, and P are mostly distributed in
fine-grained components. Li et al. 14 investigated the
composition, structure characteristics, and thermochemical
properties of gasification slag with different particle sizes and
found that particle size sieving facilitates the preliminary
separation of ash and residual carbon. Moreover, as the particle
size of the gasification slag increases, the ash content rises
while the fixed carbon content declines correspondingly. Both
the overall weight loss rate and the maximum weight loss rate
decrease, indicating a marked improvement in thermal
stability. These studies collectively indicate that during the
gasification process, there are significant differences in the
heating and cracking modes, volatile removal paths, and charslag interactions of particles with different sizes, which
ultimately lead to the differentiation of the structure and
reaction characteristics of coarse slag with particle size.15,16
Although existing studies have preliminarily confirmed the
effect of particle size on the properties of coarse slag, there are
still notable limitations.17 First, systematic characterization is
lacking regarding the occurrence states of residual carbon and
ash in coarse slag (such as interface bonding state and mutual
encapsulation relationship) and their correlation with particle
size.18 Second, the regulatory mechanism of carbon−ash
interaction on the physicochemical properties of coarse slag
(such as combustion reactivity and adsorption purification
potential) remains poorly understood,19 which makes it
difficult to support the precision and differentiated utilization
of coarse slag.
Unlike previous studies that mainly focused on elemental
distribution or combustion characteristics, the present work
systematically elucidates the occurrence morphology of carbon
and ash and establishes the relationship between the
occurrence modes and resource utilization potential. On the
basis of this, this study focuses on coal gasification coarse slag
from a Jinhua furnace, following 40 s of grinding. Different
particle-size samples (CS60 to CS200) were obtained through
sieving. Combined with multidimensional characterization
methods, the influence patterns of particle size on the
occurrence forms, composition, structure, and physicochemical
properties of carbon and ash in coarse slag were systematically
investigated, aiming to provide a theoretical basis for the
graded and targeted utilization of coarse slag.

2.2. Analytical Methods
2.2.1. Proximate Analysis and Loss on Ignition. According to
GB/T212-2008, a sample mass of 1 g was used for proximate analysis
to determine the moisture, ash, and volatile matter in the residue
samples, and the fixed carbon content was calculated. Ultimate
analysis was performed using an Elementar Vario EL cube elemental
analyzer (Elementar UNICUBE, Germany) according to GB/T 4762008.
2.2.2. Composition and Crystal Structure Analysis. The
crystal structure of the samples was characterized by an X-ray
diffractometer (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance, Germany). The ash
composition of the sample was analyzed by an X-ray fluorescence
spectrometer (XRF, ZSX Primus III+, Japan), and the test method
and mode were the tablet method and oxide method, respectively.
Functional group structure types were analyzed by using infrared
spectroscopy (FTIR, Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS20). The degree of
graphitization of the carbon was characterized by a Raman
spectrometer (Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution, Japan).
2.2.3. Surface Morphology Analysis. Scanning electron
microscopy-energy spectrometry (SEM-EDS, ZEISSigma 300,
Germany) was used to analyze the surface morphology and elemental
distributions of the samples. The composition and chemical valence of
the surface elements were determined by X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (Thermo Scientific K-α). The pore structures of the
particles were analyzed by an automatic specific surface area and
porosity analyzer (BET, Micromeritics ASAP 2460).
2.2.4. Thermogravimetric Analysis. Pyrolysis and combustion
behaviors were evaluated using a SDT Q600 thermogravimetric
analyzer. Samples were heated from ambient temperature to 1000 °C
at 10 °C/min under a carrier gas flow of high-purity nitrogen or air.
The test sample mass was 8−10 mg, an alumina crucible was applied,

3.2.2. Micromorphology. During coal gasification, inorganic minerals in coal typically aggregate into spherical or
irregular blocky particles with smooth surfaces upon melting
and cooling, driven by turbulence and surface tension, whereas
incompletely gasified residual char predominantly exhibits a
dispersed flocculent morphology.25 SEM images of parent CS
and size-fractionated CS are presented in Figure 4. Three
distinct characteristic particle morphologies are observed:
dense/smooth irregular blocky/flaky particles (type A), porous
irregular flocculent particles (type B), and dense/smooth
spherical particles (type C). The surface micromorphologies of
the size-fractionated CS samples varied markedly, generally
exhibiting intermixed, adhered, or encapsulated states. The
CS60 fraction (Figure 4b1−3) predominantly comprised largesized type A and C particles, indicating high inorganic ash
content. Pores within type B particles were filled with spherical
type C particles of varying sizes, reflecting ash embedded in

residual char pores. With decreasing particle size, the size and
abundance of type A and C particles progressively diminished,
while the abundance of type B particles increased alongside
more developed pore structures. This trend reflects decreasing
inorganic mineral content and increasing residual char content,
aligning with the proximate analysis result of an increasing
fixed carbon content at smaller particle sizes. The CS100−200
(Figure 4e1−3) and CS200 fractions (Figure 4f1−3) primarily
consisted of type B and C particles. Type B particles formed a
spider-web-like porous skeleton with multiscale pores,
displaying morphologies such as flower-like, rod-like, and
needle-like structures. Numerous type C particles agglomerated into microspheres, interspersed, embedded, or attached
to the type B surface/skeleton, forming a sintered body with an
enamel luster and abundant microbeads. This reflects ashencapsulated and cross-linked residual carbon, further explaining the relatively lower fixed carbon content in the CS100−200
fraction.

3.2.4. Pore Structure. To further evaluate the adsorption
performance and potential application value of CS with
different particle sizes, the pore structure was systematically
characterized by nitrogen adsorption−desorption isotherms
(Figure 10) and pore size distribution curves (Figure 11a).
The specific surface area and pore structure parameters are
summarized in Table 7. As shown in Figure 10, the
adsorption−desorption isotherms of size-fractionated CS are
largely consistent in shape, exhibiting a characteristic inverted
S shape. According to the IUPAC classification, it can be
classified as a type IV isotherm with a typical type H2
hysteresis loop. This characteristic indicates that all samples
have a well-developed mesoporous structure, and the pore type
is mainly slit pores or ink-bottle pores.
Further analysis of Figure 11a reveals that pore sizes in all
samples are primarily concentrated within the 3−5 nm range,
confirming that the internal pore system is dominated by
mesopores. The complex and diverse pore architecture
potentially provides ample channels for mass transfer during
adsorption and catalytic processes. Combined with the
quantitative analysis results in Table 7 and Figure 11b,c,
except for the CS100−200 fraction, the SBET, Smic, and VT of
other fractions exhibited a significant increasing trend with the
decrease of particle size. Among these, CS200 exhibited
significantly higher values, all exceeding those of other samples.
This observation may be attributed to the higher specific
surface area of fine particles, which exhibit enhanced
adsorption of tar and hydrocarbon intermediates during
gasification. This facilitates the deposition of amorphous
carbon on their surfaces and within pores, thereby enriching
the pore structure. The observed reduction in pore parameters
for the CS100−200 fraction may relate to the cross-linked
distribution of residual carbon and ash within this fraction.
Notably, the micropore-to-total pore volume ratio (Vmic/VT)
remained stable within the 84−88% range across all fractions,
indicating that micropores are the dominant component of the
pore system. Mesopores (3−5 nm) serve as auxiliary structures
to improve pore interconnectivity, collectively forming a
hierarchical pore structure with synergistic micropore−
mesopore interactions. Such a structure significantly enhances
the material’s adsorption capacity and mass transfer
efficiency.30

3.2.3. Carbon Structure. Figure 9a presents the Raman
spectra of CS and its size-fractionated samples. The G band
corresponds to the stretching vibration mode of the ideal
graphite lattice, whereas the D band is generally associated
with structural disorder and defects in carbon materials,
including edge sites, lattice distortions, and amorphous carbon
domains.14,27 Except for the CS100−200 fraction, the Raman
peak intensity gradually increased with decreasing particle size,

3.3.2. Combustion Characteristics. Figure 14a shows the
TG curves of parent CS and size-fractionated CS under air,
with weight loss percentages of 16.38, 1.29, 7.15, 11.01, 8.64,

Figure 14b reveals that the primary weight loss occurs in the
range of 500−700 °C. During this stage, macromolecules
decompose intensely, the structure begins to break down,
volatile matter is released, and fixed carbon (coke) undergoes
violent high-temperature oxidation with O2 to generate CO2,
causing significant weight loss.
To compare combustion performance, ignition temperature
(Ti) and burnout temperature (Tf) were determined via the
tangent method (Figure 14c−h), and key combustion
characteristic indices are summarized in Table 8.8 Parent CS
exhibited the highest Ti (570 °C) and comprehensive
combustion index (S = 1.12 × 10−8 %2·min−2·°C−3), likely
due to its wide particle size distribution. Coarse particles
provide thermal stability, while fine particles offer high
reactivity, collectively creating a ″gradient combustion″ effect
that mitigates limitations of single-size fractions.
Ti increased gradually with decreasing particle size,
suggesting that ignition behavior was not solely controlled by
carbon content and specific surface area but was also affected
by mineral composition and carbon−mineral interactions. As
revealed by XRD analysis, CS200 exhibited more pronounced
diffraction peaks of quartz and calcite, indicating an increased

proportion of crystalline mineral phases in the fine fraction.
These mineral components may influence heat transfer and
oxygen accessibility during the initial oxidation stage, thereby
partially offsetting the positive effects of higher carbon content
and specific surface area on ignition.34 CS60 showed a higher
Tf (732 °C), lower DTGmax, and lower S value, indicating a
slow, prolonged combustion process. The S values of the
remaining four samples were comparable. Notably, CS200 had
the highest DTGmax (4.69%·min−1), likely due to its high
specific surface area, well-developed pores, and abundant
amorphous carbon, as confirmed by BET and Raman analyses.
These structural features facilitate oxygen diffusion and provide
more active sites for oxidation reactions, leading to accelerated
combustion after ignition. Although CS200 exhibited a slightly
higher ignition temperature than CS60, it demonstrated
significantly enhanced combustion kinetics once ignition
occurred. Therefore, the combustion behavior of sizefractionated CS was determined by the combined effects of
carbon content, pore structure, carbon defects, and mineral
composition rather than by a single factor.
