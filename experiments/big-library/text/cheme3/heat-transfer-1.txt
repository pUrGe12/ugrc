ABSTRACT: Thermal storage combustion technology is one of the
most efficient methods for treating volatile organic compounds
(VOCs), with the rotary regenerative thermal oxidizer (R-RTO)
representing the latest generation. However, research on R-RTOs
remains scarce and rarely considers the impact of rotation on the flow
dynamics and heat transfer. This study employed simulations using
the standard k-ε model with enhanced wall function, porous media
model, species transport model, Finite-Rate/Eddy-Dissipation
combustion model, and the Sliding Mesh method. After validating
the simulation accuracy, the velocity and temperature distributions
and heat transfer patterns within the channels of the regenerator were
investigated. The following conclusions were drawn: The gas flow
was uniform within the regenerative chamber but became nonuniform in the lower section of the chamber and within the oxidation chamber owing to the structural and flow directions. This
nonuniformity also affected the uniformity of the temperature distribution. A small portion of the gas within the combustion
chamber exhibited a short residence time. During rotary valve rotation, inlet gas flow short-circuiting occurred, causing severe
fluctuations in the inlet/outlet pressure difference and flow rate, and reducing the VOC removal efficiency. An analysis of the thermal
storage media channels revealed that the channel length and inlet air velocity significantly affected the heat transfer coefficient.
However, the impacts of these two parameters (length and velocity) and the switching time on thermal efficiency were minor.
Furthermore, the switching time had little impact on the heat transfer coefficient.

1. INTRODUCTION
Several approaches have been developed to address CO2 as a
greenhouse gas.1,2 Volatile organic compounds (VOCs), which
are gaseous pollutants with complex sources and compositions,
are often generated during treatment processes. Regenerative
thermal oxidizers (RTOs) have demonstrated VOC removal
efficiencies of up to 99% and are widely used in industrial
settings. Recent adsorption studies further indicate that typical
industrial VOCs such as esters, ketones and aromatic hydrocarbons may exhibit significant competitive adsorption behavior, and that adsorbent properties, VOC types and intercomponent competition should be considered when adsorption or
concentration units are coupled with downstream oxidation
processes. For high-flow rate and low-concentration VOC
exhausts, a recent full-scale study demonstrated that an
integrated adsorption−fluidized-bed desorption−RTO process
can achieve stable pollutant control while reducing operating
and capital costs, showing the engineering value of adsorption−
oxidation hybrid routes.3,4 Research has estimated that
approximately 17000 RTOs are currently in operation across
China.5
An RTO equipped with a porous regenerator as the heat
exchange medium exhibits significantly higher thermal efficiency
than a thermal oxidizer (TO). Moreover, based on life cycle

assessment methodology, RTOs have been identified as the
most economical and favorable option for emission reduction.6
In addition to VOC treatment, RTOs are also applied in
methane abatement, particularly in coal mining operations.7 For
ultralow-concentration coal mine gas, advanced CFD and
multiphysics coupled models have recently been used to
optimize square rotary RTO designs and to clarify the effects
of methane concentration, oxygen supply and chamber
geometry on oxidation stability.8 As VOCs serve as the primary
fuel source, RTOs can operate without auxiliary fuel when VOC
concentrations are sufficiently high. Under such conditions,
excess heat generated by the system can be recovered and
utilized in downstream processes, such as steam boilers, heat
transfer oil heat exchangers, or gas−gas heat exchangers.9,10
Thus, the capital investment in RTOs can be offset over time,
with some installations reportedly achieving full cost recovery
within three years.

RTOs can be classified into two structural types: cuboid and
cylindrical. The cuboid RTO includes an equal number of intake
and exhaust regenerative chambers, along with a purge chamber.
The most common configuration is the 3-bed (regenerative
chamber) RTO, although 5-bed RTOs are also in use.11,12
In particular, 3-bed RTOs have been studied extensively.
Research has focused on the effects of switching time,
regenerator height, velocity, temperature in the regenerative
chamber, and VOC concentrations on the flow and heat
transfer.13−15 By optimizing these parameters, the RTO can
maintain self-combustion and achieve high thermal efficiency
under low VOC concentrations.13
Each regenerative chamber alternates between intake, purge,
and exhaust periods. Residual VOCs in the regenerative
chamber during the intake period are flushed into the
combustion chamber using an air purge, which prevents their
release into the exhaust gas and improves the VOC removal
efficiency. However, heat absorption by the purge air reduces the
thermal efficiency of the RTO. Moreover, when the intake
volume is excessively large, the large cross-section of the
regenerative chamber limits the effectiveness of the purge effect.
To address these limitations, 5-bed RTOs were developed.
These RTOs feature two regenerative chambers for the intake
and exhaust, accounting for 4/5 of the total number of
chambers, significantly more than in the 3-bed RTO (2/3 of
the total chamber count). This configuration enhances the purge
effect and promotes more complete utilization of the
regenerative chambers.16 Thus, the minimum VOC concentration required for thermal self-sustainability is reduced, and
both VOC conversion and heat recovery ratios are
improved.12,17 However, the 5-bed RTO contains 15 valves�

compared to 9 valves in the 3-bed system�leading to increased
maintenance requirements and higher capital costs.
In addition, cylindrical RTOs (C-RTOs) with 12 regenerative
chambers have been developed; they feature a dead-zone
chamber without flow to isolate the intake and exhaust. Each
regenerative chamber is switched according to the periodicity of
the intake−purge−exhaust−dead-zone. The purge regenerative
chamber accounts for 1/12 of the regenerative chambers. Thus,
compared to cuboid RTOs, C-RTOs have a smaller purge
volume and lower heat loss. Moreover, C-RTOs have a smaller
footprint and require fewer regenerators.18 C-RTOs are
categorized into multivalve and rotary valve types, and the
multivalve type is an extension of the 3-chamber RTO with a
total of 36 valves. In contrast, the rotary valve type, called the
rotary RTO (R-RTO), employs a rotating rotor to control flow
paths, which minimizes mechanical failure risk. This structural
simplification is consistent with recent square rotary RTO
design studies, which emphasized higher regenerator utilization
and smaller installation footprint relative to conventional
circular layouts.8
Although C-RTOs account for more than 1/3 of the total
RTOs in China, few studies have explored this topic. Ren18
simulated an industrial multivalve cylindrical RTO and obtained
the flow and heat transfer regulations for the entire RTO. In
addition, Sun19 simulated the regenerator and combustion
chamber of a cylindrical RTO and studied the temperature
distribution and influence of temperature on the height of the
regenerator and the flow rate. Mao20 analyzed the effects of
temperature, toluene concentration, and rotation speed on an RRTO using a small experimental device.
In addition to the study of RTOs, the thermal efficiency of the
regenerator channel has also been a focus of research. The main

have primarily been investigated in relation to their structural
parameters, physical properties, and initial operating conditions,
with the proposed corresponding optimization strategies. In
parallel, recent numerical studies have shown that simplified
multistep reaction mechanisms can better represent the
oxidation of representative aromatic VOCs such as o-xylene in
RTOs, because overly simplified reaction assumptions may
overlook intermediate formation and outlet emission peaks
under low-concentration conditions.26 However, most existing
studies have focused on 3-bed RTOs and square-channel
regenerators. Although R-RTOs have been widely adopted in
industrial applications, research on this configuration remains
limited. Current simulation studies typically model only the
regenerative and combustion chambers, without analyzing the
complete R-RTO system or accounting for the effects of rotor
rotation. Moreover, studies on plate-type regenerators are also
scarce. Therefore, after validating the simulation model, this
study investigates the flow and temperature distributions within
the full R-RTO system and explores the heat transfer behavior
within the plate-type regenerator. To address these gaps, the
purpose of this study is to establish and validate a numerical
model for the complete R-RTO, clarify the effects of rotary-valve
switching on the internal flow field, temperature distribution,
residence-time behavior, and VOC removal stability, and
evaluate the heat-transfer performance of the plate-type
regenerator under typical operating parameters. The results
are expected to provide guidance for improving flow uniformity,
reducing short-circuiting during valve rotation, and optimizing
regenerator design for industrial R-RTO applications.

2. MODEL AND SIMULATION METHODS
2.1. Model Introduction
Figure 1 presents the structural diagram of an R-RTO. Gas containing
VOCs flowed from the rotary valve to the cone, regenerative chamber,
and combustion chamber. A burner was placed at the top of the
combustion chamber. If the temperature increase caused by the
combustion heat of the VOCs did not reach the set temperature, the
burner ignited. The heated flue gas flows in opposite directions. The
rotary valve is driven by a reducer. The central column is hollow with no
gas flow.
Figure 2 illustrates the configuration of the rotary valve and stator.
The gas pressure at the intake inlet is higher than that at the exhaust
outlet. The purge inlet is located at the bottom center, directing purge
air upward toward the apex of the cone. The purge flow then exits the
designated purge channel and enters the corresponding regenerator
chamber. The dead-zone regenerative chamber remains sealed, with no
airflow, and serves to separate the intake and exhaust regions in
conjunction with the purge flow.
Figure 3 shows the R-RTO model. The regenerative and combustion
chambers have a diameter of 4.2 m. As shown, the regenerative
chamber, combustion chamber, and cone are each equipped with a 250
mm insulation layer. The regenerator in the regenerative chamber has a
height of 1.2 m, while the combustion chamber is 2 m high.

The standard k-ε model with enhanced wall function was

with higher-order turbulence models, the standard k-ε model

for full-cycle engineering simulations. In conventional RTO
numerical simulations, the standard k-ε model is commonly
adopted. In addition, the experimental validation results
presented in Section 2.3 also indicate that the selected model
can accurately reproduce the main thermal-flow behavior of the
R-RTO. Therefore, the adoption of the standard k-ε model is
considered reasonable in this study. The outer wall surface
temperature was approximately 20 K higher than the ambient
temperature, resulting in minimal heat loss. Therefore, the outer
wall was modeled as adiabatic.

the complexity of the reaction mechanism while retaining the

2.3. Model Validation against Industrial Measurements
The numerical model was validated using on-site temperature
measurements from the full-scale R-RTO installed in a production
line. The temperature in one stable operating cycle was compared with
the experimental measurements at thermocouples A, B, and C and at
the outlet. Figure 7a shows the measured temperature variation in one
cycle, and Figure 7b gives the relative deviation between simulation and
experiment.

A component transport model was used to define and control
the composition of the exhaust gas at the inlet. The Finite-Rate/
Eddy-Dissipation model was used to simulate the oxidation
reaction.
The most commonly used radiation calculation models are P-1,
Rosseland, and DO. The radiation model can be selected by
calculating the optical thickness aL (a is the absorption
coefficient of the material, and L is the length of the model).
The P-1 and Rosseland models are appropriate when aL is
significantly greater than 1. However, the optical thickness of
this model is much less than 1, and applying either of the first
two models would introduce significant errors. In contrast, the
discrete ordinate (DO) radiation model solves the radiative
transport equation using several solid domain coordinates,
where the solid angle in the coordinates corresponds to the fixed
direction angle. This approach can be used to calculate the
translucent model and solid surface radiation. It is suitable for all
optical thicknesses and yields the highest calculation accuracy
among all three models.14,30 Therefore, the DO model was
selected to control the radiation and heat transfer of polar gases
such as CO2 and H2O.
The rotating simulation of the rotary valve adopted dynamic
mesh technology. At present, the technology for simulating
partial relative motion mainly includes the deformation mesh
(DM), Overset Mesh (OM), Multiple Reference Frames
(MRF), and Sliding Mesh (SM). The DM is computationally
intensive and is mostly used to solve two-dimensional problems.
The OM is primarily used to resolve the movement of rigid
bodies. The MRF model can only be used for steady-state
numerical calculations and is suitable for fluid subdomains with
uniform rotation or translation. SM uses an interface to segment
the dynamic and static watersheds to accurately describe the
non-normal phase interaction flow field and simultaneously
process multiple rotating subdomains.20,31,32 SM is especially
suited for simulating non-normal interactions caused by the
relative motion of static and rotating regions. This study adopted
the SM approach to simulate rotary valve rotation, using a ZoneMotion User-Defined Function to control rotary valve rotation.
The inlet gas and the purge air were both set as velocity inlet, with
velocities of 7.9 and 7.40 m/s, temperatures of 540 and 340 K, and
relative pressures of 2700 and 5000 Pa, respectively. The exhaust outlet
was modeled as a pressure outlet with a relative pressure of 85 Pa.
For the simulation of the plate-type regenerator, a channel with a
large height-to-length ratio was selected, as shown in Figure 6. Four
bulges were considered for the regenerator channel, and the wall
thicknesses on both sides were halved.

3.2.3. Parameters of Heat Exchange. The height of the
regenerator, commutation time, and inlet velocity shown in
Figure 14 are commonly used parameters in engineering. As
observed from the figure, when the regenerator height increased
from 0.9 to 2.1 m, the heat transfer coefficient during the intake
stage decreased significantly from approximately 33.2 W/(m2·
K) to 24.1 W/(m2·K), corresponding to a decrease of
approximately 27.4%. During the exhaust stage, the heat transfer
coefficient decreased from approximately 29.8 W/(m2·K) to
21.0 W/(m2·K), corresponding to a decrease of approximately
29.5%. However, the average heat transfer rate changed only
slightly. Specifically, it varied from approximately 51.6 to 52.0 J/
s during the intake stage, and from approximately 57.3 to 59.2 J/
s during the exhaust stage. The corresponding channel thermal
efficiency decreased only slightly from approximately 99.8% to
98.9%. These results indicate that, within the investigated range,
simply increasing the regenerator height does not significantly
improve the thermal efficiency; instead, it reduces the heat
transfer coefficient. This result is consistent with the findings
reported by Hao.33

4. CONCLUSION
The R-RTO was developed based on the RTO used in a colorcoating line. Following verification with experimental results, the
velocity and temperature distributions within the R-RTO were
analyzed. The heat transfer behavior within the regenerator
channels was also investigated. The following conclusions are
drawn:
The velocity distribution in the lower region of the RRTO�comprising the stator, rotary valve, and cone�
was highly nonuniform. In contrast, both the velocity and
temperature were uniform across the cross-section of the
regenerative chamber. The velocity in the regenerative
chamber increased from bottom to top owing to
temperature-induced density variations. Within the
combustion chamber, the velocity and temperature
exhibited steep gradients as a result of flow redirection.
The velocity and temperature fields in both the
regenerative and combustion chambers varied periodically as the rotary valve switched positions.
Although the average residence time in the combustion
chamber was relatively long, 7.57% of the gas experienced
residence times shorter than 1 s due to the influence of
nonuniform velocity distribution.
The rotary valve remained stationary for 17 s before
rotating for 2.5 s. During the stationary phase, the
differences between inlet and outlet pressure, flow rate,
and VOC removal efficiency varied minimally. However,
during valve rotation, both the pressure difference and
flow rate fluctuated significantly, and the VOC removal
efficiency declined, negatively affecting the operational
stability of the R-RTO.
The temperature distribution within the plate-type
regenerator remained uniform. For common R-RTO
parameters-namely, regenerator height, switching time,
and inlet velocity-values were selected within typical
engineering ranges. The analysis indicated that these
three parameters had limited impact on overall thermal
efficiency, but their effects on heat transfer capacity were
different. Among them, inlet velocity had the greatest
influence on heat transfer and should be optimized
together with pressure drop and fan power. Increasing
regenerator height reduced the heat transfer coefficient
and is therefore not recommended as the primary method
for improving performance. Switching time had little
influence on the heat transfer coefficient and heat transfer
rate, although moderate extension can slightly improve
thermal efficiency. For the present plate-type regenerator,
a moderate height of about 1.2−1.5 m and an inlet
velocity near 1.5 m/s are suggested as a practical
compromise.
