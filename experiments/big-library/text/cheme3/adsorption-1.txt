Effects of Coal Rank on the Adsorption Characteristics and
Mechanism of Hydrogen Sulfide by Coal Molecules

Cite This: ACS Omega 2026, 11, 62756−62776

ABSTRACT: This study systematically investigates the molecular
structural characteristics of different rank coals (anthracite,
bituminous, lignite) and their adsorption properties and mechanisms toward hydrogen sulfide (H2S). By combining X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and solid-state 13C NMR
spectroscopy, it was found that aromatic carbon content increases
remarkably from 55.91 to 91.75% with coal rank increasing,
whereas aliphatic carbon and oxygen-bearing functional groups
decrease accordingly. Macromolecular model representatives
corresponding to each coal rank were constructed based on
experimental data, and molecular simulation and adsorption
analysis were performed using Materials Studio software. Pore
structures vary markedly with coal rank: lignite shows the greatest
total pore volume but limited connectivity, whereas anthracite develops a well-connected micropore network. H2S adsorption
follows the Langmuir−Freundlich model, with capacities decreasing in the order: anthracite > bituminous > lignite. Although the
adsorption capacity decreases with increasing temperature, the effect of temperature on the adsorption capacity of different coal
grades shows a non monotonic variation. Oxygen-bearing functional groups�particularly carboxyl, hydroxyl, and carbonyl�
dominate H2S adsorption via hydrogen bonding, whereas π-electrons of aromatic rings contribute weakly. Molecular dynamics
simulations show that the diffusion coefficient of H2S increases with temperature and is highest in anthracite. This study elucidates
the influence mechanism of coal rank structure on H2S adsorption behavior at the molecular level, providing a theoretical basis for
the treatment of H2S in coal seams.

1. INTRODUCTION
Coal is an important fossil energy and chemical raw material.
Hydrogen sulfide gas is a gas associated with coal, which poses a
serious threat to safety production, environmental protection,
and equipment corrosion during coal mining and utilization.1−3
H2S is not only highly toxic and corrosive but also one of the key
precursors contributing to acid rain. Therefore, in-depth
investigation into the adsorption characteristics and mechanisms of coal toward H2S holds significant theoretical and
practical importance for developing efficient H2S control
technologies for coal mines, predicting the occurrence and
migration of H2S in coal seam, and creating highly effective
materials for hydrogen sulfide removal.4
Coal is an organic macromolecular polymer with a highly
complex molecular structure and heterogeneity. Its physicochemical structure (such as surface functional groups, pore
characteristics, and macromolecular configuration) significantly
affects its adsorption performance towards gas molecules. Coal
rank is a key determinant of fundamental coal properties, with
systematic differences existing among different ranks (e.g.,
lignite, bituminous, anthracite) in elemental composition,
molecular structural order, and pore development.5−7

Currently, researchers have been carried out on the
adsorption behavior of H2S. Gao et al.8 found that when H2S
coexists with other gases, the adsorption capacities of CH4, N2
and CO2 decrease by 48−60%, 81−91% and 51−66%,
respectively. Li et al.9 found that under saturation adsorption
simulation conditions, the saturation adsorption of CH4 in coals
of different ranks gradually decreases with increasing H2S
content, indicating that the presence of H2S inhibits the
adsorption of CH4 by coal macromolecules. Jia and Xing10
studied the adsorption mechanism of H2S on different
functional groups and found that, at the initial stage of
adsorption, these functional groups attract H2S molecules
through electrostatic and van der Waals interactions, drawing
the H2S molecules toward the functional sites. Gao et al.11

high−specific surface area activated carbon, thereby enhancing
H2S adsorption; the adsorption isotherm follows a two-site
Langmuir model, and the maximum adsorption capacity reaches
8.5 mg/g.
In recent years, molecular simulation techniques have been
widely adopted to investigate the structure−adsorption relationships of coal. Several reviews have systematically summarized
the application of molecular mechanics, molecular dynamics,
and quantum chemistry in coal macromolecular modeling and
gas adsorption studies.18,19 For coal macromolecular model
construction, extensive efforts have been made using elemental
analysis combined with 13C NMR, XPS, and FTIR to build and
validate three-dimensional molecular models for different coal
ranks.20−23 For adsorption simulation, GCMC and MD
methods have been successfully applied to study the competitive
adsorption behaviors of CH4, CO2, and N2 in coal matrices,24−26
as well as the influence of functional groups, pore size, water
content, and temperature on gas adsorption capacity and
diffusion dynamics.27−30 Meanwhile, the pore structure
evolution during coalification and its effect on gas adsorption
has also been investigated by combining ReaxFF simulations
with experimental characterization.31,32 These studies collectively provide a solid methodological foundation and validation
framework for the present work on H2S adsorption across
different coal ranks.
Although some researchers have investigated the adsorption
characteristics of H2S gas, there is still a lack of systematic and indepth research on the adsorption patterns of H2S on coals of
different ranks, especially regarding the intrinsic correlation
between its adsorption mechanism and the multi-scale structural
characteristics of coal bodies, ranging from molecular-level
surface chemistry to nanoscale pore structure.
In this study, three representative coal samples from different
rank coals (high-rank anthracite, medium-rank bituminous, and
low-rank lignite) are selected as research subjects. It
comprehensively analyzed the surface element occurrence states
(C, O, N, S), oxygen/nitrogen/sulfur-containing functional

group distribution, carbon skeleton structure (aromatic carbon/

structure characteristics of coals with different ranks by

correlation between coal rank, structure, and adsorption
performance.
A combined approach of experimental characterization and
molecular simulation is employed in this study to investigate the
adsorption characteristics and mechanisms of H2S gas on
different rank coals across macro-to-micro scales. This provides
guidance for understanding the adsorption characteristics and
migration patterns of H2S in coal, as well as for the development
of coal-based H2S adsorption materials.

2. MATERIAL PREPARATION
In this study, coal samples were selected from high, medium, and
low ranks for experimental research. Samples of high-rank
(anthracite), medium-rank (bituminous), and low-rank (lignite)
coal were collected from Jincheng City and Changzhi City in
Shanxi Province, and Hohhot City in Inner Mongolia, and they
were designated as SJ, SZ, and NH, respectively. 500 g portions
of each coal sample were ground into a particle size of 200−300
mesh. All coal powders were dried at 60 °C for 10 h in a vacuum
drying oven and subsequently stored in sealed containers.

micropore volume and specific surface area, the more surface
adsorption sites can be provided, which is more conducive to gas
adsorption. However, in addition to the microporous structure,
the factors influencing the adsorption capacity of coal also
include the moisture, ash content and functional group
composition of the coal itself.53 This also explains why medium
and high-rank coal usually exhibits a stronger coalbed methane
adsorption capacity than low-rank coal.

increase in equilibrium pressure promotes the contact between
adsorbate molecules and adsorbent molecules, but this
promotion has a critical point. When the pressure is below
this critical point, the adsorption capacity increases rapidly as the
equilibrium pressure increases. When it exceeds the critical
pressure, the rate of increase in adsorption capacity slows down
as the equilibrium pressure increases. The adsorption of H2S gas
by different rank coals can be divided into three stages: rapid
adsorption stage, slow adsorption stage, and stable adsorption
stage. This is mainly due to the heterogeneity of the pore
structure distribution in coal bodies, which is manifested as
different adsorption potentials for different nanopore sizes.
At the same temperature, taking 273.15 K as an example, the
0−100 kPa range represents the rapid adsorption phase, where
the adsorption capacity of coal ranks from high to low increasing
rapidly to 40.27, 38.78, and 21.29 mL/g, respectively. The 100−
600 kPa range represents the slow adsorption phase, where the
adsorption capacity of coal ranks from high to low increasing
slowly to 48.00, 44.31, and 24.99 mL/g, respectively. The 600−
1000 kPa range constitutes the stable adsorption phase, where
the adsorption capacity of coal ranks from high to low is
minimal, with only medium and high rank coals showing a slow
increase, while the increase in adsorption capacity of low rank
coal is almost negligible. This occurs because during the lowpressure adsorption phase, H2S exhibits a high adsorption rate,
with H2S molecules preferentially occupying high-energy
adsorption sites. As adsorption progresses, these high-energy
sites become fully occupied, and H2S molecules gradually shift
to adsorption sites with lower adsorption potentials. On the
other hand, the slowdown in adsorption rate during the later
stages is primarily due to repulsive forces between the H2S
molecules adsorbed earlier and those adsorbed later. This
repulsion mainly originates from electrostatic mutual repulsion.56

Around oxygen atoms with oxygen-containing functional

electrostatic potential distribution shows distinct negative
potential regions. This is because oxygen atoms have a high
electronegativity, attracting electron cloud density and forming
negative potential centers. These negative potential regions can
undergo electrostatic attraction with the positive potential
regions in H2S molecules (such as hydrogen atoms), promoting
adsorption. However, for the carbonyl group, its positively
charged hydrogen atom may form a stronger hydrogen bond
with the negatively charged sulfur atom. Negative potential
regions also exist around nitrogen-containing functional groups
such as pyrrole and pyridine, but their intensity is usually weaker
than that of oxygen-containing functional groups. The electronegativity of nitrogen atoms is lower than that of oxygen atoms.
For hydrogen atoms connected to pyrrole and nitrogen atoms,
they may also form stronger hydrogen bonds with sulfur atoms.
The electrostatic potential distribution around thiophene sulfur
is rather complex. Sulfur atoms have a certain electronegativity,
but it is lower than that of oxygen and nitrogen atoms. The π
electron cloud on the thiophene ring can cause local electrostatic
potential changes. The interaction with H2S molecules is rather
complex. Therefore, the H2S molecules adsorbed near the
thiophene ring should have a configuration where the sulfur
atom is facing down directly towards the center of the thiophene
ring. The electrostatic potential distribution around aliphatic
carbon functional groups, such as methyl and oxymethyl is
relatively uniform, and the negative potential region is not
obvious. The oxygen atoms in oxymethyl can form a certain
negative potential region, but the intensity is relatively weak.
The electrostatic potential distribution around the benzene ring
shows a certain symmetry. The π electron cloud can cause slight
changes in the local electrostatic potential, but the overall
electrostatic potential distribution is relatively uniform. Therefore, the H2S molecules adsorbed near the benzene ring should
have a configuration with two hydrogen atoms facing down.

4. CONCLUSION
1 The molecular structural changes driven by coal rank
evolution are the fundamental internal factors for the
differences in adsorption diffusion behavior. With coal
rank increases, its macromolecular structure undergoes
systematic evolution: aromatic carbon content rises
significantly from 55.91 to 91.75%, while aliphatic carbon
content decreases sharply from 36.70 to 6.72%, and
oxygen-containing functional groups decrease from 33.41
to 10.34%. This leads to a structural transformation of the
coal matrix from predominantly aliphatic chains and polar
functional groups in low-rank coal to highly condensed
aromatic lamellae in high-rank coal.
2 The coal rank dependence of adsorption capacity is
synergistically regulated by the dual mechanisms of
chemical activity and pore volume to surface area ratio.
The differences in pore structure and specific surface area
among macromolecular models of different rank coals
directly reflect the evolution of chemical structure during
coalification. Lignite exhibits the largest total micropore
volume (TPV = 8226.50 Å3) and specific surface area
(8821.12 Å2) due to its loose macromolecular structure,
anthracite develops a well-connected micropore network
(APV/TPV = 47.03%) during its highly aromatic
carbonization, bituminous’s pore structure has the lowest
TPV (5425.37 Å3).
3 Different coal ranks fundamentally reflect the synergistic
evolution of “chemical composition-pore structureadsorption behavior”. The adsorption isotherms of H2S
on macromolecular models of different rank coals
conform to the Langmuir−Freundlich model. The
adsorption process exhibits clear pressure-dependent
stages: the rapid adsorption stage (0−100 kPa), the
slow adsorption stage (100−600 kPa) and the stable
adsorption stage (600−1000 kPa). The H2S adsorption
capacity follows the rule: anthracite > bituminous > lignite
across the entire pressure range. and the adsorption
capacity variation of different rank coals for H2S
molecules exhibited a U-shaped curve, indicating that
temperature exerted a greater influence on the adsorption
capacity change of medium-rank coal compared to highrank and low-rank coals.
4 Functional group adsorption selectivity exhibits clear
chemical polarity laws. The priority order of H2S
adsorption by functional groups is carboxyl, hydroxyl,
carbonyl and other oxygen-containing functional groups,
followed by nitrogen-containing heterocyclic pyrrole,
sulfur-containing heterocyclic thiophene, and aliphatic
carbon functional groups such as oxymethyl and methyl,
and finally the benzene ring. The O atom of oxygencontaining functional groups can form hydrogen bonds
with the hydrogen atom of H2S molecules, so it has a
strong adsorption force for H2S molecules, while
functional groups such as pyrrole, pyridine, thiophene,
oxymethyl, methyl, and benzene ring have a smaller
adsorption force for H2S molecules.
5 Temperature exerts a thermodynamic inhibition kinetic
promotion regulatory effect on adsorption diffusion
behavior. As temperature increases from 273.15 to

molecules adsorbed in coal macromolecules of different rank
coals increase significantly. This is because as the temperature
rises, more energy is injected into the system, and the thermal
energy of the system is converted into the kinetic energy of H2S
molecules, accelerating the molecular movement. It is easier for
gas molecules to overcome the energy barrier during diffusion.
Moreover, the thermal vibration of the aliphatic side chains and
functional groups in the coal macromolecular framework
intensifies when the temperature rises. This micro-scale
movement may lead to the opening of instantaneous diffusion
channels or fluctuations in local pore diameters, indirectly
providing additional migration paths for H2S molecules and
reducing the overall diffusion resistance.65 As mentioned in
Section 3.2.2, this is complementary to the conclusion that the
adsorption capacity decreases with the increase of temperature,
jointly forming an “adsorption-diffusion” coupling relationship:
temperature has opposite effects on thermodynamic equilibrium
(adsorption) and kinetic processes (diffusion). At the same
temperature, the diffusion coefficient of H2S shows a significant
coal rank dependence. This key rule is the result of the combined
effect of the physical properties of the pore structure and the
surface chemical properties of coal with the evolution of the coal
rank.
As mentioned in Section 3.2.1, although lignite (NH) has the
largest absolute values of total pore volume (TPV) and
accessible pore volume (APV), its APV/TPV ratio (57.31%)
reveals the high complexity and curvature of its pore structure. A
large number of pores may be “dead ends” or have narrow
throats, which greatly restrict the effective migration paths of
H2S molecules. Although the absolute APV value (2656.10 Å3)
of anthracite (SJ) is lower than that of lignite, its micro-porous
network formed by highly condensed aromatic layers has better
connectivity and a more regular structure. The geometric
resistance encountered by gas diffusion in such a relatively
balanced and open microporous system is much less than that in
the pore network of lignite. The TPV and APV of bituminous
(SZ) are both the lowest. Its structure is the densest and the
diffusion space is limited, so its diffusion coefficient lies between
the two.
As shown in the Radial Distribution Function (RDF) analysis
in Section 3.2.3, lignite is rich in a large number of strongly polar
oxygen-containing functional groups (such as −COOH, −OH,
C�O). There are strong hydrogen bonds and dipole−dipole
interactions between these functional groups and polar H2S
molecules (manifested as high RDF peaks). This strong
adsorption effect significantly prolongs the “residence time” of
H2S molecules on the surface of the lignite matrix. The diffusion
process is essentially a dynamic jump of molecules between
