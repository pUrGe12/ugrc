Catalyst of a Mytilus galloprovincialis shell
substrate: optimization of alcoholysis conditions
for the catalytic depolymerization of waste
polyester ﬁlms
Xingzhu Han, Guoliang Shen,
and Tiejun Xu

* Linlin Zhao, Ruiyang Wen, Haichen Wang, Sijin Jiang

Catalysts fabricated from waste Mytilus galloprovincialis shells feature high resource utilization,
environmental friendliness and low cost. In this work, a powdered Mytilus galloprovincialis shell substrate
was used as a catalyst for the glycolysis of a de-aluminized PET ﬁlm to synthesize bis(2-hydroxyethyl)
terephthalate (BHET), and favorable experimental results were obtained. Taking the BHET yield as the
response value, a Box–Behnken response surface design was carried out on the basis of single-factor
experiments to explore the inﬂuencing factors of the BHET yield as well as their interaction eﬀects, and
a quadratic regression model was established. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) veriﬁed that the established
model was highly signiﬁcant. The optimized glycolysis conditions for PET ﬁlm depolymerization with
ethylene glycol were determined as follows: a catalyst loading of 0.49%, reaction temperature of 198 °C,
reaction duration of 1.5 h, and ethylene glycol dosage of 15.56 g; under these conditions, the actual

Introduction

An aluminum-coated polyester (PET) lm is a vital material
widely applied in the agriculture and decoration industries. It
exhibits metallic luster and integrates the advantages of plastics
and metals, reecting 70–80% of the incident light and delivering outstanding light reectivity. At present, pesticides are
extensively applied to control pests and diseases aﬀecting fruit
trees and fruits, although pesticide application poses severe
threats to human health and the environment. Fruit bagging
technology has been widely adopted to mitigate these adverse
impacts. As an auxiliary measure for fruit bagging, fruit growers
lay aluminum-coated PET lms on the orchard ground during
fruit ripening, which accelerates fruit pigmentation and raises
the sugar content, thus improving the economic benets of
fruits. Such agricultural practices greatly boost the consumption of aluminum-coated PET lms. However, PET polymer
possesses stable chemical structures and is hardly biodegradable. Discarded PET lms accumulate in the natural environment and aggravate white pollution. Nevertheless, both the
aluminum layer and PET substrate in waste aluminized PET

aluminum-coated PET lms is critically required to mitigate
agricultural plastic pollution and realize resource recovery of

Existing PET recycling technologies fall into two categories:
physical recycling and chemical recycling. Physical recycling
struggles to produce pure polyester pellets, and even pelletization oen fails to meet industrial standards. In contrast,
chemical recycling oﬀers exible application pathways and
enables the production of high-value-added chemical products. The main chemical recycling strategies include glycolysis,1,2 hydrolysis3–5 and methanolysis.6–8 Glycolysis is regarded
as the most promising recycling route,9 as it operates under
relatively mild conditions by avoiding extremely high temperatures and pressures, cutting energy consumption and
production costs.10 However, glycolysis proceeds at an
extremely low reaction rate without catalysts.11,12 Under noncatalytic conditions, the reaction rate and eﬃciency of PET
glycolysis remain far below industrial requirements. Hence,
high-eﬃciency catalysts are essential to achieve the eﬀective
depolymerization of PET using ethylene glycol. Catalytic
glycolysis enables waste PET to be selectively depolymerized
into high-purity monomers and intermediates.13 These high-

purity products can be further utilized for fabricating diverse

Bis(hydroxyethyl) terephthalate (BHET) serves as a critical
monomer intermediate with considerable economic value in
the PET glycolysis recycling system. Commercial-grade virgin
PET is synthesized via the polymerization of petroleum-derived
terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol, which renders its
production heavily reliant on fossil resources. In contrast, BHET
production through ethylene-glycol-mediated depolymerization
of waste PET can markedly cut petrochemical feedstock
consumption and lower fossil-fuel dependence. Unlike downgraded regenerated PET products from physical recycling,
puried BHET monomers can be repolymerized to yield polyester materials whose properties are comparable to those of
virgin PET. This overcomes the inherent quality limitations of
physical recycling and enables genuine closed-loop chemical
recycling of PET. Furthermore, BHET monomers exhibit broad
application prospects. Beyond direct reproduction of PET resin,
they can be applied to synthesize diverse high-value-added
polymers including polyurethane, unsaturated polyester and
alkyd resins, which nd extensive use in packaging, textile
bers, coatings and other industrial sectors. Against the global
policy backdrop of advancing plastic circular-economy strategies, the PET chemical-recycling industry keeps expanding,
accompanied by a growing market demand for high-purity
BHET. Therefore, the selective conversion of waste PET into
BHET holds great industrial-scale implementation potential
and economic signicance.
Literature reports indicate that catalysts for PET glycolysis
mainly consist of metal salts, metal oxides and ionic liquids.
Early polyester degradation catalysts were metal salts, including
zinc acetate, zinc chloride,14 sodium carbonate,15 sodium
bicarbonate,16 and triethylamine.17 Zinc acetate was the rst
metal acetate catalyst applied in this eld. Later, various metal
oxides and modied metal oxides were introduced into PET
depolymerization research, such as ZnO,18 g-Fe2O3,19 Fe3O4,20
mixed metal oxide spinels containing Mn, Co, Zn and other
metallic elements,21 and SO42−/ZnO-TiO2 composite oxides.22
Bifunctional composite catalysts have also been developed,
such as CoFe2O4@ZIF-8/ZIF-67 (ref. 23) and Mg-Al-O@Fe3O4
microspherical catalysts.24 Most high-performance metal oxide
and metal salt catalysts contain transition metals, which cause
environmental pollution and equipment corrosion during their
use. In addition, ionic liquid catalysts developed in recent years
suﬀer from complicated synthesis processes and high costs; at
elevated temperatures, they decompose into nitrogen oxides
and sulfur oxides, which endanger human health and ecosystems.25 Hence, it is critical to develop low-cost, readily available
and eco-friendly catalysts.
In this study, waste Mytilus galloprovincialis shells were
recycled to prepare biomass catalysts. Aer a series of
pretreatment procedures, waste mussel shells were ground into
a ne bio-based powder featuring low cost and non-toxicity.
This catalyst was applied to catalyze the glycolysis of dealuminized waste PET lms (transparent PET lms obtained
by chemical aluminum stripping of metallized PET lms) from

3 Preparation and characterization of
mussel shell-based catalysts
3.1

Preparation of the Mytilus edulis shell-based catalyst

First, waste Mytilus galloprovincialis shells were rinsed with clean
water to remove surface impurities and residual attachments.
Next, the shells were repeatedly washed with 5% sodium
hydroxide aqueous solution and distilled water to eliminate
inorganic salts and soluble impurities that could interfere with
the experimental results. Subsequently, the fully cleaned mussel
shells were placed in an oven and dried at 100 °C for 4 h. The
dried mussel shell residues were transferred to a clean, dry
crucible and then heated in a muﬄe furnace under an air
atmosphere at a set temperature of 800 °C for 8 h of calcination.
Calcination removed the organic matter the inside mussel shells
and triggered crystalline phase transformation of the inorganic
components. Aer calcination, the solids were ground and
sieved to obtain the Mytilus galloprovincialis shell-based catalyst
HH-800 (Fig. 1).

4.1

Experimental methods

3 g of the dried de-aluminized PET lm was weighed and added
to a three-necked ask. A certain amount of ethylene glycol and
the HH-800 catalyst were sequentially loaded into the ask,
followed by continuous stirring and a constant-temperature
depolymerization reaction. Aer the reaction was complete,
the mixture was naturally cooled to 100 °C and poured into
a beaker prelled with deionized water. The mixture was rst
held at 90 °C for vacuum ltration to separate the undepolymerized PET residues. The ltrate was then cooled to 65 °C and
ltered under vacuum to remove oligomer byproducts. Aerwards, the ltrate was further cooled to 45 °C for a second
vacuum ltration to eliminate residual oligomers. The nal
ltrate was cooled to 5 °C and crystallized for 12 h. The
precipitated crystals were collected by ltration and dried in
a vacuum oven at 78 °C under 0.05 MPa vacuum for 10 h. The

interactions are displayed in Fig. 12. Fig. 12(a) describes the
interactive eﬀect between the catalyst dosage (A) and reaction
time (B), where the BHET yield peaks at a catalyst loading of
roughly 0.5% and a reaction time of 1.5 h. Fig. 12(b) reects the
combined inuence of the catalyst dosage (A) and reaction
temperature (C); at a xed temperature, the BHET yield rst

reveals the interaction between the catalyst dosage (A) and
ethylene glycol dosage (D). With constant catalyst loading,
additional ethylene glycol eﬀectively boosts the BHET yield, yet
the optimal solvent dosage remains below 16 g. Fig. 12(d)
illustrates how the reaction time (B) interacts with the reaction
temperature (C), showing that a higher temperature greatly

the reaction temperature (C) and ethylene glycol dosage (D), and
the maximum BHET yield is obtained at approximately 198 °C
and an ethylene glycol dosage of 16 g.

The quadratic regression model established using the Box–
Behnken response surface methodology in this study presents

4.5

Mechanism of catalytic disaggregation

Based on the experimental characterization and theoretical
analysis, the catalytic glycolysis mechanism of PET mediated by
the HH-800 catalyst is proposed in Fig. 14. First, Ca2+ active sites
on the catalyst surface form coordination bonds with the
carbonyl oxygen atoms of the PET macromolecules. This coordination weakens the C]O double bonds, lowers the activation
energy of ester cleavage and activates the PET polymer chains.
Such a coordination-activation eﬀect of metal ions on ester
carbonyl groups has been documented in previous studies
concerning calcium- and zinc-based solid-base catalysts for
polyester glycolysis. The unoccupied outer-shell orbitals of Ca2+
accept the lone-pair electrons from carbonyl oxygen to form
coordinate bonds, which induces an electron-cloud migration
toward oxygen. Consequently, the electron density of the
carbonyl carbon decreases, rendering the carbonyl carbon more
susceptible to nucleophilic attack by ethylene glycol.15,26
Ethylene glycol molecules act as nucleophiles and attack the
activated carbonyl carbon atoms, triggering transesterication

4.6 Comprehensive performance of the Mytilus
galloprovincialis shell-based catalyst
Mytilus galloprovincialis represents one of the major mariculture
species in China, with a domestic annual output of approximately 570 000 metric tons and a global annual production
ranging from 2.0 to 2.2 million metric tons. Shells account for
40–50% of the total body weight of Mytilus edulis, and
substantial quantities of waste shell are produced during
mariculture and processing. At present, most waste shells are
subjected to low-value applications, including soil amendment
and feed additives. Such routes deliver extremely low resourceutilization eﬃciency and low product added value. Meanwhile,
the random disposal of waste shells may trigger coastal
ecological pollution, which highlights the urgent demand for
developing high-value resource-recovery technologies for shell
wastes.
In this work, waste Mytilus galloprovincialis shells were
adopted as raw materials to fabricate a green catalyst for PET
glycolysis, realizing high-value valorization of aquatic solid
waste. Thermogravimetric analysis reveals that 51.38% of the
shell mass can be converted into catalytic active powder dominated by CaO via high-temperature calcination. The catalyst can
be synthesized by straightforward procedures, including
washing, calcination and grinding. This strategy eﬃciently
converts low-value aquatic solid waste into functional materials
for PET chemical recycling, which complies well with circulareconomy principles.

5 Conclusions
This study explored the green glycolysis process of waste PET
catalyzed by a biomass-based HH-800 catalyst derived from
Mytilus galloprovincialis shells. The proposed strategy realizes
the eﬃcient conversion of diﬃcult-to-recycle de-aluminized
agricultural PET lms into high-value BHET monomers.
Single-factor experiments were performed to clarify the inuence of catalyst dosage, reaction temperature, reaction time,
and ethylene glycol (EG) dosage on BHET yield, whereby the
reasonable variation range of each factor was determined. On
this basis, Box–Behnken response surface methodology was
adopted for the multi-factor collaborative optimization, and
a quadratic regression model correlating the BHET yield with
the four process parameters was successfully established.
ANOVA demonstrated that the interactive signicance of the
factors aﬀecting the BHET yield followed the order EG dosage >
reaction time > reaction temperature > HH-800 catalyst dosage.
The optimal process conditions were determined as 0.49%
catalyst dosage, 198 °C, 1.5 h, and 15.56 g EG. Under such
conditions, the average experimental BHET yield reached
83.95%, which was in good agreement with the model's theoretical prediction.

The developed Mytilus galloprovincialis shell-based catalytic
system exhibits a substantial advantage in resource utilization,
environmental sustainability, and economic performance.
Thermogravimetric results conrmed that the catalytically
active inorganic components account for 51.38% of the raw
Mytilus galloprovincialis shell mass. Utilizing waste Mytilus
galloprovincialis shell byproducts to fabricate functional catalysts achieves the harmless disposal and high-value reutilization of low-value aquatic solid waste, providing a green
alternative to conventional chemically synthesized catalysts. In
addition, the glycolysis process presents nearly 100% atomic
economy, enabling the selective conversion of PET into BHET,
while the unreacted EG solvent can be recovered and reused,
further improving the environmental friendliness of the overall
process. Compared with zinc acetate and transition metal oxide
catalysts, HH-800 is dominated by non-toxic CaO with only trace
amounts of endogenous Zn and K elements. The spent catalyst
can be recycled or directly applied as a soil amendment,
imposing negligible environmental risks.
In terms of energy eﬃciency and economic benets, the
optimal reaction temperature of this system is 198 °C, which is
lower than the typical temperature range (210–240 °C) of
conventional metal-catalyzed PET glycolysis systems, reducing the
unit thermal energy consumption by approximately 12%. Moreover, waste Mytilus galloprovincialis shells require almost no
procurement cost, cutting the raw material cost of the catalyst by
over 95% compared with commercial zinc acetate catalysts. In
conclusion, the waste Mytilus galloprovincialis shell-based catalytic system provides an eﬃcient, low-cost, and feasible technical
route for the green chemical recycling of waste agricultural PET
lms.
