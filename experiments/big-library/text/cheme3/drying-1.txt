ABSTRACT: Drying shrinkage remains a critical durability
challenge in cementitious materials, where conventional prediction
methods are limited by low temporal resolution and lack of
interpretability. This study presents an interpretable machine
learning (ML) framework for real-time prediction of drying
shrinkage in fly ash-containing concrete, integrating high-frequency
experimental measurements with physically meaningful input
features. A data set comprising 79,008 hly observations across 16
mix designs and different curing conditions was used to train and
evaluate various ML models. Ensemble and nonlinear models,
including Random Forest, Extra Trees, K-Nearest Neighbors, and
Multi-Layer Perceptron, achieved high predictive accuracy (R2 ≈
0.99) on the test set with low error margins under both hourly and
daily temporal representations. Besides, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis reveals that multiscale temporal variables
govern shrinkage evolution, with short-term capillary moisture loss and long-term pore-scale desorption captured through distinct
temporal features. Material parameters, particularly fly ash incorporation, exhibit secondary but consistent influence through
microstructural refinement mechanisms based on C−S−H phase nucleation and formation. The proposed framework enables a
transition from periodic testing toward continuous, data-driven monitoring, offering a pathway for real-time quality control and
reduced physical testing of sustainable concrete infrastructures.

1.1. Background

Cement production contributes ∼7% of global CO 2
emissions.1 Drying shrinkage is a fundamental time-dependent
deformation in cementitious materials that directly affects
cracking susceptibility, long-term durability, and serviceability
of concrete infrastructures. Many concrete structures have
functioned in moist environments for many years, with the
humidity levels within these concrete structures exposed to the
atmosphere fluctuating as a result of rainfall and seepage.2,3
The moisture content in concrete structures plays a crucial role
in determining their mechanical and functional properties,
including strength,4,5 young’s modulus,6 thermal and electrical
conductivity,7−9 as well as their overall durability. Furthermore,
fluctuations in the humidity of concrete structures lead to
shrinkage,10 fatigue,11,12 carbonization,13 and freeze−thaw
cycles,14 which compromise their integrity and diminish their
performance. The movement of water within concrete leads to
diffusion of chloride ions15,16 and corrosion of steel reinforcement.17,18
The drying shrinkage process of concrete is characterized by
nonhomogeneous and anisotropic behavior, resulting in
significant structural adaptation. This phenomenon generates

characterized by lengthy and expensive experimental methods,
frequently necessitating considerable laboratory efforts.
Machine learning (ML) algorithms are currently being
developed. ML has the capability to analyze complex data,
automate processes, and personalize experiences in many
industries. In the concrete industry, ML is capable of

relationships. These ML models demonstrate a high level of
accuracy in predicting critical concrete properties, including
compressive strength,41,47−50 fresh properties,51,52 creep,53 and

1.2. Research Gap, Objective, and Contribution

The primary contribution of this work is the development of
an interpretable ML framework for high-resolution shrinkage
prediction using automated hourly monitoring data. In
addition to predictive performance evaluation, the framework
investigates interpretable feature relationships associated with
temporal evolution, material composition, and environmental
conditions under controlled experimental settings. To the best
of our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate real-time
shrinkage sensing with interpretable ML-based prediction. The
novelty of this work is to deliver a continuous, interpretable,
and deployable framework for shrinkage monitoring. This
represents a shift from conventional discrete testing toward
data-driven infrastructure management. Two notable research
gaps in this field include:
1. The key engineering innovation lies in enabling
continuous shrinkage prediction at an hourly resolution,
which is not achievable using existing standard methods
and empirical models.
2. None of the previous studies have addressed real-time
monitoring of shrinkage of sustainable concrete using ML

2. METHODOLOGY
The research methods consist of two parts, as shown in the
flowchart in Figure 1:
(1) In this work, the term ‘real-time’ refers to automated
hourly resolution monitoring using embedded strain-gauge
sensing and data logging, representing near real-time
monitoring conditions. The real-time monitoring data
collection process involved measuring the drying shrinkage of
concrete mixtures containing four different aggregates, fly ash,
curing temperatures and relative humidity. The ASTM
standard testing of four different concrete mixtures was also
performed for comparison.26 It should be noted that the
samples’ sizes in both measurements are different. Typically,
rectangular sample shape per ASTM standard has higher
deformation than the circular shape. This can cause
deformation difference on particular sides of the samples,
subsequently leading to more unstable deformation than the
circular shape.
(2) The development and testing process of several ML
algorithms of single- and ensemble-based models for predicting
drying shrinkage properties. In this part, a correlation analysis
was performed to investigate the relationship between features
and shrinkage. The input parameters were chosen on the basis
of their established physical influence on drying shrinkage.
Temporal variables (i.e., hour and day) capture the timedependent progression of shrinkage, while material-related
parameters (i.e., aggregate type, fly ash content, basalt, and
gravel) reflect the role of the mix composition. Environmental
conditions (curing temperature and relative humidity) were
included because of their strong effect on moisture loss and
shrinkage development. These features were directly available
from our experimental program and consistent with established
factors influencing shrinkage reported in the literature. Next,
the data were divided into test-train sets, and the models were
trained and evaluated. A hyperparameter grid search with 5fold cross-validation and temporal feature comparison was also
performed in this process. After training and evaluation, highperforming models were further analyzed for explainability
using Shapley additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis.

The drying shrinkage behavior of concrete can be classified
into three progressive stages that are primarily governed by
moisture migration and microstructural evolution. The first
stage, known as the plastic shrinkage, occurs within the first 2−
3 h after water is mixed with cement particles, as the hydration
reaction progresses. Then, the early-age rapid shrinkage period
(0−200 h in Figure 3) is characterized by the evaporation of
free water from large capillary pores following demolding and
initial curing. During this phase, a rapid volumetric contraction
occurs due to steep surface moisture gradients, leading to high
internal capillary tension. These tensions initiate the development of microcracks, particularly near the paste−aggregate
interfaces where stiffness mismatches concentrate stress. The
process is strongly influenced by curing temperature, relative
humidity gradients, and the degree of pore connectivity, which
collectively determine the rate of moisture loss and the extent
of early shrinkage deformation. As the concrete transitions into
the intermediate steady-decline period (i.e., 200−2000 h), the
rate of shrinkage begins to diminish as the moisture gradients
within the material stabilize. In this stage, water molecules
continue migrating from finer capillary and gel pores,
accompanied by internal redistribution processes that relieve
localized stress concentrations. The incorporation of fly ash
plays a significant role during this phase, as the ongoing
pozzolanic reaction promotes the formation of an additional
C−S−H gel. This reaction refines the pore structure, reduces
the average pore radius, and enhances their internal humidity
buffering capacity. Consequently, the overall shrinkage rate
decreases, and the internal stress field becomes more balanced,
reflecting a progressive stabilization of the microstructural
framework. Finally, the long-term stabilization stage (i.e.,
2000−4960 h) marks the point where the moisture content of
the concrete achieves equilibrium with the surrounding
environment. Only the bound water within the finest gel
pores continues to desorb gradually, leading to a near-constant
strain response. The shrinkage curve flattens toward its
asymptotic value, indicating that most of the capillary-driven
moisture movement has ceased. Aggregate stiffness and curing
temperature continue to exert minor influences on residual
microstrains. However, their effects are marginal compared to
the earlier phases.74 Microstructurally, the pore water
molecules become stabilized, and no significant additional
deformation is observed. This steady-state condition represents
the long-term dimensional stability of the concrete, marking
the completion of the drying shrinkage process. As mentioned
above, the drying shrinkage measurement in a real-time
manner shows that the microstain values during some periods
are not stable. This is anticipated for the measuring errors. The
paper also aims to analyze the influence of input features that

2.3. Data Preparation and Correlation Analysis

The data that were measured either in real time or per ASTM
standard from Section 2.1 were stored in a preprocessed
Comma Separated Values (CSV) file. Feature extraction was
performed to identify relevant attributes, including material
type (i.e., limestone, basalt, gravel, granite), the presence of fly
ash, and temperature conditions (i.e., 23 and 30 °C). One
encoding was applied to categorical variables to enable
numerical processing by ML models. Statistical aggregates,
such as mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum
shrinkage values, were calculated based on recorded time (day
and hr.) to incorporate temporal trends. Statistical aggregates,
including mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum
shrinkage values, were computed from shrinkage trajectories
across all concrete mixtures to represent temporal evolution
characteristics of the measured data sets. These descriptors
were intended to emulate prior engineering knowledge and
historical shrinkage characteristics that may also be obtainable
from external databases or empirical standards. All input
features are standardized to ensure comparability. This process
adjusts each variable by centering it around zero and scaling it
to have unit variance. By removing differences in magnitude
and distribution across features, the method prevents variables
with larger numeric ranges from dominating the learning
process.

3.2. SHAP-Based Feature Interpretation

SHAP assigns each feature a value representing its marginal
impact on the model’s prediction. This allows mechanistic
discovery for both global model interpretation and local
explanation. Figure 8 presents the SHAP plots for the four topperforming models: KNN, RF, ETR, and MLP, using hourly
input data. Each plot ranks features by their average impact on
model output (mean absolute SHAP value). Color gradients
represent the original feature values (i.e., red = high; blue =
low). The horizontal spread reflects how much each feature
shifts the prediction across the data sets.
For the tree-based models (RF and ETR), time-based
features such as “hour” and “day” consistently dominate,
reinforcing the importance of high-resolution temporal input in
capturing shrinkage dynamics. These models also show
moderate contributions from material-related features such as
“FlyAsh_with Fly ash” and “Material_Basalt,” confirming their
sensitivity to concrete mix composition. In the RF model,
“hour” consistently dominates, confirming that short-term
temporal resolution is the key driver of shrinkage dynamics,
while “day” contributes less. By contrast, the ETR model
distributes importance more evenly across both “hour” and
“day,” representing its tendency to exploit multiple time scales
due to its more randomized split selection. In the KNN model,
the feature importance distribution is similar, though slightly
flatter. KNN leverages the distance between neighboring data
points rather than internal parameter learning. For the MLP
model, the SHAP plot reveals a broader spread of influential
features, with not only “hour” and “day” but also interaction
and environmental variables like curing conditions (i.e.,
“Temperature_30C” and “Temperature_27C”) and “FlyAsh_with Fly ash” exerting strong nonlinear effects. This indicates
the model’s flexibility in capturing complex compound
relationships, though at the cost of lower interpretability,
compared to tree ensembles. Taken together, these four
models reveal consistent patterns. Temporal resolution
(especially “hour”) emerges as the dominant predictor across
all methods, while the material-related features such as fly ash
and basalt show secondary but stable contributions. This crossmodel agreement enhances confidence in the reliability of the
conclusion regarding feature importance.
The dominance of hour temporal input across all highperforming models reflects the governing role of early-stage
capillary water evaporation. During the initial drying period
(0−200 h), moisture loss occurs primarily from larger capillary
pores, generating significant capillary tension. This induces
rapid volumetric contraction and drives the majority of earlyage shrinkage strain. The strong SHAP contribution of hour
therefore captures the kinetics of capillary pressure development and short-term moisture gradients.
In contrast, the contribution of day input represents the
transition toward diffusion-controlled moisture redistribution
within finer pore structures. In this regime (200−2000 h and
beyond), shrinkage is governed by gradual desorption of
physically bound water from gel pores and the relaxation of
internal stress fields. The reduced but persistent importance of
day indicates that ML models are capturing the asymptotic
stabilization behavior associated with long-term equilibrium
moisture conditions.

4. DISCUSSIONS AND IMPLICATIONS
Based on all findings from the work, the ML can be utilized for
prediction of the real-time drying shrinkage values of fly ashcontaining concrete. This is not only applied for laboratory
investigation, but also for the quality control process of
concrete manufacturing plants.81 Many works have been
performed to reduce labor force in quality control and quality
insurance in batching plants. This can reduce labor cost and
reduce testing time, while providing more accurate outcomes.82,83 Moreover, it can be implemented as the SHM
system in any infrastructure under service with the aims of
reducing human workforces and enhancing smart, sustainable,
and resilient cities.84,85 It is assumed that, compared to manual
shrinkage testing protocols (i.e., ASTM requiring periodic
measurements over 28−90 days), the proposed real-time
predictive framework reduces experimental frequency by up to
83% (12 times to 1 time), which corresponds to an estimated
reduction of 67% in material consumption (3 sample to 1
sample) and associated laboratory energy demand. This
reduction arises from minimizing repeated specimen preparation and long-term monitoring requirements. In addition,
improved prediction of shrinkage evolution enables earlier
mitigation of cracking risk, thereby reducing maintenance
frequency and contributing to lower lifecycle CO2 emissions in
cementitious systems.
The comparison reveals clear differences in predictive
capability, particularly in the early-age regime. ACI 209
produces smooth, monotonic shrinkage curves governed by
fixed empirical coefficients, which are unable to capture the
rapid nonlinear evolution observed within the first 100−200 h.
In contrast, the ML models accurately reproduce these
transient dynamics due to their ability to learn high-resolution
temporal dependencies from hourly data. This distinction is
critical, as early-age shrinkage contributes disproportionately to
cracking risk and structural performance.
At intermediate and long-term durations, ACI 209 provides
reasonable trend approximation but exhibits systematic bias in
magnitude and the rate of shrinkage development, particularly
for fly ash-modified mixtures. This limitation arises from the
model’s inability to explicitly account for microstructural
evolution associated with pozzolanic reactions and pore
refinement. The ML framework, by contrast, implicitly
captures these effects through data-driven relationships
between material composition and temporal progression.
Quantitatively, the ML models achieve significantly higher
predictive accuracy (R2 ≈ 0.99) compared to the ACI 209
formulation, with substantially lower error dispersion across all
time scales. More importantly, however, is the difference in
temporal resolution and adaptability. While ACI 209 is
constrained to predefined functional forms and discrete input

5. CONCLUSIONS
This work establishes an interpretable, mechanism-informed
ML framework that transforms shrinkage prediction from a
manual laboratory assessment into a real-time engineering tool
for monitoring and controlling moisture-driven deformation in
concrete. Results can be summarized below:
1. Fly ash incorporated in simultaneously reducing binder
CO2 emissions and improving shrinkage performance,
which is accurately validated by ML-driven analysis.
2. Hourly input data significantly improved prediction
accuracy, particularly in reducing the RMSE. Topperforming models such as KNN, RF, ETR, and MLP
achieved R2 values of 0.99, with RMSE values under 1.2
microstrain. This indicates a high degree of precision
suitable for real-time applications in concrete monitoring.
3. While gains in R2 were modest, the reduction in RMSE
confirmed that a finer temporal resolution enhanced
local accuracy.
4. SHAP analysis strengthened the findings by revealing
that temporal features consistently dominated model
predictions, while material properties, such as fly ash
content and aggregate type, played a critical role.
5. These insights validated the experimental design and
highlighted the potential for integrating interpretable
ML models into engineering workflows for transparent
and trustworthy decision making.
Despite these key contributions, limitations exist. The
present data set is derived from controlled laboratory
conditions using Portland Type I cement, one fly ash source,
20% fly ash replacement level, and 24-MPa concrete mixtures.
Nevertheless, the data set still includes multiple aggregate
types, curing environments, and mixture configurations,
allowing the ML models to learn across different shrinkage
patterns. Future work should extend the framework toward
broader concrete classes, additional supplementary cementitious materials, and field-scale validation. Note that the present
validation framework is based on repeated random train−test
splitting of high-frequency shrinkage observations. Because
temporally adjacent measurements from the same specimen

may appear in both training and testing data sets, partial
temporal information leakage may occur. Future work should

This paper demonstrates that real-time, explainable ML
prediction of concrete shrinkage is both technically viable and
practically impactful for the pathway toward smart, low-carbon
infrastructure management. Future work will focus on
extending this framework for deployment for other low- and
high-strength as well as special concrete mix designs and
integrating it with sensor-based monitoring systems to enable
intelligent, adaptive concrete management. Also, uncertainty
bounds for predictions, sensitivity analysis, and physicsinformed neural networks (PINNs), which rely on physical
and chemical mechanisms of concrete, shall be further
investigated. Also, in this research, statistical descriptors were
incorporated to represent overall temporal evolution characteristics across different mixture configurations and curing
conditions while also emulating prior shrinkage knowledge

Nevertheless, because these descriptors were derived from
measured shrinkage trajectories within the present data set and
combined with random train−test splitting, partial temporal
information leakage may occur. Future work should investigate
stricter temporal and specimen-level validation together with
externally available prior descriptors.
