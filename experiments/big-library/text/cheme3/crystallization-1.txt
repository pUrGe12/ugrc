ABSTRACT: The design and evaluation of biobased processes
involving crystallization can be challenged owing to the lack of
experimental data, leading to inaccurate predictions. This study
presents a method for predicting the crystal purity and recovery
yield of biobased amino acids, including L-alanine (ALA), Lhistidine monohydrochloride monohydrate (HIS), and L-citrulline
(CIT), in evaporative crystallization processes. An effective
distribution model was proposed to describe the relationship
between liquid and crystal purity by using an empirically modified
logistic curve. By coupling mass balance with an effective
distribution model, a method to predict the crystal purity was
developed on the basis of the feed purity and recovery yield of the
crystallization process. Furthermore, a statistical approach was
applied to predict the defective product disturbances. Our results demonstrate that HIS achieves a higher crystal purity more easily
than does ALA. Additionally, a quantitative analysis revealed that for all amino acids, the defective product rate decreases as the
target crystal purity increases and minimum specification purity decreases. This study provides a basis for considering downstream
recovery yield as an important metric in the design and evaluation of biobased processes involving crystallization.

1. INTRODUCTION
Rapid advancements in biotechnology have significantly
increased the requirements for comprehensive design and
evaluation of biobased material production, such as technoeconomic analysis (TEA) and life cycle assessment (LCA).1
When the final product is in a crystalline form, the
crystallization process becomes one of the most significant
stages, as it directly influences both product quality and
recovery yield.2−4 However, during the evaluation of biobased
crystalline products, experimentally validated data based on
industrial process streams are often not considered.5−7 Instead,
the assumed crystallization recovery yield and crystal purity
derived from analogous systems are frequently applied. These
assumptions can considerably reduce the accuracy of the
process design and evaluation outcomes.8
Succinic acid can be produced from feedstocks such as
glucose, cassava root, and spent sulfite liquor using Actinobacillus succinogenes. It has been experimentally demonstrated
that succinic acid with a purity of 90−99% can be recovered
through direct crystallization, at recovery yields of 57−
93%.9−12 In 2022, Tomczyk et al. conducted a TEA on the
conversion of renewable sugars from corn stover feedstock to
succinic acid using A. succinogenes.13 The crystal purity of the
succinic acid obtained through direct crystallization by cooling
to 4 °C was assumed to be 99.7%, although the assumed
crystallization recovery yield was not explicitly stated. In 2023,

Because biobased feed liquids for crystallization typically
contain a wide variety of impurities, obtaining an accurate
relationship between the crystal purity and recovery yield
requires a crystallization process development stage that is
both time-consuming and resource-intensive. However, the
most reliable results can be ensured for biobased crystalline
products with available plant data from commercial production, as in our previous study on the LCA of L-methionine and
19,20
L-lysine.
However, this approach is not feasible for earlystage evaluations. The most practical alternative for early-stage
design and evaluation is to conduct crystallization experiments
under fixed conditions, similar to the actual production
environments, to obtain limited data, including crystal purity
and recovery yield.
In industrial crystallization processes, crystal purity is
determined by the complex interactions of various process
factors, such as agglomeration, surface washing efficiency,
liquid inclusion, and solid solution formation. Recently, a
method was proposed to express these comprehensive
phenomenological results using the distribution coefficient,
which is defined as the ratio of the actual impurity
concentration in a crystal to that in a bulk liquid.21,22 This
can be seen as an attempt to apply the concept of the effective
distribution coefficient (represents the ratio of the actual
impurity concentration in the crystal to that in the bulk liquid),
which is often used in melt crystallization, to industrial slurry
crystallization.23−25 Although the effective distribution coefficient does not have a strict thermodynamic meaning,26 it is
similar to the general trend of distribution diagrams influenced
by solid solutions, which are commonly encountered in amino
acids.27−29
The main contributions of this study can be summarized as
follows: (1) effective distribution data was experimentally
obtained for biobased materials, such as L-alanine (ALA), Lhistidine monohydrochloride monohydrate (HIS), and Lcitrulline (CIT), which can be utilized in the development of
industrial processes for newly developed biobased substances.
(2) An empirical mathematical model was proposed to
interpret the effective distribution data, thereby developing a
method for quantitatively predicting the crystal purity and
recovery yield. (3) A statistical approach was formulated to
predict the defective product rate based on the distribution of
crystal purity residuals under process disturbances while
considering the target crystal and minimum specification
purities. 4) It was suggested that, in addition to the titer, rate,
and yield metrics in a fermentation process,30−33 which are
significant in the design and evaluation of biobased processes,
the downstream recovery yield metric should also be
considered important.

2.2. Evaporative Crystallization
Evaporative crystallization experiments were conducted using an inhouse 2 L jacketed glass evaporative crystallizer. The crystallizer had a
cylindrical shape with an internal diameter of 1270 mm, and it could
charge up to 2 L of the process liquid. The jacket was located outside
the crystallizer, surrounding the crystallizer to half its height. To
prevent scaling on the inner walls of the crystallizer owing to localized
evaporative crystallization, which can occur if the process liquid level
drops below the height of the jacket, the total volume of the process
liquid was set to always remain above the height of the jacket. The
bottom of the crystallizer had a rounded conical shape with an angle
of approximately 25° for the easy discharge of the slurry after the
completion of the experiment. A two-way Teflon valve was installed at
the bottom of the crystallizer to allow the slurry discharge.
Stirring was performed using a Teflon-coated paddle-type impeller
(width: 30 mm, height: 15 mm, and thickness: 4 mm) driven by an
overhead stirrer (PL-SS20DW, Poonglim Tech, Korea) through a
magnetic drive. The lower end of the impeller was positioned 30 mm
above the bottom of the evaporative crystallizer. The stirring speed
was set to 500 rpm to ensure that the crystal particles were mixed well
without causing bubble formation owing to vortex.
Distilled water was used as the heating medium for the crystallizer
jacket. The temperature of the heating medium was controlled at 80
°C using a circulation thermostat (DYNEO DD-310F, Julabo,
Germany). A glass vapor−liquid separator was positioned at the top
of the evaporative crystallizer, and it was connected to a glass
condensate container and glass condenser. The glass condenser was
coil type, with a diameter and height of 80 and 550 mm, respectively.
To ensure sufficient condensation of the evaporated vapor, the
temperature of the cooling medium was controlled at 5 °C using a
circulation thermostat (DYNEO DD-310 FF, Julabo, Germany). A
diaphragm vacuum pump (NVP-1000; Eyela, Japan) was used to
evacuate the gas inside the crystallizer. The internal pressure of the
crystallizer was maintained at 100 mbar using a digital pressure
regulator (NVC2200; Eyela, Japan).
After evaporative crystallization, the crystal slurry was separated
from the mother liquid using a basket centrifuge (H−110F, Kokusan,
Japan). During the mother liquid separation stage, the centrifuge was
operated at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The crystals were washed with
distilled water to remove any remaining mother liquid from the crystal
surfaces. The amount of wash water was set to 50 wt % of the
expected crystal mass. During the washing and subsequent dewatering
stages, the centrifuge was operated at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The wet
crystals were then evenly spread on a stainless-steel tray and placed in
a convection oven (BF 360PO, Bio Free, Korea) controlled at 70 °C,
where samples were dried for 24 h until no change of weight. In this
study, the terms ‘initial’ and ‘final’ refer to the crystallization feed state
and the dried crystal state, respectively.

A Roozeboom diagram is a type of distribution diagram that
represents the solvent-free composition of a target material in both
the liquid and solid phases in a two-dimensional graph. It can
effectively illustrate the correlation between the solvent-free purity of
the processed liquid and the purity of the crystals during
crystallization.27,29 Although the Roozeboom diagram typically
represents the thermodynamic equilibrium between the liquid and
solid phases, a similar form of an effective distribution diagram can be
constructed by using an effective solvent-free composition, even if
phase equilibrium has not been reached. This approach provides
valuable information for industrial crystallization processes, because it
considers various factors that can influence the crystallization process.
During crystallization, crystals do not typically achieve homogeneous compositions because of the significantly slower mass transfer
rate in the solid phase. Therefore, in batch crystallization processes,
the solvent-free composition of the liquid and crystals changes as the
process progresses. The weight fraction of the target material on a
solvent-free basis in the solid phase, commonly referred to as “crystal
purity” in industrial terms (wsf,sol
X ), was determined by measuring the
solvent-free composition of the crystals. The weight fraction of the
target material on a solvent-free basis in the liquid phase, commonly
referred to as “liquid purity” in industrial terms (wXsf,liq), was
experimentally determined by averaging the liquid purity of the
crystallization feed and mother liquid.

2.6. Defective Product Rate
In an actual crystallization process, even with the same crystallization
feed purity and target recovery yield, the crystal purity of the final
product may vary because of various disturbances. During the
development process, the impact of these disturbances is represented
sf,sol
by the residual crystal purity, which is the difference between wX,calc
sf,sol
calculated using eq 1 and wX,exp
obtained from actual experiments.
The subscripts calc and exp refer to the calculated and experimental
values, respectively. In this study, outliers in the crystal purity
residuals were removed using the interquartile range method,38 and
the normality was tested using the Shapiro−Wilk test.39 Subsequently,
a statistical analysis was conducted to simulate the crystal purity
distribution upon obtaining the crystallization feed purity and target
crystal purity. Finally, the defective product rate was predicted on the
basis of the minimum specified purity and target crystal purity. In this
study, the defective product rate is the probability of the fraction of
final crystal products that do not satisfy the predefined quality
specification. Specifically, a defective product refers to a final crystal
product whose crystal purity is lower than the minimum specification
purity. Because long-term plant-scale production data were not
available, the defective product rate was statistically estimated from
the distribution of the crystal purity residuals. The residual crystal
purity was assumed to follow a normal distribution, with the mean
and standard deviation obtained from the experimental residuals for
each amino acid. Therefore, for a given target crystal purity, the
defective product rate was calculated as the lower tail probability that
the actual crystal purity falls below the minimum specification purity.

If the recovery yield reaches 100% and there are no
processes that cause purity changes, such as thermal
decomposition and biological degradation, the crystal purity
converges to the feed purity. Although such a scenario is
difficult to achieve during crystallization, it is possible in a
granulation process combined with a drying process. During
the granulation and drying processes, most of the solvent is
removed from the feed, and the remaining substances form
solid particles. Although this study does not focus deeply into
this subject, the granulation process has been commercially
implemented for bioderived amino acids such as L-lysine, Lthreonine, L-tryptophan, L-valine, and L-isoleucine.45−48 Additionally, relatively low-purity granulated amino acids, such as Llysine, L-threonine, L-tryptophan, and L-valine, have been
registered with the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)
under the current animal food generally recognized as safe
(GRAS) notification after thorough safety evaluations.49
The simulation results in Figure 4 provide information only
on the impact of the increase in the recovery yield on the
crystal purity. However, in actual industrial evaporative
crystallization processes, it is not feasible to increase the
concentration infinitely to improve the recovery yield.
Increasing the concentration of the crystallization slurry leads
to an increase in viscosity, which can reduce the evaporation
rate owing to excessively high viscosity or failure to maintain
the minimum required fluidity for operating an industrial
evaporator.50 Therefore, a possible alternative for practical
industrial applications is to divide the crystallization process
into multiple stages of mother liquid crystallization. For
example, evaporative crystallization can be carried out until an
appropriate target viscosity is reached, followed by crystalmother liquid separation. The mother liquid generated from
the separation process will undergo the next stage of
evaporative crystallization. By repeating these stages, the
recovery yield can be increased without causing excessive
viscosity problems. Another alternative is to recycle a portion
of the mother liquid into the crystallization feed.51−53 This
mother liquid recycling method is economical when the fixed
cost of an evaporator is high.

0.01201, respectively. The CIT crystal purity residual data
ranged from −0.02439 to 0.03076 with a mean and standard
deviation of 0.001238 and 0.01102, respectively. According to
a Shapiro−Wilk test, the crystal purity residuals of all materials
follow a normal distribution, with p-values exceeding 0.05. This
indicates that normality was statistically significant in all cases.
Figure 5 confirms that the cumulative normal distribution
closely matches the crystal purity residuals of ALA, HIS, and
CIT. This provides a basis for using the crystal purity residuals
to statistically interpret the defective product rate through the
application of a normal distribution.

4. CONCLUSIONS
In this study, the relationship between liquid and crystal
purities during the evaporative crystallization of ALA, HIS, and
CIT was experimentally demonstrated and regressed using an
effective distribution model with a modified logistic curve. In
addition, by coupling the mass balance with the effective
distribution model, the crystal purity was quantitatively
predicted based on the crystallization feed purity and recovery
yield. Finally, the defective production rate was statistically
interpreted in relation to the target crystal and minimum
specification purities from the distribution of crystal purity
residuals. The results revealed that HIS was relatively easier to
obtain as a high-purity crystal than the other two materials,
whereas ALA was the most difficult to purify. Furthermore, in
all cases, a decrease in the target crystal purity and an increase
in the minimum specification purity led to an increase in the
defective product rate. This study emphasizes the importance
of the downstream recovery yield in the design and evaluation
of biobased processes.
