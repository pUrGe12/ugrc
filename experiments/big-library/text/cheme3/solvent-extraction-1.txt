ABSTRACT: This research aimed to establish the optimal conditions for
the purification of alpha-mangostin from mangosteen pericarp, employing a
two-stage process involving solvent extraction and liquid chromatography.
Initially, a sequential solvent extraction protocol, utilizing deionized water
and hexane, was implemented to effectively remove both polar and nonpolar
impurities. Subsequently, alpha-mangostin was extracted using acetonitrile,
and the solvent was removed through evaporation and precipitation, yielding
a xanthone-rich fraction. This fraction was then subjected to quantitative
liquid chromatography, wherein the influence of mobile phase flow rate,
mobile phase composition, feed concentration, and column temperature on
purification efficiency was systematically evaluated. The optimized
purification parameters, namely a mobile phase flow rate of 4 mL/min, a
mobile phase composition of 80% acetonitrile and 20% deionized water, a
feed concentration of 3 mg/mL, and a column temperature of 35 °C, resulted in alpha-mangostin with a purity of 83.33%.

1. INTRODUCTION
Mangosteen (Garcinia mangostana Linn) is a plant predominantly found in Southeast Asian countries, particularly
Thailand, Malaysia, and Indonesia. Currently, it stands as
one of Thailand’s most significant fruits, with a production of
251,635 tons in 2022.1 The pericarp of mangosteen is rich in
several important bioactive compounds, including α-mangostin, β-mangostin, and γ-mangostin, all of which are xanthone
derivatives. These compounds possess various pharmacological
properties, such as antibacterial,2 antiviral,3,4 and antifungalactivities.5,6
Furthermore, mangosteen extracts exhibit anti-inflammatory
effects. Chen et al. (2008) investigated the anti-inflammatory
activity of mangosteen pericarp extracts, isolating α-mangostin
and γ-mangostin. Both compounds effectively inhibited the
production of nitric oxide and Prostaglandin E2 in LPSstimulated RAW 264.7 cells, with IC50 values of 12.4 μM and
10.1 μM, respectively. Additionally, α-mangostin demonstrated
significant anti-inflammatory activity in carrageenan-induced
paw edema in mice, substantially reducing swelling. These
findings suggest that α-mangostin exhibits superior antiinflammatory efficacy compared to γ-mangostin.7
Given its potent anti−inflammatory properties, α-mangostin
has garnered considerable interest for its potential in various
cancer treatments. Numerous studies have unequivocally
demonstrated its ability to inhibit cancer cell proliferation
and reduce tumor metastasis. Akao et al. (2008) explored the
inhibitory effects of mangosteen xanthones, including αmangostin, β-mangostin, and γ-mangostin, on human colon

The extraction of α-mangostin from mangosteen pericarp
typically involves solvent extraction. The choice of solvent is
critical, as its polarity should be similar to that of the target
compound for optimal extraction efficiency. Given that αmangostin is a low-polarity molecule, its solubility in water is
limited, rendering water an inefficient solvent for its extraction.
Sage et al. (2018) reported a yield of 318 mg of α-mangostin
from 5 g of dried mangosteen pericarp using methanol as a
solvent.12 Rachmadhani et al. (2023) utilized a percolation
system with various solvents, achieving yields of 3.05% with
ethyl acetate, 0.47% with ethanol, and 0.95% with acetone for
α-mangostin.13 However, single−step solvent extraction of
bioactive compounds from natural raw materials often results
in extracts contaminated with other compounds.14 This
contamination can limit the utility of the extract, as impurities
may lead to adverse effects or obscure experimental results.
Therefore, enhancing the purity of the extract is crucial, as it
also increases the product’s value. Preparative liquid
chromatography is commonly employed for purification.
Tangprompan et al. (2023) demonstrated the isolation of αmangostin and γ-mangostin from mangosteen pericarps using
isocratic preparative chromatography, achieving HPLC peak
purities of 98.34% for α-mangostin and 98.46% for γmangostin.15 Despite its effectiveness, preparative liquid
chromatography is a high-cost technique, and its columns
and equipment are prone to clogging, requiring precise control
of operating conditions for optimal separation. Furthermore,
the process consumes a large volume of solvents.
As an alternative approach for producing high-purity αmangostin, Han et al. (2024) proposed a sequential extraction
method from mangosteen pericarp. This method involves
using solvents with different polarities to remove undesired
compounds before extracting the target compound with a
solvent of similar polarity. The proposed sequence of solvents
includes deionized water, hexane, and acetonitrile to achieve
higher purity of α-mangostin.16 Building upon this, the current
research applies this sequential extraction method within a
percolation system, which is suitable for larger-scale
production beyond the lab-scale. The resulting extract is
then subjected to reverse-phase liquid chromatography to
obtain high-purity α-mangostin. The study investigates the
influence of mobile phase composition, extract solution
concentration, temperature, and mobile phase flow rate on
the separation efficiency of α-mangostin. The outcomes of this
research are expected to provide valuable insights for further
development toward increasing the production capacity of αmangostin from mangosteen pericarp.

2. MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1. Materials. Mangosteens were sourced from Simummuang Market, a major agricultural distribution center in
Thailand. Acetonitrile, methanol, and isopropyl alcohol
(analytical reagent (AR) and high-performance liquid
chromatography (HPLC) grades) were purchased from ACI
Labscan. Deionized water (resistivity: 18.2 MΩ·cm) was
employed as a component of the mobile phase for HPLC
analysis and preparative liquid chromatography. For quantitative nuclear magnetic resonance (qNMR) analysis to
determine the purity of alpha-mangostin, 1,2,4,5-tetrachloro3-nitrobenzene (TraceCERT) was used as an internal
standard, and chloroform-d (99.8+ atom % D, containing
0.02−0.04 v/v% TMS; Thermo Scientific) served as the NMR
solvent.
2.2. Preparation of Mangosteen Pericarp Powder.
Mangosteen pericarps were separated from the fruit segments
and calyxes, then subjected to drying in a hot air oven
(Memmert, Model: UF110) at 60 °C for 48 h. The dried
pericarps were subsequently pulverized into a powder using a
grinder (Spring Green Evolution) for 1 min, yielding particles
smaller than 60 mesh. The resulting mangosteen pericarp
powder was then vacuum-packed and stored at −18 °C until
further solvent extraction and purification. To evaluate the
stability of alpha-mangostin under the drying conditions
utilized in this study (60 °C for 48 h), an experiment was
conducted. A quantity of 70 g of the vacuum-dried mangosteen
pericarp powder was mixed with 2 L of deionized water. The
mixture was then placed in a convection oven at 60 °C for 48 h
to simulate the drying process. After drying, the powder was
extracted using acetonitrile in a percolation system, as
described in Section 2.3. The alpha-mangostin content in the
extract was quantified by HPLC, yielding a value of 64.94 mg/
g. This result was compared to a control sample of vacuumdried powder (not subjected to the 60 °C heat treatment) that
was also extracted with acetonitrile, which yielded 64.36 mg/g.
The minimal difference in yield confirms that the drying
process at 60 °C for 48 h does not significantly affect the
stability of alpha-mangostin.
2.3. Sequential Solvent Extraction of Mangosteen
Pericarp. The authors adopted the methodology outlined by
Han et al. (2024) for the sequential removal of polar and
nonpolar impurities prior to α-mangostin extraction. This
strategic sequence of solvents�water, followed by hexane, and
finally acetonitrile�was chosen to systematically purify the
final extract. A modified procedure was implemented for a
larger batch scale to enhance efficiency. Initially, powdered
mangosteen pericarp (50 g) was subjected to an aqueous
extraction. Deionized water was used first to wash away highly
polar, water-soluble impurities. This is a critical preliminary
step that significantly reduces the presence of nontarget
compounds, thereby increasing the efficiency and selectivity of
subsequent extraction steps. Briefly, the powder was added to a
2 L Duran bottle with 2000 mL of deionized water. The
mixture was thoroughly agitated and allowed to settle for 2 h.
Subsequently, approximately 1400 mL of the clear supernatant
was decanted, and the residue was resuspended in an
equivalent volume of deionized water. This washing step was
repeated five times. The resulting solid residue was then dried
in an oven at 80 °C for 24 h, finely ground, and stored in a
vacuum-sealed bag.

In this set of experiments, the flow rate of the mobile phase

Subsequently, the water-extracted mangosteen pericarp
powder was subjected to hexane extraction. This step was
performed to specifically target and remove less polar,
nontarget compounds, which could otherwise coextract with
α-mangostin. By removing these impurities, the hexane
extraction serves as a crucial prepurification stage. The ratio
used was 50 g of powder to 150 mL of hexane. This extraction
was performed twice, with each extraction lasting 20 min. The
hexane extracts were then analyzed using high-performance
liquid chromatography (HPLC) to quantify the α-mangostin
content. The hexane-extracted pericarp powder was then
processed using a rotary evaporator under vacuum for 4 h to
remove residual hexane, followed by vacuum-packing and
storage.
The hexane-extracted mangosteen pericarp powder was
further subjected to extraction with acetonitrile. This final
solvent was selected because its polarity is well-suited for
isolating the low-polarity α-mangostin molecule while leaving
behind most of the remaining polar and nonpolar impurities.
This strategic sequential approach effectively serves as a
prepurification process, resulting in a higher purity of the final
extract and enhancing the overall efficiency of subsequent
purification steps, such as preparative liquid chromatography.
This extraction with acetonitrile was performed at a ratio of 50
g of powder to 150 mL of acetonitrile. This extraction was
performed in triplicate, with each extraction lasting 20 min.
The combined acetonitrile extracts were concentrated using a
rotary evaporator for 15 min. The concentrated extract was
then mixed with five times its volume of deionized water and
stirred with a magnetic stirrer for 20 min to induce
precipitation. The resulting precipitate was collected by
vacuum filtration using a Buchner funnel with nylon membrane
filter paper (0.45 μm pore size) and dried in an oven at 60 °C
for 24 h. The resulting dried powder was finely ground and
designated as the xanthone-rich fraction.

3.1. Production of Xanthone Powder. The raw material
for xanthone powder production is mangosteen peel powder,
prepared through a series of processes including drying, size
reduction, and sieving. Analysis of compounds in mangosteen
peel extract revealed a mixture of both highly polar and less

In this study, deionized (DI) water washing was

3.3 Purification of Alpha-Mangostin. Alpha-mangostin
with a purity of 86% (HPLC) was obtained from the xanthone
powder derived from the 3-step solvent extraction process, as
previously reported in section 3.1. However, to prevent
undesirable effects from impurities present in the product,
the powder was dissolved and subjected to preparative highperformance liquid chromatography (HPLC) for further

optimized by adjusting parameters such as temperature, mobile
phase flow rate, and mobile phase composition.

As depicted in Figure 7, increasing the acetonitrile

the separation time and solvent consumption. This observation

interactions, resulting in faster elution of alpha-mangostin. The
accelerated elution directly contributed to lower solvent

is a critical factor for the economic viability of the process.

4. CONCLUSIONS
Mangosteen pericarp has been successfully utilized as a raw
material for the production of high-purity alpha-mangostin.
The initial extraction involved a percolation system using
organic solvents, followed by reverse-phase column chromatography for preliminary separation. A key step in enhancing
purity involved washing the extract with water and hexane to
remove impurities before subsequent extraction with acetonitrile, also via percolation. This refined initial extraction yielded
59.74 mg/g of xanthones.
This xanthone-rich extract was then fed into a Preparative
Liquid Chromatography (C18 reverse-phase) system for
further purification. We systematically investigated the
influence of several key parameters on the separation duration
and recovery of alpha-mangostin, including mobile phase ratio,
input concentration, temperature, and mobile phase flow rate.
