Water-Splitting Hydrogen Production by an Iron-Modified Bentonite
Clay (BC) Laced with Ag Nanoclusters
Mantsopa K. Zamisa,* Giuseppe Cavallaro, Busisiwe P. Mabuea, Olayemi J. Fakayode,* Mayetu Segale,

ABSTRACT: This study examines the use of natural bentonite clay (BC) modified with aluminum and iron as low-cost
electrocatalysts for the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) in an acidic medium. The successful modification process was
accompanied by changes in the basal spacing, an increase in the BET surface area and pore volume, and promotion of mass transfer
for enhanced hydrogen production. After mechanically blending with silver (Ag) nanoclusters, the more sustainable and higher
surface-area exhibiting Fe-clay composite showed about 21% increase in hydrogen production and a 134% increase in turnover
frequency (TOF) relative to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE). The Ag−Fe−Clay composite achieved a current density of
274 mA/cm2, similar to that of the commercial palladium black catalyst at −1.20 V (vs RHE). This work demonstrates the potential
of Ag-laced Fe-modified bentonite clay as a nonexpensive sustainable alternative to the state-of-the-art highly expensive (HER)
catalysts with an industrial output of about 1018 mA/cm2 at −2.22 V vs RHE applied potential. Further work is ongoing to evaluate
the performance of the clay with other metallic catalytic agents.

INTRODUCTION
Increasing energy demands and environmental pollution
associated with fossil fuels have led to a focus on developing
alternative technological solutions. Hydrogen has been
considered as the future replacement of fossil fuels.1,2 It is
attractive based on the fact that it can be produced from
abundant resources such as biomass and water and produces
zero-carbon emissions after combustion. This has led to
increasing efforts in its production and storage for a complete
economy.3 In addition, the hydrogen evolution reaction
(HER) through electrochemical water splitting can be
performed at room temperature and atmospheric pressure,
making it the most efficient, less expensive, and eco-friendly
process,4 among most other hydrogen generation processes
(e.g., coal gasification and methane reforming).5,6
The nature of the electrocatalyst employed during the water
splitting for hydrogen production is very crucial due to its
impact on production efficiency. Noble metal platinum (Pt)
and its related metals, such as palladium (Pd), exhibit excellent

Clays are fascinating naturally available materials in many
parts of the world with several beneficial properties such as
high-surface areas, plasticity, swelling, ion exchange capacities,
surface tunability,13 and adsorption.14 Many clays exhibit
different morphologies, particle sizes, and compositions. As a
result, they have been exploited for many functions, including
water treatment,15−17 drug delivery,18−20 flame retardance,21
and as fillers in papers, cosmetics,22 paints and food
packaging.23 For example, a halloysite clay possesses a
nanotubular structure,24−26 while bentonite displays a sheetlike structural feature.27 Another fascinating thing about clay
structure is the fact that it can be functionalized or tuned to
achieve better functional performance. For example, bentonite
can be modified to enhance its catalytic functions.28−30 For
example, in 2017, Zhang et al. reported the significantly
superior performance of Au−Ti−Ce-modified bentonite clay
toward hydrogen production through methanol steam
reforming with a 72% methanol conversion and 99% of
hydrogen selectivity at 350 °C, compared to other tested
catalysts.31 Modification involves the replacement of some
cationic guest ions within the clay interlayers with some
transition-metal ions, such as those of Al, Au, Ti, Cu, Ce, Cr,
and Fe. Subjection of the modified clay to a thermal
environment causes a transformation with enhanced catalytic
properties. In terms of the water splitting process, it has been
reported that clay materials inherently favor the alkaline
medium oxygen evolution reaction (OER) better than the
acidic hydrogen evolution process (HER) counterparts due to
their intrinsic catalytic compositional make-ups and stability in
the respective media.32 However, doping the clay materials
through the inclusion of transition metals within their
structure, alongside the use of polymeric proton exchange
membrane materials, may provide a solution for stability and
further enhancement of efficiency in the acidic medium.
This report exploits the potential of bentonite clay
successfully as an electrocatalyst for water-splitting hydrogen
production in an acidic medium. Previously, many modified
clay studies for hydrogen production have focused on highenergy-demanding methanol steam reforming, biomass pyrolysis, NaBH4 hydrolysis, and acetic acid reforming. However,
our present work demonstrates a relatively lower-energydemanding electrochemical water splitting in an acidic medium
using Fe-modified bentonite clay (Fe-BC). In addition, unlike
many Ag-modified clay reports that focused on photocatalysis
or antibacterial applications, our work presents an enhancement of the hydrogen evolution reaction activity of Fe-BC
through composting with silver nanoparticles, achieving better
overpotential, lower Tafel slopes, and better charge transfer
efficiency. Furthermore, the composite material was benchmarked against the commercial Pd black product, providing
information about the overpotential and, thus, energy input
relativity.
Initially, the clay was modified with Al3+ as well as Fe2+ ions
to evaluate their effects on surface area properties, thermal
stability, morphology, as well as electrocatalytic performance.
However, for sustainability purposes, in terms of a cheaper
alternative coupled with a higher surface area and thermal
stability, Fe-modified clay was found to be better and,
therefore, was chosen over that of the Al counterpart for

EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

Chemicals
All chemicals were of analytical grade, purchased from Sigma-Aldrich,
and used without further purification. Commercially available
bentonite clay was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used as a
precursor for the preparation of the modified clays (BCs). Aluminum
sulfate hexadecahydrate (Al2(SO4)3·16H2O), iron(II) sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO4·7H2O), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were used to
prepare the metal solutions.

Preparation of Al and Fe Solutions
Modified materials were prepared from natural bentonite with a
structural formula of IV[Si 7.43 Al 0.57 ]VI[Al 2.96 Fe 0.73 Mg 0.24 ].
(Na0.71K0.21Ca0.01; O22).33 BCs were prepared by cationic exchange
reactions between the natural bentonite and hydroxy-oligomeric
solutions of Al3+ or Fe2+. The direct addition of dry clay to the metal
ion solution and the use of ultrasonication to speed up the
homogenization and intercalation steps were adopted from previous
studies.34 The precursor salts, namely, aluminum sulfate hexadecahydrate (Al2(SO4)3·16H2O) and iron(II) sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO4·
7H2O), were used as sources of Al and Fe, respectively.
The Al and Fe solutions were prepared by slowly adding 0.4 M
NaOH solution to either 0.4 M Al or Fe solutions, using Al2(SO4)3·
16H2O and FeSO4·7H2O as sources, at a NaOH/Al or NaOH/Fe
molar ratios of 2:1 (post reaction pH: Al = 3.32, Fe = 7.61). The
solutions underwent continuous vigorous stirring at room temperature for 2 h, followed by ultrasonication for 30 min. The intercalation
procedure was performed using the dry modification method,35 i.e.,
without prior swelling of the clay: dry bentonite clay (1.0 g per 25 mL
modification solution), equivalent to a metal/clay ratio of 10 mmol/g,
was subjected to constant stirring at room temperature for 2 h,
followed by 30 min of ultrasonication. The resultant intercalated clay
underwent centrifugation at 4500 rpm, followed by washing with
distilled H2O, five times in 50 mL quantities. The recovered clay was
then oven-dried for 3 h at 100 °C and finally calcined at 500 °C for 2
h.

Ag−Fe−BC Preparation
The silver particles were synthesized by heating a mixture of 50 mL of
0.01 M AgNO3 solution with a solution of 50 mL (0.1 M NaOH
solution in ethanol) from room temperature (22 °C) to boiling phase.
This was followed by boiling of the resulting solution for an additional
10 min. The particles were separated from the supernatant solution
via decantation and washed several times with water before being
dried under ambient conditions for 12 h. Afterward, 13.8 mg of the assynthesized Ag was mechanically mixed with 4.4 mg of Fe-BC to
make the Ag−Fe−BC composite.

Characterization
The textural properties, morphological characteristics, and elemental
compositions of the bentonite clay and the prepared metal-modified
BCs were analyzed using BET surface probing system and fieldemission scanning electron microscope with an energy-dispersive Xray spectrometer (FESEM−EDS, JEOL JSM-7800F, Japan). The
morphology and the clay layer spacing were evaluated with a
transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL 2100 F) operating at
an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. Ethanol was used to disperse the
clay samples, which were sonicated in a bath sonicator for 5 min.
Subsequently, a drop of the clay dispersion was deposited on a
carbon-coated copper grid and left to air-dry before analysis. The
Brunauer−Emmett−Teller (BET) nitrogen adsorption−desorption
isotherms, at −196 °C, of raw bentonite clay and BCs were measured
using a Micromeritics 3030 TriStar II Plus instrument. The sample
preparation procedure began by allowing an inert gas to flow over the
samples (∼0.2 g) at 90 °C for 1 h, followed by degassing under
vacuum at 150 °C overnight to eliminate any adsorbed gases and

Similarity in the XRD peaks was observed for all materials,

with the BCs, compared to that of the natural clay, indicates
collapsed pillars within the clay-modified structures. This
collapse of the pillared clay structure is often associated with
the thermal synthesis condition at higher temperature, typically
≥500 °C. During high heat treatment, inherent thermally
induced mechanical pressure may occur, lowering the intensity
and causing a shift in the basal space spot to the left rather than
to the right of the 2-theta crystallographic plane. This causes
changes to the typical structural ordering (collapse of the
pillar) as explained by Pichowicz and Mokaya in their report.38

BET Analysis

TGA Analysis

The textural properties of natural and modified bentonite clays
are presented in Figure 1b and Table 1. The textural properties
include BET-specific surface area (SBET), porosity, total pore
volume (Vp), micropore volume (Vmic), and average pore
diameter (dpore). A type IV(a) isotherm was observed for all
tested materials according to the IUPAC classification.39 This
confirms that all samples were porous in nature. Also, all
materials revealed an H3 hysteresis loop similar to the one
depicted in Denohue’s report (Donohue and Aranovich,
1998).40 However, the modification of the bentonite clays
(with Al and Fe) increased the SBET to some extent, suggesting
the modified clays exhibited better surface properties. The
lower N2 adsorption by natural clay denotes less contribution
from the micropores than that of the BCs.41 Desai et al.
observed a type IVa and an H2 hysteresis loop for natural
bentonite for conical pores, together with an H4 hysteresis
with slit-like pores for Cr−BCs.41 The natural clay and BCs
exhibit mesoporous features, with pore diameters of 6.06 nm
for natural bentonite and as low as 3.7 nm for Fe-BC. Al-clay
shows little change in the pore dimension relative to that
shown by the natural clay, causing a slight change in the
specific surface area (about 37% vs 315% for Fe-clay). Upon
modification, the surface area of bentonite increased by 4.2
times for Fe−BCs post calcination, higher than that of Al-BC
by 204%. The micropore and total volumes of the BCs
increased with an increase in surface area, while the pore
diameters decreased. Increase in the total pore volume (Vpore)
ranged from 13−199%, and this was from 0.028 cm3/g for the
natural bentonite to 0.031 and 0.083 cm3/g for Al- and Fe−
BCs, respectively. A good porosity distribution of both
micropores (<2 nm) and mesopores (2−50 nm) is highly
beneficial toward mass transfer of reactants and products.
Overall, the Fe-clay exhibited the highest surface area among
the tested materials. Compared to other previous reports, the
relatively smaller BET surface areas reported in this study were
due to a number of reasons. First, the commercial bentonite
used in this study had a low initial surface area of 9 m2/g due
to dense particle aggregation, unlike purified montmorillonites
that typically start at 50−100 m2/g. Also, the use of higher
calcination temperature, viz., 500 °C, may be suboptimal for
the modified BCs; lower temperatures (350−400 °C) might
preserve more microporosity. Furthermore, the presence of
some metal oxide particles may necessitate blocking of
micropores through deposition on external surfaces rather
than intercalating. Nonetheless, despite the modest BET
increase (from 9 to 37 m2/g for Fe-BC), the material showed
significant improvement in the HER performance, suggesting
other factors, in addition to the surface area, played important
roles.

Figure 1c,d shows the TG and DTG curves of the natural and
modified clays. As a general result, the materials exhibited three
mass losses. The first mass loss was detected from room
temperature to ca. 150 °C due to the removal of physically
adsorbed water from the surface of the clay sheets, as well as
water weakly attached either within the BC galleries or bonded
to the polycations or modifiers of the BCs. The second mass
loss was observed between 382 and 500 °C, resulting from the
removal of H2O molecules aligned with the modifiers. This
indicates that the clay’s structure of the material was
maintained, suggesting good thermal stability of the BCs due
to the clay’s crystalline structure. However, at this stage, the
natural clay underwent higher mass loss than the BCs. The
third mass loss (after 500 °C) is typically associated with the
destruction of the clay’s crystalline structure alongside
dehydroxylation processes.42,43 However, while the clay and
Al-BC showed a loss in mass at 600 and 800, respectively, the
Fe-BC exhibit negligible loss at this amorphization stage,
suggesting its relatively stronger thermal stability compared to
those of the clay and Al-BC.

It was notably observed that the Fe/Ag system showed
comparative performance with the commercial Pd black
catalyst at −1.2 V (vs RHE), exhibiting a current density of
274 mA/cm2. This is approximately equivalent to about 50%
increase in energy/electricity consumed relative to that
consumed by the commercial Pd black catalyst (Table 4).
Thus, further tuning of either the Fe-clay or its Ag
nanocomposite structure may be necessary to improve the
efficiency of HER activity, in terms of shifting the energy input,
i.e., the electricity consumed, to a very low potential. This is
important for achieving more sustainability advantages during
industrial upscaling. In the context of Tafel plot analysis, the
order of increasing Tafel slope was graphite/Nafion/Pd <
graphite/Nafion/Fe-clay/Ag < graphite/Nafion/Fe-clay/Agext
< graphite/Nafion/Fe-clay (Figure 4c). This means, in terms
of reaction rate kinetics, the Pd system exhibited the fastest
HER activity, while the Fe-clay electrode showed the least,
supporting the information obtained from the LSV analysis.
However, this also means a higher corrosion resistance for the
Fe-clay electrode than for the Pd or Ag composite system. In a
similar vein, the results of the polarization resistance analysis
obtained from the Nyquist plot are listed in Figure 4d.
Typically, the higher the polarization resistance, the greater the
resistance to charge transfer, implying a higher corrosion
resistance. As revealed in Figure 4d, the order of increasing
polarization resistance was graphite/Nafion/Pd < graphite/
Nafion/Fe-clay/Ag < graphite/Nafion/Fe-clay, further supporting the Tafel analysis reports.

Current at Constant Applied Potential (Bubble Effects)

To make an industrial application feasible, the behavior of the
current at a constant applied potential should be evaluated.
Figure 5a−d shows the chronoamperometric analysis (CAA)
of the graphite/Nafion/Fe-clay/Ag electrode at −0.55 and
−2.30 V vs RHE. These potential values were chosen due to
their capability to generate bubbles during HER activity. CAA
is a powerful tool which enables elucidation of current stability
or kinetic reaction information. Thus, it reveals bubble effects
on the stability of the current or its dynamics. For example, a
reaction with a faster rate produces gas bubbles which have a
negligible time of residence at the electrode surface, whereas a
slow kinetic reaction rate tends to produce bubbles with
significant residence time. The latter stays longer at the
electrode’s surface from its adsorption point, through growth
and finally desorption. This causes the current to produce a
zigzag wave-like signal. The larger the space between two wave

CONCLUSION
In this work, bentonite natural clay (BC) was successfully
modified with Al and Fe, resulting in Al- and Fe-modified BCs
with improved textural and thermal stability properties. The
shift in basal spacing confirmed the success of the modification
process. Enhancement in the BET surface area upon
calcination, along with a decrease in pore size and an increase
in pore volume, resulted in the more exposure of the Fe-BC
active sites and the formation of loosely packed clay layers,
thereby facilitating the mass transfer of reactants during
hydrogen production. After lacing with Ag, the performance of
the Fe-clay material improved significantly (about 21%
increase in hydrogen production and 134% increase in TOF
at −0.60 V applied potential vs RHE). The Ag@Fe-Clay
composite exhibited the same current density of 274 mA/cm2
as that of the commercial Pd black catalysts at −1.23 V vs
