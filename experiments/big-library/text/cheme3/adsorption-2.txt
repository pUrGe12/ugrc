1. Introduction
The continuous rise in atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2)
concentration remains one of the most critical environmental
challenges associated with anthropogenic climate change.
Atmospheric CO2 levels have increased from the pre-industrial
value of approximately 280 ppm to 422.6 ppm in 2024,1,2
driven primarily by fossil-fuel combustion and emissions from
carbon-intensive industries, including cement, steel, and power
generation.3,4 This persistent increase contributes substantially

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are among the most
promising classes of porous solid adsorbents because of their
high specic surface areas, tunable pore architectures, and
chemically adjustable internal surfaces.6 Aluminum-based
MOFs have received increasing attention for gas adsorption
and separation owing to their relatively high thermal and
hydrothermal stability, low metal toxicity, and strong metal–
oxygen coordination environments.7–10 Among them, MIL100(Al) and MIL-110(Al), both constructed from aluminum
nodes and trimesate-based linkers, are attractive candidates
because they possess distinct pore environments and complementary structural features. MIL-100(Al) contains large mesoporous cages accessible through microporous windows,
whereas MIL-110(Al) exhibits one-dimensional channel-type
porosity. These structural characteristics can provide diverse
adsorption domains and diﬀusion pathways for gas molecules.
Nevertheless, the practical application of pristine MOFs
remains constrained by several limitations, including high
synthesis costs, limited mechanical robustness, crystallite
agglomeration, diﬀusion resistance in bulk powder form, and
challenges associated with shaping and scale-up.8,11 These
limitations have motivated the development of MOF-based
hybrid materials in which crystalline frameworks are integrated with low-cost, sustainable, and structurally complementary supports.12
Biochar has emerged as an attractive carbonaceous support
for porous adsorbents because its pore structure and oxygencontaining surface groups can inuence adsorbate-accessible
texture and local polarity. When a MOF is crystallized in the
presence of biochar, the carbon surface may alter precursor
distribution, nucleation environments and crystallite aggregation, and wider pores or interparticle voids may provide access
pathways to narrower adsorption domains.13–17 These possibilities are general design hypotheses. Faster molecular transport,
reduced diﬀusion resistance and improved operational stability
in the present materials cannot be concluded without timeresolved uptake, breakthrough, cycling and humid-gas
measurements.
The combination of an aluminum trimesate framework with
activated biochar may alter crystallite distribution, pore
connectivity and the chemical heterogeneity of the accessible
surface. Oxygen-containing carbon functionalities can create
polar adsorption environments, while coordinatively unsaturated or polar Al-containing sites may interact with CO2. Such
interactions are chemically plausible because the quadrupole of
CO2 responds to local electrostatic elds; nevertheless, their
occurrence in the present composites is not directly demonstrated by FTIR, equilibrium adsorption or CO2-TPD. Previous
studies of MIL-100 frameworks and functionalized carbon
materials show that local metal coordination, ultramicropore
connement and oxygen-containing groups can inuence CO2
adsorption.18,19 When MOFs are grown in situ on a biochar
matrix, the carbon scaﬀold can provide heterogeneous nucleation sites, suppress excessive crystal agglomeration, and
improve the dispersion of MOF crystallites across a porous
support. Studies of Cu-BTC/aminated graphite oxide composites demonstrate that combining a MOF with a chemically

modied carbon component can alter CO2 adsorption performance.20 The contribution of analogous interactions in the
present biochar hybrids remains to be established.
Beyond improving structural stability, the interfacial chemistry between MOFs and biochar can signicantly inuence
adsorption performance by regulating crystallite dispersion,
pore connectivity, and the accessibility of coordinatively
unsaturated metal sites. Simultaneously, oxygen-containing
functional groups on activated biochar provide heterogeneous
adsorption environments that promote favorable interactions
with CO2 through Lewis acid–base and dipole–quadrupole
interactions. Consequently, rational interfacial engineering has
emerged as an eﬀective strategy for improving adsorption eﬃciency without relying solely on increasing the specic surface
area. Previous studies have demonstrated the potential of MOF/
biochar composites for environmental adsorption and CO2related applications, e.g. in situ grown NH2-MIL-101/biochar
and MIL-100(Fe)/biochar systems for adsorption applications,
as well as CuBTC- and Mg-MOF-based biochar composites
investigated for CO2 adsorption and regeneration behavior.21–25
However, these reported systems have predominantly relied on
the deposition or growth of a single MOF phase on a carbonaceous support. Consequently, the role of phase coexistence in
regulating the structure and adsorption-relevant surface
chemistry of MOF/biochar hybrids remains insuﬃciently
understood. Although MIL-100(Al) and MIL-110(Al) are both
aluminum trimesate frameworks, they exhibit distinct pore
architectures: MIL-100(Al) provides cage-type environments,
while MIL-110(Al) oﬀers one-dimensional channel-type
porosity. Their simultaneous integration within a biomassderived carbon scaﬀold is therefore attractive because it may
combine complementary pore geometries with biochar-derived
interfacial functionality. Nevertheless, the deliberate construction of dual-phase MIL-100(Al)/MIL-110(Al)/biochar hybrids has
not been systematically investigated. More importantly, the
inuence of biochar loading on the interfacial architecture,
pore accessibility, heterogeneous adsorption-site distribution,
and equilibrium CO2 adsorption behavior remains poorly
understood. Establishing quantitative relationships between
biochar loading, framework evolution, textural properties, and
CO2 adsorption performance is therefore essential for developing sustainable aluminum-based MOF composites with
rationally engineered adsorption characteristics.
In this study, dual-phase MIL-100(Al)/MIL-110(Al)/biochar
hybrids were synthesized through the in situ solvothermal
growth of aluminum trimesate frameworks on activated
bamboo-derived biochar. The inuence of biochar loading on
crystal structure, morphology, textural properties, thermal
stability, CO2-interaction-site distribution, and equilibrium CO2
adsorption behavior was systematically investigated. Particular
attention was devoted to elucidating how the activated biochar
scaﬀold regulates crystallite dispersion, accessible microporosity, heterogeneous adsorption sites, and interfacial interactions governing CO2 adsorption. By establishing a quantitative
structure–property relationship between biochar loading and
adsorption performance, this work provides a sustainable
materials-design strategy for engineering dual-phase

2.

Materials and methods

2.1

Materials

All chemicals and reagents were purchased from commercial
suppliers and used without further purication unless otherwise stated. Aluminum nitrate nonahydrate (Al(NO3)3$9H2O,
98%; Merck & Co., Inc., USA) was used as the aluminum
precursor,
while
trimethyl
1,3,5-benzenetricarboxylate
(Me3BTC, 98%; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was used as the ester
precursor of the trimesate linker for aluminum trimesate
framework formation. Deionized (DI) water and nitric acid
(HNO3, 65%; RCI Labscan, Thailand) were employed as the
reaction medium and acid modulator, respectively. Bambooderived biochar (98%) was obtained from Thai Carbon &
Graphite Co., Ltd. Potassium hydroxide (KOH, 85%; KemAus,
Australia) was used as the chemical activating agent to enhance
the porosity and surface functionality of the biochar scaﬀold.
2.2

Preparation methods

2.2.1 Activation of bamboo-derived biochar. Raw bambooderived biochar was chemically activated using KOH to enhance
its porous structure and surface functionality. Briey, the biochar was impregnated with KOH at a KOH : biochar mass ratio
of 1 : 2. The impregnated sample was then transferred to
a tubular furnace and heated to 600 °C at a ramping rate of 5 °
C min−1 under a continuous nitrogen ow of 20 L h−1. The
activation temperature was maintained for 2 h to promote pore
development within the carbon matrix. Aer cooling to room
temperature under nitrogen, the activated product was thoroughly washed with deionized water until the ltrate reached
neutral pH to remove residual KOH and soluble inorganic
species. The washed solid was subsequently dried at 100 °C for
24 h. The resulting activated bamboo-derived biochar was
denoted as A-BC.
2.2.2 Preparation
of
biochar/MIL-100(Al)/MIL-110(Al)
composites. The biochar/MIL-100(Al)/MIL-110(Al) composites,
denoted as BC-n, where n represents the nominal biochar
content, were synthesized through a solvothermal route designed
to promote the in situ growth of aluminum trimesate frameworks
on activated bamboo-derived biochar. In a typical synthesis,
Al(NO3)3$9H2O, 0.230 g, 0.613 mmol, and Me3BTC, 0.104 g,
0.413 mmol, were dissolved in a mixed solvent containing 2.8 mL
of deionized water and 0.77 mL of 1 M HNO3. Under acidic
hydrothermal conditions, Me3BTC is expected to undergo in situ
hydrolysis, generating trimesate species that coordinate with Al3+
centers to form MIL-100(Al)/MIL-110(Al) frameworks. Excess
aluminum precursor was used to maintain suﬃcient Al3+ availability for framework nucleation, particularly because oxygencontaining functional groups on the biochar surface may
partially coordinate Al3+ during the early stages of crystallization.
For the preparation of biochar-containing composites, activated bamboo-derived biochar (A-BC) was added to the
precursor solution during the initial sonication step. The A-BC

crystalline structure and phase evolution of activated bambooderived biochar (A-BC), the pristine mixed-phase MOF (BC-0),
and the corresponding biochar-containing hybrids (BC-10 and

aluminum trimesate frameworks may partially overlap, particularly in the low-angle region. Their positions and relative

enables the formation of dual-phase MIL-100(Al)/MIL-110(Al)/
biochar hybrids without substantial disruption of the primary
crystalline framework. Biochar incorporation is associated with
changes in particle distribution and aggregation. Its surface
may inuence precursor dispersion or provide heterogeneous
nucleation environments, although a crystallization-directing
role has not been established by experimentation.

3.7 Proposed interfacial mechanism for CO2-interaction
behavior
Fig. 8 illustrates a proposed interfacial mechanism for the
formation and CO2-interaction behaviour of the MIL-100(Al)/
MIL-110(Al)/biochar hybrids, based on the combined results
of PXRD, FTIR, SEM, textural, TGA and CO2-TPD. The adsorption trends may reect diﬀerences in accessible pore environments72 and framework or carbon surface chemistry. Acid sites
characterized in MIL-100(Al) provide context for possible polar
adsorption environments,73 but do not identify the CO2-binding
sites in these composites. Wider access pathways are plausible
in hierarchical porous materials.75 These proposed contributions remain non-unique and do not establish an interfacespecic mechanism. Hence, the activated bamboo-derived biochar scaﬀold is proposed to impact the solvothermal assembly
of the aluminium trimesate frameworks by oﬀering a heterogeneous carbonaceous surface with oxygenated functional groups.
FTIR analysis conrmed the presence of hydroxyl- and carboxylcontaining functionalities on A-BC, and SEM observations
revealed that the incorporation of biochar aﬀected the spatial
distribution and aggregation behaviour of the MOF crystallites.

Paper
These ndings are consistent with the biochar surface acting as
a scaﬀold to support nucleation during framework growth,
which facilitates the more dispersed generation of MIL-100(Al)/
MIL-110(Al) crystallites throughout the carbon matrix.37,40–43 It is
suggested that the adsorption mechanism is due to a combination of micropore lling and heterogeneous surface interaction. The high CO2 uptake at very low relative pressure conrms
that the initial adsorption process is dominated by the micropore lling in ultra-micropores, where the overlapping adsorption potentials generate strong connement eﬀects for the CO2
molecules. At the same time, coordinatively unsaturated
aluminium centres in the MIL framework serve as Lewis acid
sites, while hydroxyl, carboxyl and phenolic groups on the
activated biochar provide polar adsorption sites that can
interact with the quadrupole moment of CO2 via weak Lewis
acid–base and dipole–quadrupole interactions.57–61 The good
tting of the Freundlich isotherm model is also evidence of the
adsorption on heterogeneous energetic surfaces due to the
synergistic integration of the aluminium based framework and
the activated biochar.
The results of CO2-TPD and equilibrium adsorption agree
with each other in determining the optimum composition of
BC-10. The maximum total CO2-interaction-site density below
400 °C (6.827 mmol g−1) and the highest equilibrium CO2adsorption capacity (39.59 cm3 STP per g, 1.77 mmol g−1) were
observed for the BC-10 sample, suggesting that moderate biochar loading optimises the accessibility of the adsorption sites
while preserving the intrinsic porosity of the dual-phase
framework. BC-20, in contrast, possessed a lower BET surface
area (381.23 m2 g−1), lower CO2-interaction-site density
(4.984 mmol g−1), and lower equilibrium adsorption capacity
(30.77 cm3 STP per g, 1.37 mmol g−1). These results suggest that
over loading biochar partially masks micropores derived from
the MOF, restricts the accessibility of the pores and reduces the
amount of accessible adsorption sites without majorly changing
the primary aluminum–trimesate coordination framework. The
proposed mechanism shows that the CO2-interaction behaviour
of the dual-phase MIL-100(Al)/MIL-110(Al)/biochar hybrids are
governed by a biochar-loading-dependent structure–property
relationship. A moderate amount of biochar (10 wt%) improves
the dispersion of crystallites, retains accessible microporosity
and increases the density of heterogeneous adsorption sites,
resulting in the highest CO2 uptake of the materials investigated. In contrast, the high loading of biochar reduces the pore
accessibility and partially masks the adsorption sites from the
framework. These results suggest that it is the engineering of
the interface rather than the increase of surface area that
governs the CO2 adsorption performance in the present dualphase aluminum-based MOF/biochar system.

The comparison further highlights that adsorption performance depends strongly on framework composition, pore
architecture, activation strategy, adsorption temperature, pressure, and surface chemistry. Several reported MOF-based
adsorbents achieve higher CO2 capacities through highsurface-area frameworks, open metal sites, bimetallic nodes,
or post-synthetic functionalization. In contrast, the present
work demonstrates a simple biochar-assisted interfacialengineering strategy for tuning adsorption-site accessibility in
aluminum-based dual-phase MOF hybrids. Therefore, the main
contribution of this study lies not in achieving the highest
absolute CO2 uptake, but in establishing how controlled biochar incorporation regulates framework dispersion, accessible
microporosity, heterogeneous adsorption-site distribution, and
equilibrium CO2 adsorption behavior.
Overall, the principal contribution of this work is the
establishment of a biochar-loading-dependent structure–property relationship in dual-phase aluminum trimesate–biochar
hybrids. Moderate biochar incorporation suppresses particle
agglomeration, promotes a more dispersed hybrid morphology,
preserves accessible microporosity, and introduces additional
oxygen-containing surface functionalities. These combined
eﬀects generate energetically heterogeneous adsorption environments, as reected by the Freundlich model tting and
enhanced CO2-TPD response of BC-10. In contrast, excessive
biochar loading partially restricts pore accessibility and masks
framework-derived adsorption sites, resulting in lower CO2
uptake. These ndings provide design guidance for biomassderived MOF hybrid adsorbents in which interfacial

4. Conclusion
Activated bamboo-derived biochar was incorporated during the
in situ synthesis of aluminum trimesate–biochar hybrids to
examine how nominal biochar addition aﬀects morphology,
accessible porosity and equilibrium CO2 adsorption. PXRD
features are consistent with the coexistence of phases assigned
to MIL-100(Al) and MIL-110(Al), while microscopy reveals
composition-dependent morphological diﬀerences. These
observations do not establish a crystallization-directing role for
biochar or a crystallographically integrated dual-phase interface. BC-10 and BC-20 denote precursor-based synthesis
formulations; their nal biochar mass fractions remain
unquantied.
BC-10 was the best-performing composition among the
investigated samples, exhibiting an equilibrium CO2 uptake of
39.59 cm3 STP per g (approximately 1.77 mmol g−1), approximately 52% higher than BC-0, despite its lower BET surface area
of 496.79 m2 g−1. This comparison indicates that BET surface
area alone does not explain the observed uptake trend. The
lower uptake of BC-20 may reect dilution, changes in framework formation or reduced pore accessibility; these contributions cannot be separated from the available measurements.
Freundlich tting provides a better statistical description than
Langmuir tting, although neither model fully represents BC10. This preference is consistent with a heterogeneous adsorption response but does not identify its molecular origin. CO2TPD is interpreted as a protocol-dependent desorption response
rather than an absolute adsorption-site density, and TGA
characterizes thermal decomposition under N2 rather than
operational stability.
The study identies descriptive composition-dependent
adsorption trends rather than a statistically validated structure–property relationship or demonstrated interfacial synergism. Without biochar-only CO2 adsorption, a compositionmatched physical mixture and independent replicates, the
