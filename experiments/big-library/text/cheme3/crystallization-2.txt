1. INTRODUCTION
Gallium nitride (GaN) is a cornerstone material in the field of
compound semiconductors.1,2 With its remarkable properties,
including high electron mobility (1300 cm2/(V·s)), high
electron saturation velocity (2.5 × 107 cm/s), excellent thermal
conductivity (1.3 W/(cm·K)), and a wide bandgap (3.4 eV) that
ensures superior resistance to high temperatures and high
breakdown fields (3.3 MV/cm), GaN has emerged as a “star
technology” in applications such as power delivery fast charging,
electric vehicles, and solar photovoltaics.3−5 These exceptional
characteristics position GaN as a promising alternative to
silicon-based semiconductors. As a result, it is increasingly
regarded as one of the most promising technological directions
in the field of power electronics.6,7 Currently, commercial GaN
crystals are predominantly grown using the Hydride Vapor
Phase Epitaxy (HVPE) method.8,9 However, the significant
lattice and thermal mismatch between the heteroepitaxial
substrate and GaN often leads to high density dislocations in
the GaN epitaxial layer, with densities typically ranging from 108

They delineated the boundaries for GaN crystal formation

crystal morphology evolution. Kawamura et al.29 developed a
temperature-dependent metastable for crystal growth by
quantitatively evaluating the nitrogen concentration and
supersaturation in Ga−Na melts, thereby defining the kinetic
boundaries for inhibiting spontaneous polycrystalline nucleation. These preliminary studies provide an important reference
for GaN crystal growth. Based on the quantitative evaluation of
nitrogen solubility and supersaturation, the optimal temperature−pressure (T-P) window for stable GaN epitaxial growth is
determined to be 1073−1133 K and 3−5 MPa. Within this
range, the supersaturation remains inside the metastable region,
effectively suppressing spontaneous polycrystalline nucleation
while maintaining sufficient driving force for single-crystal
growth. Therefore, defining this quantitative T-P window is
essential for achieving high-quality GaN epitaxy. To this end, the
present study aims to delineate the optimal processing regime,
defined as the T-P window in which the dissolved nitrogen
concentration in the Ga−Na melt is sufficient to sustain
continuous single-crystal growth while remaining below the
critical threshold for spontaneous polycrystalline nucleation.
This study establishes a multiscale approach coupling
macroscopic finite element analysis (FEA) with molecular
dynamics (MD) simulations. By cross-validating our simulation
results with reported experimental data, we have precisely
reconstructed the T-P phase diagram, thereby delineating the
critical thermodynamic windows for synthesizing high-quality
GaN crystals via the Na-flux method. These findings not only
deepen the fundamental understanding of GaN crystallization
kinetics but also hold promise for addressing critical technical
bottlenecks in the growth of large-size, low-defect single crystalssuch as parasitic polycrystalline nucleation and the precise
control of growth conditions. By providing quantitative
theoretical guidance on optimal T-P parameters, this work lays
a robust foundation for suppressing spontaneous nucleation,
stabilizing epitaxial growth, and ultimately facilitating the
growth of high-quality GaN crystals.

GaN crystal growth is carried out using the Na-flux liquid phase epitaxy
(LPE) method. The commercial GaN single crystals of size 10 × 10.5
mm2 are used as seeds. Starting materials, including metal Ga
(99.9999%), Na (99.5%), and C (99.85%), are mixed in a molar ratio
of Ga: Na: C = 27:73:0.5 (mol %) and placed into a boron nitride (BN)
crucible, ensuring uniform mixing.24 The addition of carbon (C) is
intended to suppress the formation of polycrystalline GaN on the
surface of the Ga−Na solution.30,31 The crucible is then rapidly
transferred to a high-pressure, high-temperature reaction vessel, which
is placed into an electric furnace.
During crystal growth, temperature induce thermal convection in the
solution, while concentration gradients lead to diffusion, both of which
influence the transport of nitrogen ions. Furthermore, material
transport affects the temperature distribution. Steady-state numerical
analysis is conducted on the heat transfer, thermal convection, and
nitrogen diffusion interactions in the solution. As shown in Figure 1, the
computational model is established based on a laboratory-fabricated
apparatus, with dimensions consistent with those employed in the
experiments.

originates from the critical role of Na at the gas−liquid interface:
at elevated temperatures, Na effectively ionizes N2 molecules,
significantly enhancing the equilibrium solubility of nitrogen in
the Ga−Na melt. Since supersaturation is defined as the
difference between the actual solute concentration and the
equilibrium solubility, the thermodynamic driving force (supersaturation) required to sustain GaN epitaxial growth decreases
continuously as temperature rises and solubility increases,
approaching the thermodynamic equilibrium limit in the hightemperature range of 1123−1173 K. Concurrently, elevated
temperatures strengthen nitrogen diffusion and interfacial
atomic mobility, further reducing the dependence on high
supersaturation from a kinetic perspective. At a constant
temperature, supersaturation increases markedly with rising
nitrogen pressure, a trend that strictly follows Sieverts’ law. The
solubility of nitrogen in the Ga−Na solution is proportional to
the square root of the nitrogen partial pressure; increasing
pressure promotes N2 dissolution, thereby elevating the
equilibrium solubility of nitrogen in the melt. To overcome
the interfacial energy barrier and drive epitaxial growth, the
system requires a higher absolute solute concentration, leading
to a corresponding increase in the supersaturation threshold to
compensate for the enhanced thermodynamic stability of
dissolved nitrogen under high pressure.

This range closely resembles the metastable region for GaN
crystal growth investigated by Kawamura et al.,29 mapping the
metastable region by calculating the change in nitrogen
solubility under various temperature and pressure conditions.
Their finding that the metastable region expands with increasing
growth temperature is consistent with our calculations.
Concurrently, we delineate the high-quality crystallization
zone based on the variation in supersaturation across different
temperature and pressure values, which offers a more practical
framework for adjusting the required temperature and pressure
settings in experimental procedures.
Experimental data points reported in various literature
sources are overlaid on the diagram to validate the phase
diagram. The data (such as the red star points from ref 29) is
concentrated within the intermediate region between the two
curves, which is essentially consistent with the reported results
for Na-flux GaN crystal growth under the aforementioned
conditions. The yellow data point at 1023 K, 5 MPa (ref 48), is
located above the spontaneous nucleation curve, indicating a
high propensity for polycrystal formation under these
conditions. This is consistent with the GaN crystal morphology
published in the corresponding literature. At this temperature,
Zhou et al.49 investigated GaN crystal growth at 7 MPa (pink
data point), and their study demonstrated the possibility of
growing hexagonal pyramidal GaN crystals, although the crystal
surfaces were still covered by polycrystals. In the hightemperature regime, Tandryo et al.50 comparatively studied
the effects of 1143 and 1173 K on crystal growth at 4 MPa (light
blue data points). Their findings revealed that at 1173 K and 4
MPa, no polycrystal formation is observed on the GaN crystals.
Concurrently, Wu et al.20 investigated the influence of varying
temperatures 973 to 1123 K on the GaN crystal growth
morphology at 4 MPa (red triangle data points), discovering that
as the temperature increased, the crystal size gradually enlarged,
and the content of polycrystals on the crystal surface
progressively decreased. Furthermore, Wang et al.13 also
investigated GaN crystal growth under different temperature
and pressure conditions, concluding that the quality of GaN
crystals exhibits a positive correlation with temperature and a
negative correlation with pressure. These collective research
findings substantially validate the accuracy of our calculated
results.

4. SUMMARY
In this study, a synergistic multiscale framework coupling
macroscopic FEA and MD simulations was established to
systematically investigate the crystallization kinetics of GaN
grown via the Na-flux method. By cross-validating the
theoretical model with reported experimental data from the
literature, the synergistic regulation mechanisms of T-P were
elucidated. The main conclusions are as follows:
(1) Construction of the T-P Phase Diagram: A precise T-P
phase diagram based on dynamic supersaturation was
successfully constructed for the range of 973−1173 K and
1−7 MPa. The exceptional agreement between our
theoretical predictions and existing literature experimental results strongly validates the accuracy of this multiscale
model.
(2) Delineation of the High-Quality Growth Window: The
thermodynamic boundaries for GaN crystallization were
explicitly defined. A critical “high-quality growth window”
was identified, which is strictly bounded by the minimum
thermodynamic requirement for stable epitaxial growth
(the lower bound) and the critical threshold for parasitic
spontaneous polycrystalline nucleation (the upper
bound).
(3) Expansion of the Growth Window under High Temperature and Pressure: The study reveals that the high-quality
growth window significantly expands as temperature and
pressure increase. This phenomenon perfectly aligns with
the fundamental thermodynamic principles of GaN
crystal growth: elevated temperatures substantially
enhance nitrogen solubility in the Na−Ga melt; meanwhile, as nitrogen pressure increases, a greater number of

physical insights and quantitative theoretical frameworks for
process parameter optimization. By uncovering the critical
thermodynamic boundaries and growth window expansion
mechanisms, this study establishes a robust basis for the
mitigation of spontaneous nucleation and the stable growth of
high-quality GaN single crystals.
