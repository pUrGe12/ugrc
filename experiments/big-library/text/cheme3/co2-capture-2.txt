ABSTRACT: This research discusses a novel approach to reduce
the demanding regeneration phase in the carbon dioxide
adsorption process. Through the employment of the magnetic
induction swing adsorption technology, in situ heating adsorbents
were developed, which were responsive to the induced magnetic
field. The novel material, superparamagnetic Fe3O4@Mg-MOF-74,
was synthesized via a layer-by-layer approach that integrates
iron(III) oxide (Fe3O4) as its magnetic core and Mg-MOF-74 as its
highly porous shell in a defined core−shell structure. Ultrasonication and solvothermal methods were adopted to enhance
nucleation efficiency and achieve control over particle morphology
and phase composition. Morphology analysis using X-ray
diffraction and FTIR confirmed the homogeneous formation,
while transmission electron microscopy imaging revealed a shell morphology. The composite exhibited thermal stability up to 350
°C, along with favorable surface area (261.24 m2/g) and pore volume (0.23 cm3/g), while displaying mesoporous and multilayer N2
adsorption. It is a promising CO2 adsorbent as evaluated in isothermal measurements at 25.00 °C up to 1.20 bar. The Type 1
isotherm plot was revealed, indicative of microporous physisorption. The composite achieved a maximum uptake of 0.60 mmol/g,
which was attributed to the porous Mg-MOF-74. Lastly, magnetic analysis using vibrating sample magnetometer demonstrated
superparamagnetic behavior from the sigmoidal hysteresis curve. Its high magnetic saturation and minute remanence, at 67.95 A·m2/
kg and 1.34 A·m2/kg, respectively, with coercivity at 10.98 G, highlight its excellence responsiveness to magnetic fluctuations, crucial
for magnetic hyperthermia and efficient phase transition. The combined properties in Fe3O4@Mg-MOF-74 make it a multifunctional
adsorbent with strong potential for carbon capture and magnetically assisted regeneration applications.

1. INTRODUCTION
The increasing concentration of carbon dioxide (CO2) in the
atmosphere, mostly fuelled by industrial emission, has driven
global efforts to investigate efficient carbon capture techniques.
Emerging from the long list of candidates, adsorption-based
methods are seen as promising alternatives due to their energy
efficiency, scalability, and tunable material properties. Metal−
organic frameworks (MOFs), particularly Mg-MOF-74, have
garnered significant attention for CO2 capture due to their
high-surface area, strong affinity for CO2 molecules, and open
metal sites.1 However, capturing CO2 from postcombustion
flue gas may pose difficulties because of low partial pressure of
CO2, humid gas flow, and presence of other acidic gases (e.g.,
SOx and NOx).2 Therefore, this highlights the need for
materials of high CO2 selectivity and stability under harsh
conditions. These demands have prompted the integration of
magnetic components to enhance separation efficiency and
recyclability. In this context, the engineering of superparamagnetic Fe3O4@Mg-MOF-74 composites offers a novel
approach through the combination of the high adsorption
capacity of MOFs and magnetic responsiveness of Fe3O4

Gas adsorption system for CO2 capture works by selectively
binding CO2 molecules onto the surface of solid adsorbents,
such as MOFs, under specific conditions. Once saturated, the
sorbents require regeneration to store captured CO2 and
replenish their capacity. Notably, the regeneration phase is
energy-intensive and represents a major operational challenge
in postcombustion capture systems.3 For instance, direct air
capture systems require 0.54 to 18.70 GJ per tonnes of CO2 for
its regeneration phase.4 Meanwhile, total energy consumption

However, its regeneration step remains a bottleneck for largescale installation. Innovative adsorption techniques such as
magnetic induction swing adsorption (MISA) are being
explored to reduce the energy penalty and improve the overall
system efficiency.
MISA is an emerging technique that enhances the energy
efficiency of gas separation processes, particularly during the
regeneration phase of the adsorption process. It is an extension
of conventional temperature swing adsorption but replaces
external heating with in situ thermal activation. MISA employs
composite materials that integrate high-capacity adsorbents
with magnetic nanoparticles, such as iron oxide. When exposed
to an alternating magnetic field (AMF), the magnetic species
generate heat through magnetic hyperthermia, in which
magnetically responsive materials absorb electromagnetic
energy and convert it to thermal energy. This localized heating
enables rapid and uniform desorption of CO2 without the need
for external thermal sources. By minimizing thermal losses and
improving regeneration rates, MISA offers a promising
pathway toward more efficient and sustainable green
technology.
Recent studies have demonstrated the high regeneration
efficiency of magnetic composites under AMF, reinforcing the
potential of MISA as a low-energy desorption alternative.
Under a field strength of 42 mT, complete CO2 desorption was
achieved within 240 s with 100% release efficiency.7
Conversely, MISA systems have reported energy consumption
as low as 1.26−1.29 MJ/kg CO2, almost half of energy
consumed from using conventional adsorbents such as
activated carbon.7
Designing an efficient magnetic framework composite
requires an in-depth understanding of the magnetic behavior
of the material. Key magnetic parameters include magnetic
saturation, remanence, coercivity, and the hysteresis loop,
which altogether describe how a material responds to an
external magnetic field. A hysteresis loop is a visualization of
the parameters, in which it plots magnetization versus
magnetic field strength and reveals the energy loss during
magnetization cycles.8 Magnetic saturation refers to the
maximum magnetization a material can achieve under an
applied field. Conversely, remanence is the residual magnetization retained after the external field is removed, while
coercivity is the intensity of the reverse field required to bring
magnetization back to zero.9 Superparamagnetic nanoparticles,
which are magnets composed of a single magnetic domain, are
ideal due to their ability to rapidly align with the AMF and lose
all magnetization once the field is removed. It eliminates
remanence, thus generating a narrow or almost zero hysteresis,
and enables fast, energy-efficient magnetic hyperthermia.
Heat generation in superparamagnetic materials occurs via
two mechanisms: Néel relaxation, where the magnetic moment
flips direction due to thermal energy, and Brownian relaxation,
involving physical rotation of the entire nanoparticles in
response to the changing field.10 Both mechanisms dissipate
energy as heat, generated through internal friction and viscous
drag, thus triggering the desorption phase in a MISA system.
Achieving superparamagnetic behavior requires precise control
over particle size, crystallinity, and surface functionalization.11

Various strategies were reported for the synthesis of
magnetic framework composites, which mostly fall into five
general approaches. First, is the direct mixing method that
offers simplicity and broad applicability but must compromise
long-term stability due to weak interfacial bonding.12 Whereas,
in situ growth provides better integration of magnetic
nanoparticles within the MOF matrix, thus preserving porosity
and enhancing magnetic responsiveness, though it requires
complicated separation steps. Conversely, embedding method
facilitates a more flexible incorporation of presynthesized
magnetic species with MOF, albeit its potential reductions in
surface area due to pore occupation.13 Then, there is the layerby-layer growth method, which excels in constructing a defined
core−shell structure with tunable shell thickness, which is
advantageous for CO2 adsorption to optimize its capacity.14
Finally, the encapsulation method utilizes polymer interfaces to
promote uniform MOF growth around MNPs to build core−
shell structures with enhanced compatibility and functionalization. Among these, the LbL approach stands out for its control
over reaction kinetics and adaptability, making it a promising
strategy for novel adsorbents in carbon capture technologies.
Despite the potential of MOFs as adsorbents in carbon
capture applications, significant research gaps still shroud the
synthesis and characterization process of core−shell structured
composites, specifically Fe3O4@Mg-MOF-74. While its MOF
counterpart, Mg-MOF-74, is known for its high CO 2
adsorption performance from its open metal sites and large
surface area, an integration with Fe3O4 nanoparticles that form
a core−shell architecture offers magnetic responsiveness,
especially in a superparamagnetic material. Energy requirement
in the regeneration phase can be reduced from the in situ heat
generated from the exposure to induced magnetic field.
However, the synthesis route, structural properties, and
adsorption performance of this composite are not yet fully
understood. This gap is crucial, given the high energy demands
from conventional adsorption technologies. Nevertheless,
physicochemical properties and CO2 adsorption efficiency of
the newfound material require systematic investigation to
validate their feasibility in carbon capture solutions.

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Successful formation of Fe3O4@Mg-MOF-74 composites was
first determined through XRD analysis, which also revealed the
crystal size of the structure. Figure 3 shows the diffractograms
of iron(III) oxide nanoparticles and Fe3O4@Mg-MOF-74
composites. Overlapping identity peaks of iron(III) oxide at
30°, 35°, 43°, 53°, 57°, and 63°, identical to JCPDS file No.
98-003-5001, show presence of magnetic species in both
materials. Admittedly, the identity peaks for Mg-MOF-74 are
relatively minute compared to iron(III) oxide’s peaks.
Characteristic peaks for Mg-MOF-74 were observed at 6.7°,
11.7°, 18°, 71°, and 74°, albeit subdued compared to the
magnetic counterparts. The peaks obtained from the
composite are 6.7°, 11.7°, 18°, 30°, 35°, 43°, 53°, 63°, 71°,
and 74°.
It was revealed that iron(III) oxide peaks were present in
both XRD graphs, thus indicating its presence in both
materials. Hootifard et al. also reported overlapping peaks
from both iron(III) oxide and its MOF phase in their work for
the synthesis of Fe3O4@iron-based MOF composites.15 The
group also reported a reduction in crystalline size upon
formation, which was noted due to interfacial strain and
confinement effects within the organic framework. Evidently,
this situation is aligned with the current work on the synthesis
of Fe3O4@Mg-MOF-74.
The average crystalline size as calculated using Scherrer’s
equation for iron(III) oxide was 226.89 Å, which diminished to
168 Å after being encapsulated by Mg-MOF-74. Notably, the
diminished size of the composite might be due to the
interaction between the two material classes.16 When the
mixture was exposed to ultrasonication waves during the
synthesis process, the electrostatic bond was formed between
oxygen atoms of iron(III) oxide and Mg2+ ions of magnesium
hydrates.17
To further determine the successful integration between
iron(III) oxide and Mg-MOF-74 precursors, EDX was used to
quantify the presence of Mg element in the composite. Figure
4 shows the EDX graph generated from the composite which
reports the presence of Mg element, thus confirming formation
of the Mg-MOF-74 framework, together with Fe element from
iron(III) oxide. Thorough washing cycle using DMF and
ethanol had reduced the probability of unreacted magnesium
hydrates. Additionally, magnetic separation also ensures that
the collected yield is magnetized.
The distribution of the elements in the composite was
visualized through comparison between electron scanning and
EDX color mapping in Figure 5a,b. With the overall color
looking green, it represents O element from the ligands that
bridged the magnesium ions and its organic linker, 2,5dihydroxyterephthalic acid. Red dots that represent carbon are
visible throughout the map as part of the organic linker in the
benzene ring and carboxylate group. Yellowish hue of Fe

The internal architecture of Fe3O4@Mg-MOF-74 was
processed using TEM, as shown in Figure 8. In this figure, a
lighter outer shell can be seen surrounding a darker core. Clear
distinction between these two regions is observable from their
contrast difference with iron(III) oxide being the core while
Mg-MOF-74 being the shell. This deduction is supported with
the invisibility of Fe element in EDX color mapping and the
assumption that Mg-MOF-74 formed on the surface of
iron(III) oxide during solvothermal synthesis. Additionally, it
can be established that the composite has iron(III) oxide as its

heavier density at 5.17 g/cm3, compared to the density of MgMOF-74 at 0.88 g/cm3.19
Correlating with the results obtained from XRD analysis and
the internal structure of Fe3O4@Mg-MOF-74, the diminished
MOF peaks can be ascribed to the thin framework that grew
around the core. This is consistent with previous report,
especially one conducted by Li et al., on the epitaxial synthesis
of Fe3O4@Mg-MOF-74 on the iron(III) oxide core.16 The
team observed weak MOF peaks relative to the defined peaks
of its core. It was deduced that the diminished peak intensity
was attributed to the dominant magnetic phase and thin MOF
shell.
With the iron(III) oxide as its core, homogeneous heat
generation is to be expected from the composite.20 While the
porous Mg-MOF-74 shell acts as the carbon dioxide
adsorption sites. Hence, it is confirmed that the composites
synthesized through the one-pot solvothermal method is a
core−shell Fe3O4@Mg-MOF-74 with an iron(III) oxide core
and MOF shell.

stability and component composition of the Fe3O4@Mg-MOF74. As shown, it revealed an initial slight increase in mass up to
∼100 °C due to surface oxidation. A plateau followed between

composite as the magnetic species retained its structural and
magnetic integrity.

4. CONCLUSION
The successful synthesis of Fe3O4@Mg-MOF-74 demonstrates
a well-integrated core−shell architecture, with magnetite
(Fe3O4) serving as the magnetic core and Mg-MOF-74
forming the porous shell. Structural analyses via XRD and
FTIR confirm the compositional homogeneity of the
composite, as evidenced by the distinct presence of both Mg
and Fe signatures. TEM further reveals the core−shell
morphology with a lighter contrast band clearly delineating
the MOF shell. Magnetic characterization yielded parameters
of 67.95 A·m2/kg (saturation magnetization), 1.34 A·m2/kg
(remanence), and 1.098 mT (coercivity), while the absence of
a hysteresis loop and the sigmoidal magnetization curve
confirm the material’s superparamagnetic behavior�making it
highly responsive to magnetization and demagnetization, a key
feature for magnetic hyperthermia and energy-efficient
