As lithium-ion battery (LIB) active material and cell manufacturing costs continue to drop with wider adoption of
electric vehicles, electrode and cell processing costs remain too high in terms of reaching the ultimate U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE) cell cost target of $80/kWh. This paper primarily covers major materials chemistry
advancements made over the last 10 years at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) in the space of advanced
manufacturing science for LIBs with the aim of simultaneously meeting the ultimate cost target, 500 Wh/kg
gravimetric energy density, 10-15-min fast charge times, and 1000 deep discharge cycles. Aqueous electrode
processing with a variety of active anode and cathode materials is now a standard procedure at the DOE Battery
Manufacturing R&D Facility at ORNL (BMF), including the latest processes developed for Ni-rich cathodes. New
results on cobalt-free LiNi0.8Fe0.1Al0.1O2 (NFA 811) are also included and discussed in an electrode processing
advantage context. In addition, colloidal processing advancements have been made for Si/C composite anodes for
achieving >600 mA h/g capacities. Optimization of electrode coating parameters and drying protocols have been
completed, which has elucidated how key processing variables need to be changed when parameters such as
slurry solids loading, solvent type, and wet electrode thickness are changed. ORNL has also increased the line
speeds at which thick cathodes can be processed using ultra-fast electron beam (EB) curing and substantially
decreased formation cycling times to <1 day. Key details of these advancements are discussed in the context of
materials chemistry and process-property-performance relationships.

1. Introduction
Despite the many recent advances in lithium-ion battery (LIB) active
materials, electrode design, energy density, and cell design, key
manufacturing challenges remain in order to lower the cost of cells for
widespread transportation and grid storage commercialization [1,2]. The
major steps that contribute to excessive manufacturing costs are relatively low coating line speeds (up to 100-200 ft/min), drying (solidiﬁcation) of the electrode coating, electrolyte ﬁlling and wetting, and
formation of the anode solid electrolyte interface (SEI) and cathode
electrolytbib1e interface (CEI) layers [3,4]. The relationship between
materials chemistry and processing plays a major role in completing
these steps at low cost while achieving high-quality cells with low scrap
rate. Optimal colloidal chemistry and dispersion mixing leads to LIB
electrodes with good distribution of the active materials, conductive
additive, and polymer binder, as well as better deposition of the

dispersion onto the current collectors. Proper primary drying and solidiﬁcation of the dispersion are also critical to achieving electrode architectures with high energy and power densities. The higher the line speed
of the coating process, the more difﬁcult obtaining uniform electrode
architectures becomes, as coating defects become more prominent such
as streaking, pinholes, agglomeration, etc. [5,6] Once high-quality electrodes are produced, they must also be assembled into pouch or cylindrical cells with care and formed under time consuming cycling
conditions [2,7].
If electrodes can be produced with alternative solvent-free methods
such as UV or electron beam (EB) curing, line speeds could be substantially increased to 500-1000 ft/min, which would dramatically cut
operating and capital costs. In order to reach these line speeds with
current conventional solvent (NMP, water, isopropyl alcohol, etc.) based
processing, extremely long drying lines are required, which are prohibitive from a capital equipment standpoint. Miltec UV International

There are signiﬁcant challenges for both active materials
manufacturing and electrode processing that are prohibiting high line
speeds and low cell cost. The current ultimate U.S. Department of Energy
(DOE) Vehicle Technologies Ofﬁce (VTO) cell cost target is $80/kWh,
and current manufacturing costs are about double this ﬁgure [14].

material costs according to a basic analysis using the Argonne National

The main issue associated with processing NMC 811 in water is Liþ/
Hþ exchange, which can modify the surface morphology and produce a
highly basic solvent pH. Previous studies have shown that lithiumcontaining transition metal oxides generate basic solutions when
exposed to water [24–27]. This is of particular concern for cathode
slurries, since a high pH can corrode the aluminum foil current collector
and lead to lower capacities [28–30]. This pH rise is due to Liþ/Hþ exchange between the metal oxide and water, which generates LiOH by the
following equation:

Overall, this work clearly demonstrates that aqueous processing is
compatible with Ni-rich NMC 811, which will simultaneously increase
the energy density and decrease the cost of Li-ion batteries. However,
Fig. 4b clearly suggests there are two different methods of capacity fade
for the NMP processed baseline and the water based NMC 811 cathodes.
In addition, it has recently been reported that the rate capability is about
10–15% lower at higher discharge rates (2-3 C) for the water processed
NMC 811 [21]. As for the former issue, it is thought that the slightly
lower water-processed capacity retention could be due to the formation
protocol used, which was the same for both cases, and the NMC 811 CEI
formed after water processing. For example, due to the alteration of the Li
surface chemistry after water exposure, a more ionically resistive NMC
811 surface may exist. Furthermore, it is possible that the NMC 811 after
aqueous processing is more susceptible to transition metal dissolution
over long cell cycling times, which could result in gradual poisoning of
the anode SEI via transition-metal crossover. Protective thin-ﬁlm coatings such as metal oxides, carbon, LiPON, etc. could alleviate this issue.
With respect to the slightly lower rate capability at higher discharge
rates, this issue could also be due a more ionically resistive anode SEI and
CEI or inferior carbon-binder network distribution causing an electronic
resistance increase. These issues could be solved with further optimization of the formulation and mixing protocols used for aqueous processing
and modiﬁcations to the formation protocol.
There are several other issues that can arise with NMC 811 aqueous
processing if formulation chemistry is not handled properly, which are
Li/Hþ exchange in solution, formation of a highly basic solvent pH, and
modiﬁcation of the NMC 811 surface morphology. It is important to solve
these issues because the cost reduction promise of aqueous cathode
processing is substantial offering about a 50% savings over NMP based
operating costs [37], and the cost of NMP drying has been estimated at
3-4% of the total battery pack cost (with almost 6 MW of battery plant
energy required for drying per 100,000 battery packs produced) [38].
New plant capital costs will also be tremendously reduced (by ~$5-10 M)
since an NMP solvent recovery unit operation will not need to be added
[37]. It is also important to note that aqueous cathode processing is one
of those technologies that is meant to preserve the current, or
state-of-the-art, performance while offering major cost and environmental beneﬁts. Further challenges with cathode (or anode) aqueous
processing related to electrode cracking will come into play as the LIB
industry continues to raise the areal capacity to 6-8 mA h/cm2, or even
higher, to increase energy and power density of cells.

Another modiﬁcation which must be considered is the change in
binder that is essential to accommodate the large volumetric changes of
Si, which must also be compatible with aqueous processing. Polymers
with carboxylate functionalities (e.g. NaCMC and LiPAA) are generally
accepted to cycle best due to interactions with the SiOx surface. Unlike
their acidic counterparts (CMC and PAA), these binders rely on aqueous
processing since they are not soluble in NMP. While additional oxidation
may appear problematic from aqueous slurries, this is largely negated
from the beneﬁts of binders such as LiPAA [45]. First, neutralized LiPAA
has a higher decomposition temperature than PAA. Decomposition of
PAA, through dehydration, occurs at temperature as low as 140  C.
Complete Liþ titration of PAA to LiPAA greatly increases this temperature, allowing for further drying of the anode. Accurate water content can
be difﬁcult to measure in electrodes containing carboxylate-based
binders through conventional Karl Fischer (KF) titration techniques
that utilize ovens to remove adsorbed water. This is problematic since the
decomposition product of these binders is water itself, which results in
inaccurate water contents. Minimizing water content proves important
since small amounts (ppm level) cause HF formation, SEI composition
alteration, and impedance rise.
In addition to minimizing the water content, cells with anodes containing LiPAA display superior electrochemical cycling due to increased
Coulombic efﬁciency. Coulombic efﬁciency improves as the protons on
PAA are easily reduced at low potentials, thus forming LiPAA. In full cells
high Coulombic efﬁciency is required to maintain a constant N/P ratio.
Fig. 5 shows that without this constant N/P ratio, any long-term cycling is
impossible.
Adding 15 wt% Si to the graphite anodes will have a negligible impact
on cell cost, and it may even slightly raise the $/kWh value since current
sub-micron Si powders are quite expensive. However, this cell chemistry
modiﬁcation will have a major impact on energy density, as anodes can
be made much thinner while still having higher speciﬁc capacity and
enable cell balancing through the use of thick composite high energy
cathodes such as 6-8 mA h/cm2 NMC 811 or
NCA. This technology is one that will ultimately be paired with the
aqueous cathode processing methods discussed in Section 5 to realize
fully water-processed cells, but there are still major non-manufacturingrelated scientiﬁc challenges associated with raising the Si content from
the current 5-8 wt% used in industry today to the 15-30 wt% levels of
interest to the U.S. DOE Si Anode Deep Dive Consortium.

The inclusion of Si nanoparticles brings a unique set of challenges,
however, that are not found within cells built with only graphite as the
anode active material, especially when applied to aqueous processing.
One major difference between Si and graphite falls in the inherent
reactivity of the particles themselves. High surface area Si particles react
with basic water to form H2 gas [39], but in ORNL studies, like others in
the Li-ion battery ﬁeld, it was found that the Si particles are passivated by
a thin oxide layer that forms upon exposure to air [40]. With this
passivation, one would expect the Si to be largely inert to surrounding
water during slurry processing. Instead, the Si continues to slowly
oxidize, forming a thicker shell when mixed in the presence of carbon
additives and polymer binder. The product of this reaction is largely H2
gas, which is a safety concern, but it also includes a small amount of CO2,
indicating decomposition of the binder. Furthermore, it was determined
that a basic environment was not necessary to mediate this reaction as
the pH was maintained neutral by the anionic polyacrylate. Instead it was
proposed that this reaction occurred through a proton coupled electron
transfer between the Si, carbon black, and water. The reaction of the Si
surface during slurry processing ultimately leads to decreased capacity,
decreased Coulombic efﬁciency, and increased voltage decay. To mitigate this reaction, a thicker oxide layer can be utilized on the particle
surface by controlled calcination [41,42]. Zhang and coworkers investigated Si oxidation in aqueous slurries at elevated storage temperatures
and found that storage of 80 nm Si particles, at 75  C, for 3 days resulted
in large amounts of H2 generation; however, when these particles were
calcined at 400  C beforehand this reaction was largely halted. This
temperature was optimal as higher calcination temperatures resulted in
capacity loss and decreased Coulombic efﬁciency, due to the irreversible
reduction of the SiOx forming lithium silicates and oxides [43]. Touidjine
et. al. examined the difference between the surface oxide in Si nanoparticles formed in the presence of water versus standard calcination.
They found that the oxide formed by air was considerably denser than
that in water, which improved slurry storage times and resulted in
improved long-term cycling of the Si in half cells. As an alternative to an
increased oxide layer, the addition of a 3 wt% carbon coating was also

upper cut-off voltages yields higher initial capacity but more rapid capacity fade over the cycle life. Performance degradation stems from
instability of both the standard carbonate electrolyte and NMC cathode at
high voltage. Challenges for high voltage operation include (1) electrolyte oxidation and associated gas formation, (2) reduction of transition
metals at the NMC surface and the formation of a surface reconstruction
layer, (3) dissolution of transition metals from the NMC surface and
crossover to the anode, and (4) microcracking of NMC particles. These
different side reactions accelerate cell impedance rise and capacity fade
in high voltage cells.
Surface coatings on the NMC particles can address many of the
challenges that arise from instability at the electrode/electrolyte interface. For example, atomic layer deposition (ALD) can produce
nanometer-thick coatings of a protective metal oxide. ALD Al2O3 and
TiO2 coatings on Ni-rich cathodes signiﬁcantly lowered impedance rise
and increased cycle life in large-format 2 A h pouch cells [48].
High-resolution transmission electron microscopy revealed that the
coatings prevented the transformation from the layered phase to a spinel
phase at the cathode surface. Further characterization using electron
energy loss spectroscopy established that all three transition metals
(nickel, manganese, and cobalt) were reduced at the surface of the uncoated cathode material after cycling. ALD coatings on NMC prevented
the reduction of transition metals at the surface consistent with the
retention of the layered phase. Neutron diffraction conﬁrmed that the
phase change was limited to a thin surface region, since no changes in the
bulk crystal structure of the NMC were measured at the end of cycle life
[46].
Instability of the cathode/electrolyte interface can also impact the
chemistry at the anode. Battery scientists are just beginning to understand the complex reaction pathways that can contribute to electrode
cross-talk and impact cell performance. One widely accepted source of
cross-talk is dissolution of manganese ions from the cathode surface and
crossover through the electrolyte to the anode. Manganese deposition on
the anode alters the SEI chemistry and likely contributes to SEI growth
and impedance rise [46,49]. Because the cathode, anode, and their interactions via electrode cross-talk all contribute to cell stability, full cell
studies are critical to optimize performance. For example, the choice of
graphite material in the anode signiﬁcantly impacted the long-term
cycling stability of full cells built with the same nickel-rich cathode
[50]. The best graphite anodes enabled capacity retention around 90% in
full pouch cells over extensive long-term cycling compared to only 82%
for cells with lower performing graphite. Studies like these show that the
anode chemistry cannot be neglected as the limits of nickel-rich cathodes
continue to be pushed.
Cell gassing is another source of electrode cross-talk that impacts
battery safety and cycle life. NMC 811//Graphite cells charged to 4.4 V
generated signiﬁcantly more gas than identical cells charged to 4.2 V
[51]. The gases generated at the cathode and anode were isolated by
cycling symmetric cells where the same type of electrode (either graphite
or NMC 811) served as both the working and counter electrode. CO2 and
ﬂuorinated alkanes were the dominant gases evolved on the cathode side

7. Cobalt-free cathodes
ORNL’s newest synthesis and processing achievement is the development of novel cobalt-free cathode materials. Recently, the primary
focus of LIB research has been centered around making batteries cheaper,
safer and energy dense. Though batteries have become safer and more
energy dense, achieving the goal of cost reduction still remains a challenge, especially in a world of changing global battery raw material
market scenarios and the rapid rise in electric vehicle projections over
the next half century. When pursuing the goal of cost-reduction, it is
paramount to note that, at the material level, the cathode cost contributes
to around 30% of the total cost of LIBs. To achieve cost reduction goals,
LIB research should be focused on primarily reducing the cathode cost
and in particular reducing the cobalt loading in the cathodes because
most present-day LIB cathode materials are heavily dependent on cobalt.
Substantial reduction in cobalt loading in the cathodes needs to be achieved in order to reduce overall costs to meet current and future targets.
However, the conventional Li-ion battery technologies presently
employed in commercial applications employ LiNixMnyCo1-x-yO2 (NMC)
or LiNi0.8Co0.15Al0.05O2 (NCA) cathodes, which have substantial amounts
of cobalt in their compositions. The cobalt content in these cathodes is
nowhere near what is required to meet the cost reduction targets for nextgeneration LIBs, particularly for electric vehicle applications. On the
other hand, the price of cobalt has been in a state of constant ﬂuctuation
with prices nearly tripling during the last few years. This scenario has not
enabled in creating robust and low-cost manufacturing opportunities for
batteries for the EV market.
To address these challenges, ORNL has developed new cathode materials that use no cobalt in their compositions and have material and
electrochemical properties similar to that of conventional cobalt containing cathode materials, enabling these cobalt-free materials to have
minimal entry barrier to manufacturing. As an example, one of these new
cathode materials is the NFA class with general composition LiNixFeyAl1x-yO2 (x  0.8), which replaces the cobalt with low-cost elements such as
iron and aluminum. This material class has a layered crystal structure and
similar electrochemical (capacity ~200 mA h/g at 0.1C) properties to
NCA, enabling it to be processed using conventional methods used in
nickel rich cobalt containing cathode processing. The NFA cathode material was initially synthesized as hydroxide precursors using the coprecipitation process in a continuous stirred tank reactor (CSTR) followed by calcination in oxygen rich atmospheres to obtain the ﬁnal
cathode material. The obtained material was coated onto aluminum

8. Coating parameters and drying protocols
Primary and secondary drying optimization is critical to minimizing
the thermal budget and operating expenses for the solidiﬁcation process
of the electrodes and removing chemisorbed water prior to cell electrolyte ﬁlling, respectively. One essential parameter is the rheology of the
slurry, which affects the coating window, drying protocols, and
manufacturing throughput and cost. Raising the solid content reduces the
amount of solvent needed, which not only enables cost savings on raw
materials, but decreases the cost of both the primary drying and solvent
recovery steps. Lower solvent content is also beneﬁcial for decreasing
sedimentation in the slurry and mitigating binder migration during primary drying. However, increasing the solid content can raise the viscosity, which reduces the maximum coating speed [15]. Fig. 8 compares
the viscosity of NMC 532 slurries made with NMP and water-based
processing. Replacing the polyvinylidene diﬂuoride (PVDF) binder with
PVDF latex and carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) increases the solid content from 45 wt% to 65 wt% without increasing the slurry viscosity,
allowing similar coating parameters to be used. In addition, the higher
solid content dramatically shortens the primary drying time (i.e. from 51
s to 34 s for a graphite coating with a loading of 10 mg/cm2) [37].
Although the heat capacity of water (4.18 J/g K) is much higher than
NMP (1.76 J/g K), the total energy consumed during primary drying is
much lower for water-based processing due to the elimination of the
solvent recovery step. For instance, the energy savings is >25% when
drying an aqueous-processed graphite anode with a loading of 11
mg/cm2 and 32 wt% solids [37].
There are few studies on drying protocols in the literature, and the
interplay of drying protocol, pore structure, and electrode performance is
not well understood. However, there is consensus that fast drying and/or
drying at high temperatures promotes binder migration and inhomogeneity in composition distribution [52]. Thus, it is beneﬁcial to
start with low temperatures in the initial heating stage, gradually increase the temperature during the constant-rate stage, and further raise
temperature during the falling-rate stage.
Electrodes go through a secondary drying process to further remove
residual moisture before cell assembly. Electrodes from water-based
processing tend to adsorb more moisture, as their binders are more hydrophilic compared to PVDF. We found that NMC532 cathodes processed
with NMP and water contain 200 and 625 ppm of residual moisture at 20

curing speed and reduce the engineering cost in maintaining a low O2
level. The combination of dry processing or ultra-high-solids processing
with high-speed EB curing is a promising way to move forward based on
the excellent observed electrochemical performance. Therefore, high

and high energy density LIB electrodes.
As stated in Section 1, the major disadvantage of UV curing is the low
penetration depth of UV photons, which makes it less attractive for
composite electrode curing. On the other hand, EB curing also comes
with disadvantages such as oxygen inhabitation of the crosslinking reactions by free radical scavenging [62]. Inerting the EB curing chamber
with nitrogen is the most efﬁcient strategy to overcome oxygen inhibition. However, this inerting system and large volume of required

residual moisture level can be obtained for the water-based electrodes by
increasing the drying temperature by 10–20  C, albeit at a higher energy
consumption. Other methods, including infrared heating, microwave
heating, and radiofrequency heating, have also been applied to secondary drying to shorten the drying period and reduce the thermal budget.
When integrating dehumidiﬁed air and infrared heating at 150  C, a
2-min secondary drying process resulted in comparable residual moisture
to vacuum drying at 70  C for 4 days without sacriﬁcing cell performance
[54]. This process is compatible with roll-to-roll processing and can
signiﬁcantly improve the throughput and would dramatically reduce the
cell processing cost and thermal budget required for secondary drying, an
often overlooked contribution to the total $/kWh value.
The importance of secondary drying cannot be underestimated, as it
lowers adsorbed water content of the anode and cathode to ≪400 ppm
water. If this step is not done properly and thoroughly, excess adsorbed
water molecules and hydroxyl groups will react with the electrolyte salts
(LiPF6, LiClO4, etc.) and form strong corrosive acids (HF, HCl, etc.) in the
electrolyte solution that will react with the electrode material constituents, SEI and CEI layers, and current collector foils. Even though these
acids are in trace amounts once formed, their reactivity is enough to
signiﬁcantly degrade protective SEI and CEI layers, reduce the cohesiveness of the secondary particle network through carbon black corrosion, and decrease the adhesiveness of the electrodes to the Al and Cu
foils, all of which exacerbates the cell capacity fade phenomenon [55].
9. Novel high-speed methods of producing electrodes
ORNL has developed both EB and UV curing (the latter in partnership
with Miltec UV) methods over the past ﬁve years and is now combining
these approaches with electrostatic spraying to achieve the combination
of solvent free processing with ultra-high line speeds as shown in Fig. 9a.
EB/UV curing has been developed to replace the solvent-based methods
in certain sectors of the coating industry [56]. Signiﬁcant process energy
savings, ultra-high speed, and much more compact footprint are the
advantages of radiation curing compared to conventional drying ovens.
UV light and EB are the energy source in radiation curing to chemically
polymerize and crosslink small molecules (i.e. monomers or oligomers)
into high molecular weight (MW) crosslinked polymers. This chemical
process is usually completed in less than 1 s. A minimal amount of, or
even no, volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are required during the
coating formulation and deposition. Both UV and EB cured LIB cathodes
have been reported to have good performance in lab coin cells.57,58
However, the penetration of UV is limited by its low energy and the black
solid powders (active material and carbon black) further decreasing the
curing efﬁciency due to blockage of the UV light. The highest loading
