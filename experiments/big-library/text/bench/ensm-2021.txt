Artiﬁcial barriers, usually with either electrochemically active or inactive coating materials, are deployed on
cathode material surfaces to mitigate detrimental side reactions by suppressing direct contact of cathode and
electrolyte called surface coatings. These surface coatings are commonly known to increase the wettability of
liquid electrolyte and reduce the interfacial charge transfer resistance. An important caveat is the selection of
appropriate coating material with appropriate thickness for achieving enhanced electrochemical performance. As
modern battery materials are increasingly developed with some type of surface coating, a careful and thorough
examination of their role in mitigating the cycle life issues of cathode materials is paramount. This comprehensive
review article extensively covers the selection criteria of coating materials based on their chemical and physical
properties and electrochemical functionalities. Additionally, the article discusses the concept of critical coating
thickness and methods of achieving homogeneous coating architectures that deliver desired performance beneﬁts.
Furthermore, this comprehensive article summarizes the recent advancements, eﬀectiveness, necessity of cathode
surface coatings and identiﬁes the key aspect of structure-property correlation between coating type/thickness
and lithium-ion diﬀusion through coating layers as the linchpin that validates surface coating approaches especially for high capacity nickel-rich cathodes.

1. Introduction
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have largely been the impetus that
promises to usher in the era of electric vehicles (EVs) [1,2]. Modern
LIBs are vastly diﬀerent from the earliest versions, wherein each minuscule battery component has undergone years of extensive research and
development to achieve its present state of performance [3–6]. For example, the earliest positive electrode employed in the ﬁrst Li-based battery was TiS2 which proved to be incapable to handle the rapidly growing battery demand across the world [7–9]. After years of development,
lithium cobalt oxide (LiCoO2 ) emerged as the most promising candidate
for the role of the positive electrode to the extent that the ﬁrst commercial LIB deployed in the year 1990 employed LiCoO2 as the cathode
[10–12]. Further extensive technological advancements spanning three
decades led us to the present-date LIB which employs a carbonaceous
anode and a transition metal-based cathode (LiNix Mny Coz O2 (NMC),

LiNix Coy Alz O2 (NCA), LiFePO4 (LFP), etc., where x+y+z=1) [13–18].
At this critical juncture, just as LIBs are poised to drive the EV revolution, today’s mainstream LIBs are facing signiﬁcant challenges to keep
up with the public expectation [10,19]. This is because the continuously
evolving EV performance demand poses several challenges to the electrode materials that constitute the main component in LIBs [18]. In particular, cathode materials are the most challenging and most unforgiving component that needs to be adapted to the EV demands [14,16,20].
Four of the most desired cathode parameters include; (i) fast/chargedischarge capability, (ii) higher capacities – needed for improving energy density which would result in increased range, (iii) good mechanical stability – needed for long cycle life and (iv) low cost – needed to
grow the EV market and public acceptance [8,10].
To achieve these targets simultaneously, the battery manufacturing industry has two options; (i) development of new cathode materials which would require years of optimization before deployment

as the most promising material level optimization approach to improve
existing cathode materials [22–24]. From the earliest days of battery research, surface coatings have constantly been explored as an improvement strategy for cathode materials. There has been several unsettled
debates across the wide literature landscape on the role, necessity, and
eﬀectiveness of cathode surface coatings. Cathode surface coating literature includes a wide assortment of surface coatings ranging from
metal-based (metal oxides, metal phosphates, etc.,) to polymer-based
coatings [24–27]. There are also numerous coating techniques which
can lead to varying degrees of coating thicknesses, morphologies, and
performance diﬀerences [28,29]. In speciﬁc cases, even other cathode
materials have been employed as coatings to achieve desired performance beneﬁts. The plethora of research articles available in this vast
research area has undoubtedly caused major challenges in determining
the best cathode surface coating approaches. There are also several prior
reviews which have attempted to address this; however, the question regarding the best surface coating and the best coating approach remains
largely unsettled [22,24,30–35].
Despite this, surface coating approaches remain a key aspect of LIB
research and development to the extent that several present-day commercially available cathode materials have surface coatings on them.
It is therefore important to carefully examine the concept of surface
coatings and their role in mitigating the issues thoroughly suﬀered by
cathode materials. Presently, there are numerous reviews available on
cathode coatings available in prevalent literature discussing several different aspects and issues [22,24,30–35]. However, to date, it is still not
thoroughly understood - (i) how surface coatings behave during material and electrode fabrication? (ii) what is happening to the coating
itself during electrochemical cycling? are there any eﬀects that arise
from cathode anode crosstalk? and (iii) how they interact with the various electrolytes and their additives? Additionally, the synergistic eﬀects
of these coatings in the presence of other cell components such as the
cathode, electrolyte, conductive additives, and current collectors. There
is still a dearth of fundamental and mechanistic information regarding
these questions. Despite two decades of research, capacity fading, and
other safety issues still plague modern battery cathodes even though several coatings and coating methodologies have been even explored, investigated, and adopted for battery material manufacturing. This makes it
clear that there is a need for further understanding in the case of cathode
surface coatings. Our comprehensive review, for the ﬁrst time, summarizes the recent advancements, eﬀectiveness, necessity of cathode surface coatings and identiﬁes the key aspect of structure-property correlation between coating type/thickness and lithium-ion diﬀusion through
it as the linchpin that validates coating approaches while providing a
future outlook that also explores the advantages, key requirements and
limitations of cathode material coatings for next-generation solid state
battery architectures.

1) Ideally, surface coatings should be thin and homogenous. This helps
in improving the charge transfer between the cathode surface and
electrolyte interface. If the coating is thick, it will impede the Li+
mobility and thus increase the charge transfer resistance (Rct ) at the
cathode/electrolyte interface, resulting in poor battery performance.
2) Surface coatings should be ionically and electronically conducting so that it can allow the Li+ diﬀusion/electron migration during charging/discharging depending upon speciﬁc rate needs. Ionically conducting coatings can improve the charge transfer between
the cathode/electrolyte interface. Whereas, electronically conductive coatings can help in faster electron transfer from cathode to the
current collector, thus resulting in improved battery performance,
especially at high C-rates.
3) The coatings should be mechanically rigid and stable upon
charge/discharge cycling. There are always volume changes in the
cathode materials associated with intercalation and de-intercalation
of Li+ , resulting in the generation of mechanical stresses. The repeated cyclic mechanical stresses during cycling can lead to material
fatigue, which results in the formation of cracks and de-lamination
of coating material from the cathode surface. This could expose the
bare cathode surface to the electrolyte, which will lead to unwanted
side reactions resulting in poor electrochemical performance. A good
coating material, therefore, should minimize these stresses, enabling
eﬀective mitigation of the generated stresses associated with volumetric changes during cycling. The resulting low-stress gradient
would minimize the probability of delamination of the coating layer
from the cathode.
4) The coating process should be easy and scalable. Wet coating processes are extensively employed in commercial cathode material
manufacturing. However, these wet processes can modify the surface chemistry of the cathode due to lithium leaching and add to
extra cost due to the consequent wastewater treatment and subsequent drying of the coated material. Whereas, dry coating processes
are more attractive in terms of cost and handling.
However, the dry coating process has several issues which hinder
the broad commercial adoptability. Generally, dry coating processes
result in inhomogeneous and island coatings since it is diﬃcult to
control the homogeneity and thickness in the dry process. Therefore,
further optimization of the dry coating process is essential before
successful deployment in commercial applications.
5) Finally, the type of coating material and processes employed should
be inexpensive with raw materials readily available, and environmentally friendly.
3. Role/Functionality of Surface Coatings
The surface coatings applied to the cathode material serve several
functions, as summarized in Fig. 1 (b). Cathode materials in the charged
state are unstable and react violently with the non-aqueous electrolytes,
especially at elevated temperatures. Consequently, unwanted side reactions occur between the cathode material and the electrolyte resulting
in poor battery performance. Surface coatings have proved to be eﬀective to suppress these unwanted surface reactions. Thus, improvement
in the performance of lithium-ion batteries in terms of capacity retention, long term cycling, thermal stability, and high-temperature stability
can be achieved using surface coatings. Therefore, it has been widely
accepted by the industry and research communities that surface coating approaches are a possible way to improve the performance of battery materials. Nevertheless, the exact role and mechanism of surface
coatings is still a topic of interest that is continuously being explored
by the battery research community. To this extent, several hypotheses
have been proposed by various researchers to explain the possible role
of these coatings in the performance of cathode materials. It is worth
mentioning that the role of the coating depends on the type of coating material employed. Some of these hypotheses on the role of surface
coatings are listed below:

2. Requirements of Cathode Surface Coatings
Cathode surface coatings are artiﬁcial physical barriers developed on
the surface of electrochemically active cathode particles. The primary
role of such coatings is to act as a protective passivation ﬁlm which prevents the direct contact of the cathode material and the electrolyte, thus
mitigating the detrimental side reactions that can degrade the battery
performance. Ideally, coating layers should be thin and have superior
ionic and electronic conductivity compared to the cathode material depending upon need. A good coating can also enhance the charge transfer
between the cathode material and electrolyte while also providing electronic percolation pathways between the cathode material and the current collector. Fig. 1 (a) illustrates the requirements and the main challenges of the surface coatings. Speciﬁc requirements of surface coatings

3) An electronically and an ionically conductive layer which results
in enhancement of charge transfer at cathode/electrolyte interface.
Surface coatings with improved conductivities would result in fast
electron transfer from cathode particles to the current collector and
decrease in charge transfer resistance (Rct ).
4) A surface chemistry modiﬁer which facilitates the ionic charge transfer at cathode/electrolyte interface. In certain cases, the coating material may diﬀuse into the particle surface and modify the surface
chemistry of the cathode material itself, forming a new interface
layer, that has similar surface composition to the bulk, but with better structural stability and charge transport properties.

5) A structurally stable coating, which mitigates the stresses during the
phase transformation process during extensive cycling. This is essential because, cathode materials could react with the electrolyte,
especially in the de-lithiated (high-voltage) state, which results in
the detrimental phase transformation of the cathode surface by the
release of oxygen which leads to poor electrochemical and thermal
properties.
4. Coating Structures/Morphologies
The coating on the cathode material can have several diﬀerent morphologies depending upon the nature of the coating material and the
type of coating process used. However, an ideal coating should fully
cover the cathode particles and should be homogeneous and thin. This
will help in achieving the best performance in terms of battery cyclability and c-rate. Coating morphologies can be broadly divided into (1)
homogeneous coatings, (2) thick coatings and (3) island/rough coatings
as illustrated in Fig. 2.
4.1. Homogeneous Thin Coatings
Ideally, the coating surface should be homogeneous and thin. The

5. Coating Processes/Strategies

4.2. Thick Coatings

5.1. Wet Chemical Techniques

The thick coatings provide a good physical barrier between the cathode and the electrolyte, which may result in improved electrochemical
performance. However, depending on the nature of coating materials,
the thickness of the coating surface can impede the lithium diﬀusion
during intercalation/de-intercalation, which will result in poor electrochemical performance. However, thick coatings might work very well
during elevated temperature operation by protecting the cathode from
the electrolyte. Thick coatings are generally developed through several techniques including dry milling, sol-gel, co-precipitation etc. The
proper selection of coating material is also a critical factor in achieving
enhanced performance when employing thick coatings.

There are various wet chemical routes used to coat cathode materials, namely, sol-gel process, solvothermal and hydrothermal routes. The
compatibility between the solvents and the cathode materials used is a
primary selection criterion for this type of coating approach.

There are various coating processes/strategies that are commonly
employed in the battery research and development sector to achieve
the desired material and electrochemical goals. These can be broadly
classiﬁed into four categories – (i) wet chemical techniques (sol-gel, hydrothermal, solvothermal), (ii) gas-phase chemical processes (chemical
vapor deposition, atomic layer deposition, dielectric barrier discharge
technique), (iii) dry coating process (ball milling) and (iv) advanced
physical processes (pulsed laser deposition, sputtering) [14,28,43–
46,52–62].

5.1.1. Sol-Gel Coatings
The sol-gel coating process is usually extensively used to synthesize several diﬀerent types of cathode materials for battery systems
[14,60,62]. Apart from cathode material synthesis, it is also the most
used process for surface coatings of cathode materials in industry and
research. The process itself is simple which includes the following steps
in general, (1) dissolving a coating precursor in water or suitable solvent,
(2) adding the cathode material into the coating precursor solution followed by mixing, (3) removing the cathode material from the solvent by
ﬁltration, (4) oven drying and post-processing of the cathode material to
remove the adsorbed solvent and, (5) ﬁnally heat treating the cathode
material to develop a homogeneous coating layer on the cathode material surface. The process itself is viable, easy, and scalable, making it an
ideal candidate for industrial-scale mass production. Apart from this, the
process oﬀers homogenous mixing of the precursor at the atomic scale,
good control over coating stoichiometry, better crystallinity, and can
achieve thin and homogeneous coatings. The sol-gel process also oﬀers
the ﬂexibility of applying it to several coating materials. However, due
to the use of water or other solvents, the process cost is increased due to
additional drying of cathode materials and the subsequent treatment of
wastewater. One of the major caveats of this process can come from the
possible modiﬁcation of the surface chemistry of the cathode because of

4.3. Island/Rough Coatings
In general, it is hard to achieve a homogeneous and a thin coating
throughout the material using dry coating and wet coating processes. It
is generally observed that coatings developed through these processes
are rough, with some of the areas heavily coated, while other areas
have barely any coating. Nevertheless, these types of coatings have also
shown positive eﬀects on the electrochemical performance of cathode
materials mainly acting as HF scavengers and partial physical barrier.
In these cases, the un-coated areas remain as sites for unwanted side reactions with the electrolyte. The morphology achieved largely depends
upon the type of coating precursors, concentration, and process conditions.

5.3.2. Atomic Layer Deposition (ALD) Coatings
Atomic layer deposition (ALD) is a thin ﬁlm deposition technique
which is widely being used in the semiconductor industry for surface
coatings of several types of electronic components and devices including DRAM capacitors, magnetic recording heads, solar cells, bio/gas sensors and gate oxides [49,50,71–73]. In recent times, many reports have
been published reporting the use of ALD coating to improve the performance of cathode materials [32,41–46]. ALD process is a subclass
of chemical vapor deposition (CVD) which uses the sequential use of
gas-phase chemical reactions on the substrate material. In general, the
ALD process can be divided into two types such as conventional and ﬂuidized bed process. The ALD process can be summarized as followed and
also presented graphically in Fig. 3(e) [74], (i) the substrate is loaded
into the ALD chamber, (ii) the chamber is purged with inert gas to remove any gases from the chamber, (ii) the ﬁrst precursor gas is pulsed
into the reaction chamber, (iv) the chamber is again purged with inert gas to remove any excess reactant, (v) second reactant gas is then
pulsed into the reaction chamber and (vi) ﬁnally the chamber is again
purged with inert gas to remove any excess reactants and the reaction
by-products. The coating layers formed by ALD are atomic level thick,
and the thickness can be increased by increasing the number of reaction
cycles. The biggest advantage of ALD based coatings is the formation of
homogenous, crystalline, uniform and high-quality coatings which can
be achieved with precision and accuracy. It can be used to deposit a
wide range of coatings including metals, metal oxides, metal sulﬁdes,
metal nitrides and complex compounds. ALD based coating is commercially not viable for cathode material applications due to low yield, slow
process times, high cost of precursors, diﬃculties in handling the precursors due to toxicity and the process complexity. Therefore, ALD based
coatings are sophisticated and expensive to be applied to commercial
cathode materials.

5.1.2. Hydrothermal/Solvothermal Coating Process
The hydrothermal coating process is also a wet process involving a
solvent. There are several reports on the coating of cathode materials
using hydrothermal process [52,63,64]. This process utilizes low temperatures and high pressures. The process involves, (1) mixing of precursor salts in a solvent (DI water or other solvents) in specialized apparatus called autoclaves, (2) adding the cathode material to the precursor
solution, (3) subjecting the autoclave to elevated temperatures for a certain period, (4) washing of cathode material with a suitable solvent, (5)
drying the cathode material to eliminate the adsorbed solvent and, (6)
heat-treating the dried cathode material to form a homogeneous and
crystalline coating layer on the surface. The coatings developed using
hydrothermal/solvothermal process are in the order of nanometers and
are homogeneous with good control of coating stoichiometry. However,
this process of applying surface coatings is not suitable for scalability
due to diﬃcult processing, expensive precursor salts and low yield of
the process.
5.2. Dry Coating Process
The dry coating process is potentially the most viable and suitable
process for surface coatings of cathode materials and is actively being
used in the industry for various materials and applications. The process
is very simple and involves the following steps: (1) mixing of cathode
material and coating material/precursor using ball milling or other mixing techniques, (2) heat-treating the mixed materials (cathode material
and coating material). Dry coating process has many advantages over
other coating processes such as viability, scalability, cost-eﬀectiveness,
and ease of handling. The process can be used for many types of coating
materials and is therefore very ﬂexible. However, generally, it is challenging to achieve a homogeneous coating layer using the dry coating
process. Recently Nisar et al. employed a dry coating process to coat
SiO2 on LiNi0.5 Mn1.5 O4 to form a uniform and homogenous coating
layer with ultra-high rate performance as already discussed in section
4.1.1 [29].

6. Types of Coating Materials
The type of coating material employed solely depends on the speciﬁc
requirement and the type of cathode material itself. For example, oxidebased cathode materials such as a layered oxide (NCM811, NCM622)
and spinel (LMO, LNMO) are usually coated with an oxide-based coating for improving the electrochemical performance. This is expected to
provide a physical barrier between the highly unstable de-lithiated cathode (during charge) and electrolyte, acting as an HF scavenger. Whereas,
phosphate-based materials, such as LFP, are typically coated with carbon materials, which helps in improving the electronic conductivity of
these materials which in turn improves the overall electrochemical performance. Several diﬀerent types of coating materials have been investigated on the cathode materials for lithium-ion batteries which include
carbon [33,75–79], metal oxides [80–95], phosphates [96–98], ionic
conductors [99–103], active materials [104–109], and conducting polymers [110–114]. For the purpose of this review, the following sections
would focus on the major material classes that are widely employed as
coatings for the mainstream cathode materials investigated by prevalent
research eﬀorts in addition to the ones commercially manufactured.

5.3. Gas-Phase Chemical Processes
There are various gas-phase chemical processes used to coat cathode materials, namely, chemical vapor deposition (CVD), atomic layer
deposition (ALD) [49,50,53–58,71].
5.3.1. Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD)
Chemical vapor deposition (CVD) is a thin ﬁlm deposition technique
widely employed in the semiconductor industry. The materials are deposited from the vapor phase by the decomposition of the reactants on
the substrate material at a certain temperature. In a typical CVD process
(Fig. 3(d)), (1) the material/substrate is placed inside the CVD chamber,
(2) the coating precursors are placed before the substrate, (3) the CVD
furnace is heated to the required temperature under a continuous ﬂow
of gas, which acts as the carrier gas and provides a suitable reaction
environment, (4) the precursor materials are converted to vapor phase
and react on or near the substrate where they form a thin coating layer.
The volatile by-products produced during the reactions are removed by
the carrier gas ﬂowing through the reaction chamber. By controlling the
CVD experimental parameters such as substrate temperature, substrate
material type, coating precursors, gas ﬂow etc., a wide range of coatings with versatile physical and chemical properties can be achieved.
Major beneﬁts of CVD technology are the production of homogeneous
and thin coatings with low porosity, a wide range of coating possibilities
as well as the coating on complex shapes. Therefore, numerous reports
are available in the literature for CVD coating of cathode materials [53–
58].

6.1. Metal Oxides
The metal oxide coatings act as a physical barrier between the cathode materials and the electrolytes. Typically, the metal in these coatings
have only one stable valence state at ambient conditions, and access to
other valence states is challenging; therefore, the coating does not take
part in electrochemical reactions and are thus inactive. These coatings
are generally cheap and easy to apply. Several processes, including ball
milling, sol-gel, CVD, solvothermal, hydrothermal and ALD processes,
have been used to develop these kinds of coatings on cathode materials
[80–85,88–95,103]. The shortcomings of these coatings are that they are
generally inactive to lithium ions and thus have low lithium-ion conductivity. The thicker coating is applied to such materials though achieve

6.6. Surface doping
In general, surface coating approaches involves the development of a
new physical barrier layer on the cathode surface. This physical barrier
layer is generally less reactive to the electrolyte when compared to the
cathode material, therefore improving the structural and thermal stability of the bulk materials. However, new concepts of surface coating
and optimization such as surface doping, involving the doping of foreign
ions to few nanometers on the surface of the bulk cathode material. This
may also be considered as surface coating since it develops a new interface on the surface which is more resistant to the electrolyte and thus
improves the overall electrochemical performance of the cathode material [159–163]. This type of surface optimization approach is very useful
since the crystal structure, and the composition change at the interface
between the surface layer and the bulk is similar; therefore, there is no
physical barrier present for Li+ diﬀusion which results in low charge
transfer resistance (Rct ). This also results in low mechanical stresses at
the interface; thus, the chances of delamination are reduced, and it also
results in improved electrochemical performance.

All solid-state batteries are considered to be the next big advancement in battery research and development. Organic electrolytes in conventional lithium-ion batteries raise many issues related to safety and
electrochemical performance due to the formation of unstable SEI interface. Therefore, with the advent of solid-state batteries (SSB), it was
initially believed that due to the elimination of liquid-based electrolytes,
the issue of electrolyte and interface instability with cathode could be
easily resolved. However, current research has shown that solid electrolytes (SE) also have instability issues when in direct contact with
cathode materials, especially during high voltage operations [191–198].
The SE is prone to oxidation when in direct contact with the cathode
material, which results in the formation of resistive interfaces, and increasing the impedance of the cell. This leads to poor electrochemical
performance and fast capacity fade. Therefore, protective surface coatings of cathode materials are also required in SSB to stabilize the cathode
materials and SE interface by suppressing these oxidative reactions.

8.2. Challenges of Cathode Material Coatings in SSBs
Despite the beneﬁts gained by the application of coatings on cathode materials in SSBs, there are a considerable number of complexities
in the coating of SSB cathodes that needs to be well understood. It is well
known that there is a need for a good homogenous coating of cathode
active particles and the coating layer should be a poor electron conductor. However, full coverage of cathode materials with coating layer
would, therefore, inhibit the electrode from functioning since an electronic percolating network is needed. To achieve electron percolating
network, the cathode material particles must be in direct contact with
each other or with a conductive additive. Therefore, designing a homogeneous coating with full coverage of cathode particles, and at the same
time allowing the cathode particle-particle contact to provide a conductive percolation network is a real challenge. It can be speculated that the
particles may form a contact during the electrode fabrication process,
while the electrode is pressed under high pressure either during the calendaring processes (in case of a cast electrode) or during electrochemical testing under pressure. Hard and brittle coatings are prone to crack
formation during these processes and thus can form particle contacts.
However, at the same time, these regions of direct particle contacts are
new regions for SE decomposition. In contrast, if the coating is plastic
and deformable, it may form new contact points by deforming without
forming the cracks and rupture.
Moreover, it is well known that the cathode materials undergo volume changes during intercalation and de-intercalation, which results
in contact loss between diﬀerent particles and delamination of coating from the particle surface. This also results in an increase in the
impedance of the cell and capacity loss. Therefore, the applied surface
coatings should suppress or accommodate these volume changes, in order to maintain the particle-particle or particle-SE/conductive additive
and the coating layer itself. In comparison, in conventional lithium-ion
batteries, these volume changes are accommodated to a large extent due
to the ﬂuidic nature of the electrolyte and thus, the electrode/electrolyte
are always in contact with each other.
Additionally, there is also the possibility of interdiﬀusion between
cathode active material and SE when they are in contact, especially
during high voltage operations. However, the presence of coating layer
on the cathode particles is expected to suppress this interdiﬀusion and
therefore, mitigate the formation of resistive interfaces. In general, these
interfaces are poor ionic conductors; thus, increasing the impedance and
charge transfer resistance (Rct ). However, depending on the type and
composition of these interfaces, the stability of cathode materials may
also increase. Fig. 12 illustrates the key issue arising at the cathode/SE
surface and resulting advantages achieved in the presence of thin and
homogeneous surface coating applied to these particles.

Inorganic SEs can be divided into three main categories, (1) oxides, (2) sulﬁdes and (3) argyrodites. Sulﬁde based SEs are of immense
signiﬁcance due to their exceptionally high ionic conductivities when
compared to oxide-based SEs. Unlike oxides, sulﬁde-based SE have low
chemical stability and are only stable in a narrow voltage window [196].
This make it diﬃcult for sulﬁde SEs to be employed in practical SSB due
to the limited amount of energy density delivered in the limited operational voltage window. Additionally, the sulﬁde-based SEs form resistive interfaces when in direct contact with the cathode material and thus
an increase in interfacial resistance is generally observed. However, to
successfully employ them in SSBs, applying surface coatings on cathode materials are considered a promising alternative to overcome their
intrinsic electrochemical stability issues. Thus, the proper selection of
coating materials and interface design are challenging tasks in SSB research. Some of the many diﬀerent coatings that have been applied to
cathodes in SSBs include LiNbO3 , LiTaO3 , LiBO2 , Li4 Ti5 O12 , Li2 O-TiO2 ,
ZrO2 , SiO2 , Li2 SiO3 , Li4 GeO4 , Li3 PO4 , Al2 O3 etc. and have resulted in
considerable performance enhancements in all SSB cells [144,200–207].

9. Conclusions and Future Outlook
To summarize, the beneﬁts, role, necessity, and types of coating materials were comprehensively discussed in this review. Coatings typically
based on oxides, phosphates, polymers, ionically conductive materials
and in speciﬁc cases certain cathode materials are employed to improve
the electrochemical performance of battery cathode materials. The role
of coatings in minimizing detrimental electrolyte-cathode side reactions
was also discussed brieﬂy in the review. Mitigation of interfacial charge
transfer resistance because of the application of various coatings appropriate for speciﬁc cathode materials was also discussed here. The review

material manufacturers to adopt cost-eﬀective strategies as a compromise approach despite the reduced beneﬁts.
iii Most widely used cost-eﬀective and scalable coating materials exhibit very low lithium-ion diﬀusivity and their maximum coating
thicknesses are usually recommend to around ∼10 nm for eﬀective
utilization of the beneﬁts of such coatings. This is because some coating materials which are generally quite inert and stable (e.g. ZrO2 or
Al2 O3 ) may react with lithium ions and form secondary phases (e.g.
Li2 ZrO3 ) resulting in capacity fading issue
iv With the advent of next generation all solid-state batteries that
promise energy dense and safer li-ion batteries, it has become evident that achieving stable interfaces in these systems are the crucial step before commercial deployment. Cathode surface coatings
in particular would be instrumental in achieving the required properties that promote stability at the cathode-SE interface during battery operation. Coatings that can provide a physical barrier with desired mechanical properties, suppresses solid-electrolyte decomposition, enables an ion conducting percolation network while providing
pathways for electron conduction between the conductive additive
and cathode material have the potential to enable rapid deployment
of SSBs and therefore, can revolutionize modern battery technologies.
