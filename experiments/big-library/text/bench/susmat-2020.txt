a b s t r a c t
Separation of cathode materials from the current collector remains a challenging task for the recycling of both
spent lithium-ion batteries and cathode scraps. Dissolving the organic binder polyvinylidene diﬂuoride (PVDF)
with an organic solvent to recover both cathode materials and Al foils is an efﬁcient and promising method. However, the use of toxic solvents limits their practical application in recycling large amounts of cathode scraps generated during the manufacturing process. Cyrene, a bioderived green solvent, is proposed here for a solventbased separation process. This study investigated a closed-loop recovery process to reclaim cathode materials,
Al foils, and PVDF binder from cathode scraps. Additionally, the reuse of the Cyrene solvent led to a circular
recycling process. The Cyrene-based separation process embraces a sustainable electrode recovery and reuse
platform and paves the way for battery recycling.

1. Introduction
As the explosive growth of electric vehicles continues, huge numbers of spent lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) that once powered those cars
are generated, requiring appropriate disposal and recycling in an economical and environmentally friendly manner [1–4]. Besides end-oflife LIBs, there are large amount of electrode scraps generated during
the manufacturing process, which are ready for immediate recycling.
Three recycling strategies—the pyrometallurgical process, hydrometallurgical process, and direct recycling process—have been employed or
proposed for the recycling of end-of-life LIBs, especially for the recycling
of cathode materials. Direct recycling aims to recover the cathode particles with minimal changes to their crystal structure and morphologies
[3]. This approach can lead to retaining a substantial fraction of the energy embodied in the materials from their primal manufacturing process [5]. However, cathode materials are tightly bonded to Al foils

the separation of electrode materials from Al foils challenging. The
solvent-based wet chemical process has been considered as a simple
and efﬁcient method to separate the electrode materials from the current collector [6–8]. Dipolar aprotic solvents, including N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), are usually
employed to dissolve the PVDF polymer for reclaiming the active materials. However, NMP and DMF are toxic and are under increased regulatory pressures that severely limit their industrial applications. The key
driver behind replacing these solvents is their high toxicity, with both
NMP and DMF recently added to the Registration, Evaluation and Authorization of Chemicals (REACH) restricted substances list, severely
limiting their use [9,10]. Thus, replacing those traditional solvents
with a greener and safer alternative is critical.
As a green bioderived solvent from waste cellulose,
dihydrolevoglucosenone, also known as Cyrene, is considered a potential replacement for toxic dipolar aprotic solvents such as NMP and
DMF [9,11,12]. Hansen solubility parameters including dispersion (δd),
polar (δp), and hydrogen bonding (δh) interactions for solvents such
as NMP, DMF, and Cyrene were plotted in a three-dimensional Hansen
space as shown in Fig. 1a. All those solvents are located inside the
Hansen sphere of PVDF and are close to the center, indicating that Cyrene can dissolve PVDF similarly to NMP and DMF [13]. Compared
with NMP and DMF, Cyrene shows lower toxicity, is safe to handle,
and is also barely ecotoxic, as illustrated in Fig. 1b [11,14]. To this end,
Cyrene has been proven to be a viable alternative to both NMP and
DMF in many applications [12,14–18]. For example, Cyrene has been

Here, the authors propose using the greener, safer, and more
sustainable solvent, Cyrene, to dissolve PVDF and separate the cathode
materials from Al foils. In this study, Cyrene was assessed for its suitability in substituting for NMP and DMF in the separation process. By studying the physical properties of the PVDF/Cyrene solution, a Cyrene-based

separation process at an elevated temperature was proved to successfully reclaim the cathode materials while avoiding the re-precipitation
of PVDF. Additionally, a thermally induced phase separation (TIPS) approach was proposed and demonstrated to recover the PVDF polymer.
The authors also found that Cyrene can be recycled and further reused.
The authors believe that the green, solvent-based separation process
will pave the way for the direct recycling of cathode materials from
both spent LIBs and manufacturing scraps.

2. Experimental

2.2. Characterizations

2.1. Materials

The viscosity of the PVDF/Cyrene mixture was measured by a rheometer (Discovery HR-3, TA Instruments) at a shear rate of 11 s−1.
The dissolution of PVDF was measured by a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC, Q100, TA Instruments). Thermogravimetric and derivative thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, DTG) were performed on a TA
Instruments Q50 to determine the decomposition temperature of the
surface coating layer. The crystal structure, surface morphology, and
composition of the recovered materials were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD; PANalytical X'pert PRO) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Zeiss MERLIN) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDX). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Bruker) was
used to analyze the existence of PVDF in the recovered cathode material
as well as the Cyrene solvent before and after use. The lithium content of
the NMC622 cathode materials before and after recovery was measured
using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICPOES, Agilent Technologies 5110).

Cathode scraps/rejects were from the trimmings of a
LiNi0.6Mn0.2Co0.2O2 (NMC622) cathode that was fabricated using a
pilot-scale slot-die coater (Frontier Industrial Technology) at the US Department of Energy Battery Manufacturing Facility at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. The cathode consisted of NMC622 (Targray), carbon
black (CB, Denka), and PVDF binder (Solvay Solef 5130) in a weight
ratio of 90:5:5. The areal loading of the NMC622 cathode was
~10.2 mg/cm2. Cyrene was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used
without any treatment.

2.1.1. Separation and recovery process
Cathode scraps (50 g) were cut into 1 cm2 pieces manually and
immersed in 100 mL of Cyrene solvent. After stirring at 100 °C for
1 h, Al foils were separated from the mixture of cathode powder
and Cyrene via screen ﬁltration. The cathode powder was then recovered through centrifugation while the mixture was hot. The supernatant containing PVDF/CB were then cooled down to room
temperature to induce phase separation, followed by ﬁltration to obtain PVDF/CB composites and recycled Cyrene solvent. The separated
cathode powder was calcined in air at 600 °C for 2 h to remove surface coatings and CB.

2.2.1. Electrochemical measurements
The recovered NMC622 powder was mixed with PVDF and CB in a
weight ratio of 90:5:5 in NMP. The obtained slurry was coated onto Al
foil, followed by drying and calendering. A half-cell coin cell conﬁguration was adopted for the electrochemical test. The electrolyte used
was 1.2 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate/ethylmethyl carbonate
(EC/EMC = 3:7 by weight, SoulBrain). The cells were cycled between

3. Results and discussion
The dissolution behavior of PVDF in Cyrene was ﬁrst studied using
rheology and DSC tests to guide the design of the Cyrene-based separation process. PVDF can be dissolved in NMP at room temperature to
form a viscous solution (e.g., 8 wt% PVDF/NMP solution), which is commonly used for producing a slurry for cathode coating [19]. Although
Cyrene has similar Hansen solubility parameters to those of NMP, the
authors found that the PVDF binder was barely soluble in Cyrene
below 80 °C, whereas it quickly dissolved in Cyrene at a higher temperature (e.g., 100 °C). As shown in Fig. 2a and Fig. S1a, clear solutions were
formed for both 0.5 and 5 wt% PVDF/Cyrene at 100 °C. Interestingly,
phase separation for the PVDF/Cyrene solution was observed during
cooling down, producing an opaque solution (Fig. 2a). The viscosity at
different temperatures was measured using a rheology meter as plotted
in Fig. 2a. The viscosity at room temperature (20 °C) was three orders of
magnitude larger than that at 80 °C. During the cooling process, PVDF
started to precipitate out and gelation occurred, leading to the formation of an opaque jelly-like mixture as illustrated in Fig. 2a and
Fig. S1b. Fig. S1c shows that the glass vial was inverted and no ﬂow
was observed, suggesting the formation of an organogel for 5 wt% of
the PVDF/Cyrene mixture at room temperature. DSC curves in Fig. 2b
further reveal that the PVDF binder dissolved in Cyrene at ~90 °C, indicating that the separation process should be kept above that temperature. This observation was conﬁrmed by placing the cathode coatings

in Cyrene at different temperatures. As shown in Fig. S2, cathode coatings were intact below 80 °C, whereas separation occurred at 100 °C.
Based on the dissolution behavior of PVDF in Cyrene, Fig. 2c
shows the proposed ﬂowchart for the Cyrene-based separation
process. In this study, cathode rejects or scraps were investigated
for recycling. Controlling the temperature was critical because
PVDF would precipitate out if the temperature was low. Therefore,
a hot ﬁltration method using screen ﬁlters was proposed to separate foils from the black mass solution. Similarly, hot separation
methods, such as hot centrifugation and hot ﬁltration, were utilized to separate the black mass from Cyrene to prevent the reprecipitation of PVDF. After separation, the supernatant contains
PVDF, CB, and Cyrene solvent. A TIPS process was employed to recover the PVDF/CB as well as the solvent, leading to a complete
and closed-loop recycling process.
FTIR spectroscopy was used to monitor the removal of PVDF from
the cathode rejects. Fig. 3a compares the FTIR spectra of the pristine
PVDF, the NMC622 cathode rejects, and the reclaimed cathode powder ﬁltered at room temperature and high temperature
(e.g., 100 °C). Characteristic bands at 1402, 1172, and 871 cm−1,
which can be ascribed to the vibration of CH2, symmetrical stretching
of CF2, and bending vibration of CH2, respectively, can be used as representative peaks for PVDF [20]. Both cathode rejects and separated
cathode powder at room temperature showed a distinct band at
871 cm−1, indicating the existence of PVDF. This also demonstrates
the reprecipitation of PVDF along with the cathode particles after
cooling down to room temperature. The absence of the typical band
at 871 cm−1 for the reclaimed powder that was ﬁltered at high temperature demonstrated the removal of PVDF binder. The FTIR results

The morphology of the cathode scraps and the reclaimed cathode
particles were characterized by SEM as shown in Fig. 3b and c. The
NMC622 cathode scraps were composed of NMC622 secondary particles and CB nanoparticles that were bonded by PVDF binder. The size
of the secondary particles ranged from several to tens of micrometers
with primary particles being hundreds of nanometers. After the
Cyrene-based separation process, the reclaimed microparticles
displayed identical morphologies and sizes as the cathode scraps, emphasizing that this solvent-based process did not destroy the active materials, which is especially important for the direct recycling approach
[3]. Fluorine content represented the amount of PVDF binder, which
was characterized by EDX spectroscopy. The EDX spectra in Fig. 3d
and e clearly illustrate the substantial decrease of the ﬂuorine signal,
conﬁrming the successful removal of PVDF binder. The mass ratio of
ﬂuorine element on the surface of the cathode rejects was 12.49%, and
that value was greatly reduced to 0.14% after the Cyrene-based separation process. Furthermore, the relative ratios of Ni, Mn, and Co from
NMC622 were consistent before and after the separation process, demonstrating that the process does not leach those transition metals. This
observation was also conﬁrmed from the ICP-OES results, as displayed
in Table S1. It is noteworthy that lithium leaching from NMC622 occurred after recovery. The Cyrene molecule has two ether groups,
which will attract lithium cation because ether is electron-donating,
resulting in lithium leaching and causing the high-Ni cathode more
unstable.

The electrochemical performance of the recovered NMC622 particles was evaluated in half-cell coin cells. Fig. 5a shows the voltage
proﬁles of the baseline and recovered NMC622 at a charge/discharge
rate of C/10. The ﬁrst charge capacities for the baseline and recovered
NMC622 were 205.6 and 194.0 mAh/g, respectively. The recovered
NMC622 maintained 91.87% of the reversible discharge capacity.
Fig. 5b shows the cycle life of the recovered cathode at a charge/
discharge rate of C/3. The recovered NMC622 sample exhibited
stable cycle performance with an increased capacity (100.9% capacity retention after 40 cycles) due to the electrochemical relithiation
in the half-cell test. The recovered NMC622 showed a discharge capacity of 158.0 mAh/g after 40 cycles, which maintained 92.67% of
the baseline capacity. The electrochemical degradation could be attributed to both the lithium leaching and possible surface changes
caused by the Cyrene solvent. A potential path to solve the issue is
to restore lithium and reconstruct the surface through thermal annealing with the addition of lithium precursor. In addition, the electrochemical performance for the regenerated NMC622 will be
evaluated in the full-cell test.
Fig. 6a shows the SEM image and EDX spectrum for the recovered Al
foil. No particles were found on the surface of the Al foil, conﬁrming the
successful separation of cathode materials from the current collector
with a high peeling-off efﬁciency. Unlike high-temperature heat treatment, which decomposes the organic PVDF binder and produces toxic
HF gas, the Cyrene-based separation process recovers PVDF binder, as
well, further expanding the recycling revenue. Based on this observation, a TIPS process was used for the recovery of PVDF. After reclaiming
the active materials, the supernatant was composed of PVDF and CB in
Cyrene. By cooling down the hot supernatant to room temperature,
phase separation occurred in which PVDF polymer precipitated out together with the residual CB particles. The recovered PVDF/CB mixture
was characterized by SEM and FTIR. Fig. 6b shows that CB particles
were embedded in the PVDF polymer composites with no observable
active material particles. Fig. 6c compares the FTIR spectrum of pristine
PVDF with that of recovered PVDF, where the IR bands matched well
with each other. The recovered PVDF can be potentially reused as electrode binder or processed as membrane for desalination and water
treatment.
The Cyrene-based separation process is both economically and environmentally feasible because the process does not produce waste. The
solvent Cyrene can be recovered after separating from the phaseseparated PVDF/CB mixture. Fig. 6d shows the FTIR spectra of pristine
and recovered Cyrene, which exhibited similar band signals. No PVDF
signals were detected in the recovered Cyrene, suggesting complete
phase separation of PVDF from Cyrene. The recovered Cyrene was further used for the separation process for several cycles and similar separation phenomena were realized. Thus, the Cyrene-based separation
process represents a circular recycling approach that will greatly reduce
the recycling costs.

4. Conclusions
In summary, a bio-derived green solvent, Cyrene, was demonstrated
to successfully separate cathode materials from the current collector for
direct recycling of cathode scraps. The reclaimed cathode materials
showed similar crystal structure, morphology, and electrochemical performance compared with the pristine cathode materials. Additionally,
both PVDF binders and Cyrene were recovered through the TIPS process. In this regard, the authors developed a closed-loop recycling process with a solvent that can be repeatedly used. The authors believe
the circular recycling approach will pave the way for the direct recycling
of LIBs. With the vast stockpile of spent LIBs looming on the horizon, future work will be focused on optimizing the separation parameters to
reclaim spent cathode materials as the long-term cycling impacts the
cathode properties.
