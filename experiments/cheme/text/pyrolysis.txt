ABSTRACT: To investigate the effects of reaction atmosphere on
the oxidative thermal conversion and product-evolution characteristics of oil shale, Maoming oil shale and its extracted kerogen were
examined by thermogravimetric analysis (TG) and simultaneous
thermal analysis coupled with Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy and mass spectrometry (STA-FTIR-MS) under air and
nitrogen. The oil-shale mass-loss process was divided into three
characteristic stages. In the present experiments, the onset of the
principal mass-loss region under air occurred approximately 50 °C
earlier than under nitrogen, and kerogen displayed pronounced
exothermic behavior in air but a mild endothermic response in
nitrogen. Isoconversional OFW and DAEM analyses yielded
atmosphere-dependent apparent kinetic parameters; because
oxidation, oxygen uptake, volatilization, and combustion may overlap during heating in air, these parameters describe the overall
mass-change process and are not interpreted as intrinsic thermal-cracking barriers directly comparable with those obtained under
nitrogen. The uncalibrated MS signals and FTIR spectra show distinct atmosphere-dependent evolution profiles for H2, H2O, CH4,
CO2, and light hydrocarbons. These observations identify differences in product-evolution temperature ranges and relative signal
profiles but do not establish absolute product yields, a carbon balance, or a unique reaction pathway. The results support further
laboratory-scale evaluation of air-assisted conversion, while field-scale feasibility requires quantitative heat-balance, oxygen-demand,
heat-loss, front-stability, and energy-efficiency assessments.

1. INTRODUCTION
As a sedimentary rock rich in organic matter, oil shale is an
important component of unconventional oil and gas resources,
and the oil and gas products contained within it are mainly
obtained through thermal conversion of the raw rock.1,2 In
recent years, with the development of in situ pyrolysis oil
recovery technology, the thermal reaction mechanism during
the formation heating process has gradually become a research
hotspot.3−5 During pyrolysis, macromolecular organic matter
such as kerogen undergoes a series of complex physicochemical reactions. This is not only the core link in oil and gas
generation but also directly drives the evolution of the pore
structure and changes in seepage capacity within the rock
mass.6,7 Therefore, an in-depth investigation into the pyrolytic
evolution characteristics of kerogen and its deep acting
mechanisms is of significant theoretical and engineering
importance for optimizing in situ pyrolysis process parameters
and improving oil and gas recovery.8
Domestic and international scholars have conducted
extensive research on oil-shale pyrolysis.9,10 Thermal-analysis
techniques (TG/DTG) are widely used to characterize
conversion regions and derive apparent kinetic parameters
for fossil fuels.11 Most previous studies have emphasized

2.3.2. Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA). Thermogravimetric
tests were performed using a TGA203F3 thermogravimetric analyzer
(NETZSCH, Germany). For each test, approximately 5 mg of rock
sample was weighed into a crucible. Samples were heated from room
temperature to 800 °C at 5, 10, and 15 °C min−1 under air or
nitrogen. The reaction purge-gas flow rate was 50 mL min−1, and the
nitrogen protective-gas flow rate was 20 mL min−1. Each condition
was independently repeated twice under identical conditions. The
duplicate measurements showed consistent overall TG/DTG profiles
and characteristic reaction regions. Because only two replicates were
available, statistically robust uncertainty estimates based on three or
more independent replicates were not calculated; accordingly,
differences in peak temperatures and mass-loss rates are interpreted
as observed trends rather than statistically significant differences.
2.3.3. Kerogen Extraction. Referring to the national standard
(GB/T19144-2010), kerogen was extracted from the oil shale using
the HCl-HF-HCl acid washing method. The oil shale powder was
sequentially treated with 20% hydrochloric acid and a mixed acid
(20% hydrochloric acid and 40% hydrofluoric acid mixed at a volume
ratio of 1:2) under a 70 °C water bath condition, alternating stirring
multiple times to remove inorganic minerals such as carbonates and
silicates. The acid-washed samples were washed with deionized water
to near neutrality and then placed in a 105 °C oven to dry to a
constant weight. Upon testing, the loss on ignition (LOI) of the
kerogen prepared in this experiment was 88%, satisfying the
regulatory requirement of an LOI greater than 75% specified in the
national standard.
2.3.4. STA-FTIR-MS Coupled Testing. Online monitoring of
product evolution was performed using a simultaneous thermal
analyzer (NETZSCH STA 449 F3) coupled with a Fourier-transform
infrared spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Nicolet iS50) and a mass
spectrometer (NETZSCH QMS403D). Approximately 10 mg of
extracted kerogen was heated from room temperature to 800 °C at 10
°C min−1 under air or nitrogen. The reaction purge-gas flow rate was
50 mL min−1, and the protective-gas flow rate was 20 mL min−1. The
evolved gases were carried to the FTIR and MS instruments for
synchronous detection. The MS outputs shown in Figures 9−13 are
raw, uncalibrated ion-current signals reported in amperes (A). They
are used to compare temperature-dependent signal profiles within
each monitored ion and are not interpreted as absolute concentrations, molar flow rates, or product yields. Because species-specific
response factors were not determined, direct quantitative comparison
among different species and elemental carbon balances were not
performed. The raw currents were not mathematically normalized;
therefore, the original physical unit (A) is retained on the figure axes
rather than relabeling the data as arbitrary units.

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Pyrolysis Behavior of Raw Oil Shale and Isolated
Kerogen

Thermogravimetric analysis shows that raw oil shale and
isolated kerogen exhibit three characteristic mass-change
stages, while the atmosphere influences the observed reaction
regions and profile shapes. In the intermediate-temperature
region, the principal mass loss under air occurs at
approximately 250−600 °C, compared with approximately
300−600 °C under nitrogen in the present experiments. These
intervals are reported as observed atmosphere-dependent
features and are not assigned statistical significance because
only duplicate measurements were available.
This difference is particularly evident for isolated kerogen
(Figure 4a), for which the onset of the principal mass-loss
region under air occurs approximately 50 °C earlier than under
nitrogen. Under nitrogen, kerogen shows a main mass-loss
peak near 442 °C and a mild endothermic response, whereas
heating in air produces two mass-loss features accompanied by
a pronounced DSC exotherm between approximately 300 and

The OFW and DAEM methods were applied to compare the
apparent kinetic behavior of the overall mass-change process
(Figures 5 and 6). The resulting activation-energy estimates
vary with conversion, reflecting the changing contributions of
overlapping reactions as heating proceeds. Under nitrogen, the
process is dominated by thermal decomposition; under air,
oxygen uptake, oxidative decomposition, volatilization, and
combustion may contribute concurrently. The apparent
parameters obtained under the two atmospheres therefore do
not necessarily represent the same reaction network and
should not be interpreted as directly comparable intrinsic
bond-cleavage barriers23,24 (Figure 7).
The apparent activation-energy values obtained under air are
lower than those obtained under nitrogen over much of the
investigated conversion range. This difference characterizes the
distinct overall conversion processes under oxidative and inert
conditions; it does not by itself demonstrate that oxygen
lowers the intrinsic activation barrier of thermal cracking.

3.3. Evolution of Gaseous Products and Reaction
Pathways

3.3.1. Release Pattern of H2. As shown in Figure 9, the
characteristic H2 signal appears at lower temperatures under air
(approximately 250−600 °C, with a maximum near 330 °C)
than under nitrogen (approximately 300−800 °C, with a
maximum near 450 °C). Several pathways could contribute,
including oxidation-associated fragmentation, dehydrogenation, reactions involving partially oxidized intermediates,
and secondary reactions among H-containing and oxygencontaining species. Thermodynamically, the exothermic
water−gas shift reaction (CO + H2O ⇌ CO2 + H2) is
increasingly favored toward CO2 and H2 as temperature
decreases and could contribute within part of the investigated

3.3.4. Release Pattern of CO2. Under air, a pronounced
CO2 signal develops mainly within the principal oxidative
mass-loss region (Figure 12), whereas the nitrogen experiment
exhibits a weaker and broader profile. The enhanced signal
under air is consistent with oxygen-involving decomposition
and oxidation reactions. In the main nitrogen-pyrolysis interval
of approximately 300−600 °C, CO2 is attributed primarily to
thermal decarboxylation and decomposition of intrinsic
organic oxygen-containing functionalities, including carboxyl-,
carbonyl-, and ester-containing structures.25 Carbonate-mineral decomposition, if present, would be expected mainly at
higher temperatures and cannot be resolved here because
mineralogical characterization was not performed. The
uncalibrated MS data do not permit quantitative source
apportionment or closure of a carbon balance.
3.3.5. Release Pattern of CnHm. The light-hydrocarbon
ion-current profiles also vary with atmosphere (Figure 13).
Under air, characteristic signal features occur mainly between
approximately 250 and 600 °C, with a maximum near 430 °C
for the most responsive monitored ion. These earlier features
are consistent with atmosphere-dependent changes in
fragmentation and secondary reaction behavior. Because the
monitored ions have different response factors and the signals
were not calibrated, the data do not establish absolute
hydrocarbon yields or a unique precursor-to-product pathway.
FTIR spectroscopic analysis in conjunction with the mass
spectrometry results, FTIR was utilized to qualitatively
characterize the gaseous products. The assignments of FTIR
absorption peaks are presented in Table 3.
Figures 14 and 15 provide qualitative FTIR evidence for
atmosphere-dependent evolved-gas compositions. Under air
(Figure 14), bands assigned to H2O and CO2 become
prominent near 400 °C, while hydrocarbon-region bands are
comparatively weak. Under nitrogen (Figure 15), C−H-related
bands are evident during the principal decomposition interval.
The listed peak positions are treated as apparent maxima for
qualitative assignment; modest differences between atmospheres may reflect concentration, overlapping bands, baseline
treatment, spectral resolution, and gas-transfer conditions
rather than direct evidence of a changed molecular environment.
3.3.6. Unified Rule of Product Evolution. Taken
together, the MS and FTIR observations indicate an
atmosphere-dependent competition among product formation,
release, and secondary reaction. Air produces earlier oxidative
mass-change and stronger relative H2O/CO 2 detector
responses, while hydrocarbon-related signals differ from
those under nitrogen. These profiles are consistent with
oxygen altering both primary fragmentation and secondary
consumption; however, the present data do not quantify
absolute yields, demonstrate stoichiometric conversion of CH4
or other hydrocarbons into CO2, or uniquely identify
individual reaction pathways.

4. CONCLUSION
This study compared the atmosphere-dependent thermal
behavior, apparent kinetic parameters, and evolved-product
signal profiles of Maoming oil shale and extracted kerogen
under air and nitrogen. The principal conclusions are as
follows:
Atmosphere-dependent thermal behavior: In the present
experiments, the principal mass-loss region began approximately 50 °C earlier under air than under nitrogen, and
kerogen exhibited a pronounced exothermic response under
air. The apparent OFW and DAEM parameters describe
different overall mass-change processes under oxidative and
inert conditions and cannot be used to claim that air directly
lowers the intrinsic activation barrier of thermal cracking.
Product-evolution profiles: The uncalibrated MS signals and
qualitative FTIR spectra show earlier and/or altered H2, H2O,
CH4, CO2, and light-hydrocarbon evolution features under air.
