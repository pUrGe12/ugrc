ABSTRACT: To address the critical challenges of high energy
consumption and low efficiency in conventional hydroquinone
(HQ)−catechol (CAT) purification, this study presents a
systematic computational investigation of vacuum distillation via
Aspen Plus. Leveraging HQ and CAT’s thermal sensitivity and
nonazeotropic behavior (40.0 °C boiling point difference at
101.325 kPa), an accurate thermodynamic framework is built using
the NRTL (non-random two-liquid) activity coefficient model,
with parameters calibrated via UNIFAC and the Aspen database. A
vacuum distillation configuration (column, condenser, reboiler) is
initialized with 100 kg·h−1 feed flow, 100 °C inlet temperature, and
50 kPa system pressure, with rigorous vapor−liquid equilibrium
and mass transfer simulations conducted via the RadFrac module.
Systematic single-factor analysis of core variables (pressure, theoretical plates, feed stage, reflux ratio) is performed, with purity,
recovery, and specific energy consumption as metrics. Integrating sensitivity analysis, response surface methodology (RSM), and
multiobjective optimization, a predictive model identifies optimal operating conditions: 10 kPa operating pressure, 16 theoretical
plates, 11th-tray feed position, 3.143 mass reflux ratio, and an optimized distillate-to-feed (D/F) ratio of 0.092 (reduced from the
industrial baseline D/F ratio of 0.121). These optimal conditions yield 99.2% hydroquinone (HQ) purity, 99% catechol (CAT)
purity, 98.5% HQ recovery, and 97.8% CAT recovery, with core distillation energy consumption reduced by 23.7% (23.2% for the
full process, including vacuum pumps) versus the industrial baseline operating condition.

1. INTRODUCTION
Hydroquinone (HQ, 1,4-dihydroxybenzene) and catechol
(CAT, 1,2-dihydroxybenzene) are essential organic chemical
feedstocks utilized extensively in pharmaceuticals, dyes, rubber
additives, and other industrial applications.1 Hydroquinone is a
crucial precursor in the development of photographic
developers and polymer antioxidants,2,3 while catechol serves
as a vital intermediate in the synthesis of fragrances, pesticides,
and bioactive molecules.4 As phenolic isomers, the two
compounds share highly similar molecular structures, as
shown in Figure 1, which leads to nearly identical
physicochemical properties and poses significant challenges for
their efficient separation. However, industrial processes
frequently produce these compounds as mixed products,
necessitating efficient separation methodologies to improve
product quality and cost-effectiveness. Conventional separation
techniques, such as solvent extraction5−7 and crystallization8 are
hindered by inherent limitations, including suboptimal separation efficiency, excessive energy consumption, and environmental concerns associated with solvent usage. Consequently,

through its advanced thermodynamic databases and detailed
unit operation modules (e.g., RadFrac).26,27 Complementing
this, Response Surface Methodology (RSM) provides a
statistical framework to establish nonlinear relationships

While previous studies have explored individual separation

systematic investigations combining Aspen Plus-based vacuum

constraints of multitower cascaded industrial processes�such

work addresses these gaps by delivering three key novel
contributions: (1) the first rigorous simulation of a single
tower (within a multitower cascaded vacuum distillation
process) for HQ-CAT separation from mixed phenol extraction

rigorously modeled via the RadFrac module of Aspen Plus, with

lower the boiling point of mixtures, thereby facilitating gas−

quality and yield.31 Additionally, decreased operating pressure
increases the relative volatility between components, improving
the thermodynamic driving force for distillation-based separation. According to Raoult’s32 and Dalton’s law,33 under the gas−
liquid equilibrium, the partial pressure of component (i) in the

section systematically introduces the principles, advantages and
limitations of typical optimization methods for distillation
processes, including the grid search method, AI-driven
optimization methods, and the RSM method adopted in this
study.

global optimization method. Its core principle is to divide the
value interval of each optimization variable into several equal
gradient levels, and then combine all levels of all variables to
form a complete variable space traversal scheme. The optimal

solutions with strong nonideal behavior.36,37 Grounded in the

solution is obtained by simulating all combined working
conditions and comparing the response values. The advantage
of grid search is that the principle is simple, there is no need for
complex mathematical modeling, and there is no risk of falling
into a local optimal solution; however, its core limitation is the
“curse of dimensionality”: for an optimization problem with n
variables, if each variable is divided into m levels, mn simulation
trials are required. For the five continuous optimization variables
in this study, even if only 10 levels are set for each variable, 105
rigorous process simulations are required, which will bring
extremely high computational cost and long calculation time. In
addition, grid search can only obtain the optimal solution within
the set gradient interval, and cannot accurately locate the
extreme point of the continuous response surface, resulting in
limited optimization accuracy.
2.4.2. AI-Driven Optimization Methods. In recent years,
AI-driven16,41 optimization methods have been widely developed in the field of complex chemical process optimization,
among which AutoML, gene expression programming and metalearning are the most representative methods:
AutoML (Automated Machine Learning): AutoML realizes
the automatic selection of machine learning models, automatic
tuning of hyperparameters, and automatic optimization of
feature engineering, which avoids the bias caused by manual
experience in traditional machine learning modeling. For highdimensional and ultracomplex distillation systems, AutoML can
construct high-precision proxy models with strong generalization ability; however, its model training requires a large
number of labeled sample data, which is not suitable for the
scenario in this study where the single simulation cost is high and
it is difficult to generate massive samples.
Gene Expression Programming (GEP): GEP is an evolutionary algorithm that combines the advantages of genetic
algorithm and genetic programming, which can automatically
identify the structural relationship between input variables and
output responses, and construct an explicit mechanism model.
GEP has significant advantages in the modeling of complex
nonlinear systems with unknown mechanism, but in this study,
we have completed the rigorous mechanism modeling of the
distillation process through Aspen Plus, and there is no need for
additional model structure identification, so the application of
GEP will bring unnecessary computational complexity.
Meta-Learning: Meta-learning, also known as ″learning to
learn″, realizes fast optimization of new tasks by learning the
common laws of a large number of similar tasks. For the
optimization of multiseries distillation processes, meta-learning
can greatly reduce the number of samples required for
optimization; however, this study focuses on the optimization
of a specific hydroquinone-catechol vacuum distillation system,
and there is a lack of a large number of similar pretrained tasks,
so the advantages of meta-learning cannot be fully utilized.
2.4.3. Response Surface Methodology (RSM). Response
Surface Methodology (RSM) is a statistical optimization
method that combines experimental design and mathematical
modeling. Its core principle is to select representative variable
combination working conditions through reasonable experimental design, obtain the response values corresponding to
each working condition through simulation or experiment, and
then use multiple regression fitting to construct a quantitative
nonlinear relationship between the optimization variables and
the response values, and finally obtain the optimal process
parameters through the response surface model. Compared with
grid search and AI-driven methods, RSM has the following core

Article

advantages for the research scenario of this study: (1) high
computational efficiency, where only a small number of
simulation trials are required to construct a high-precision
response surface model, which greatly reduces the total
computational cost; (2) strong interpretability, where the fitted
quadratic polynomial model has an explicit mathematical
expression, which can clearly explain the interaction between
different operating variables, rather than a “black box” model;
(3) suitable for thermally labile systems, where reducing the
number of simulation iterations can avoid the cumulative error
of simulation caused by repeated high-temperature residence
time calculation of thermally sensitive materials. Based on the
above advantages, this study selects Box−Behnken Design
(BBD) combined with RSM as the core optimization method.

4.1. Influence of Operating Pressure on Separation Effect

A series of simulations were conducted to investigate the impact
of operating pressure on separation efficiency, with pressure

Through in-depth mechanism analysis, the core reasons for
the above unexpected deviation phenomenon are clarified as
follows:
Nonideal vapor−liquid equilibrium behavior of the system:
The hydroquinone-catechol system has strong intermolecular
hydrogen bond interaction, and the activity coefficient of the
system changes sharply under extremely low pressure and low
temperature conditions, leading to the deviation of the vapor−
liquid equilibrium ratio from the ideal linear trend. This change
amplifies the influence of reflux ratio on the vapor−liquid
circulating load in the column, resulting in a nonlinear increase
in energy consumption.
Pressure drop effect in the packed column: Under extremely
low vacuum conditions, the proportion of pressure drop in the
packed column to the overhead operating pressure increases
significantly (from 8% under 10 kPa to 22% under 3 kPa),
leading to a significant increase in the pressure difference
between the top and bottom of the column. The bubble point
temperature of the column bottom rises sharply with the
increase of pressure, which directly leads to a nonlinear surge in
the reboiler heat duty.
Physical property change of the thermally labile system:
Under extremely low pressure, the density difference between
the vapor and liquid phases in the column decreases
significantly, leading to the distortion of the vapor−liquid load
distribution in the column, and the mass transfer efficiency of the
structured packing decreases. To achieve the same separation
effect, a higher reflux ratio is required, which forms a positive
feedback of energy consumption increase, leading to the
unexpected sharp rise of energy consumption.
This deviation phenomenon also has important guiding
significance for industrial operation: the optimal operating
pressure range of this system is 5−10 kPa. This pressure range
not only avoids the nonlinear sharp increase of energy
consumption under ultrahigh vacuum (<5 kPa), but also
controls the maximum temperature in the column below 180
°C, which effectively reduces the risk of polymerization and
deterioration of the thermally labile hydroquinone and catechol
system.

5.1. Optimization Objectives and Methods

To minimize energy consumption while maintaining product
purity and recovery rate, an integrated optimization strategy
combining sensitivity analysis (SA) and response surface
methodology (RSM) was employed. Considering alternative
optimization approaches widely reported in chemical process
optimization, basic grid search, AutoML, gene expression
programming and meta-learning were also carefully evaluated
in this work. Grid search suffers from the curse of dimensionality

for multivariable continuous optimization, requiring massive
simulation runs and extremely high computational cost. AIbased methods such as AutoML and meta-learning rely on a
large number of labeled data sets for model training, which is
impractical for this thermally labile system with high single
Aspen Plus simulation cost. Gene expression programming is
more suitable for mechanism model structure identification,
while rigorous thermodynamic and process modeling has
already been completed in this study, making those alternative
methods less applicable. Therefore, SA-RSM was finally selected
to balance calculation accuracy and computational efficiency.
SA was first conducted to quantify the impact of each
operating parameter (e.g., reflux ratio, feed stage, pressure) on
the objective function, identifying key variables with the highest
sensitivity indices. Subsequently, RSM was applied to construct
a quadratic regression model linking these critical factors to the
response variables (energy consumption, purity, recovery). The
model was validated using ANOVA (F-value = 12.8; p < 0.001)
and optimized via numerical search, yielding a 15.3% reduction
in specific energy consumption (from 0.124 to 0.105 kwh/kg)
while meeting purity targets (catechol ≥ 0.99; hydroquinone ≥
0.99). This synergistic approach efficiently balances thermodynamic rigor with practical process economics.
All simulation and optimization calculations in this work were
performed on a workstation with Intel Core i7-14700K CPU
(3.4 GHz, 20 threads) and 64 GB DDR5 memory. The single
steady-state simulation convergence time of the distillation
column is about 12−18 s; the total computational time for
sensitivity analysis and RSM experimental design runs is
approximately 14 min; model regression, ANOVA and
numerical optimization consume around 45 s; the whole
modeling-to-optimization workflow takes about 2.5 h in total.
By contrast, grid search with equivalent optimization accuracy
would require over 100 h of computational time, further
demonstrating the efficiency advantage of the adopted SA-RSM
strategy.

5.7. Optimization Results

Regression analysis of simulation results yielded a quadratic
model relating objective functions (purity, recovery, energy
consumption) to operating parameters. Solving this model
identified optimal conditions: 10 kPa operating pressure, reflux
ratio of 3.143, and feed stage at the 11th tray.
Under these conditions, the overhead catechol purity reached
0.99, while bottoms hydroquinone purity achieved 0.992.
Corresponding recovery rates were 98.5% (catechol) and
97.8% (hydroquinone), with a reboiler heat duty of 12.175
kW. Compared to initial simulation conditions, these results
represent improvements of 16.8% in catechol purity, 1.8% in
hydroquinone purity, and 19.3% in energy efficiency (reduction
in specific energy consumption from 0.152 to 0.124 kWh/kg).

6.2.1. Key Assumptions of the Proposed Methodology. The simulation and optimization methodology
proposed in this study is based on the following core
assumptions, which are the preconditions for the establishment
of the model and optimization results:
6.2.1.1. Steady-State Operation Assumption. The simulation is based on the ideal steady-state operation of the
industrial process, without considering the dynamic disturbances in actual industrial production, such as feed flow fluctuation,
feed composition fluctuation, ambient temperature change, and
equipment performance attenuation during long-term operation.
6.2.1.2. Mass Transfer Efficiency Assumption. The mass
transfer efficiency of the structured packed column is calculated
by a constant height equivalent to a theoretical plate (HETP) of
0.4 m, without considering the change of packing mass transfer
efficiency under different vapor−liquid load conditions, and the
influence of nonideal flow such as wall flow and entrainment on
mass transfer efficiency.
6.2.1.3. Adiabatic Operation Assumption. The distillation
column is assumed to operate under adiabatic conditions,
without considering the heat loss of the column body to the
external environment during actual industrial operation.
6.2.1.4. Phase Equilibrium Assumption. The vapor and
liquid phases on each theoretical plate are assumed to be fully
mixed and reach complete thermodynamic phase equilibrium at
the outlet of the theoretical plate, without considering the mass
transfer resistance and nonequilibrium effect in the actual
vapor−liquid mass transfer process.
6.2.1.5. Feed Stability Assumption. The feed stream is
assumed to be saturated liquid with stable composition and flow
rate, without considering the influence of trace impurities in the
actual industrial feed on the phase behavior and separation
effect.
