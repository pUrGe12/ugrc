Immobilizing visible-light-responsive photocatalysts within recoverable porous architectures requires
a balance among active-phase retention, interfacial accessibility, and photon exposure. Here, Cu2O–Agcontaining domains were immobilized on a three-dimensional loofah scaﬀold through an acrylicassisted strategy to construct a recoverable Cu2O–Ag@ACR/LF-3D monolith integrating a lignocellulosic
sorptive framework, a polymeric immobilization interface, and visible-light-responsive domains. FTIR,

phase, retention of crystalline Cu2O, spatial distribution of Cu- and Ag-containing domains, and an

leached species alone. Response surface methodology identiﬁed optimum conditions at pH 5.94, an
initial dye concentration of 68.07 mg L−1, and a monolith dosage of 0.71 g, yielding a predicted
decolorization eﬃciency of 98.14% and an experimentally validated value of 96.43%. TOC decreased by

sorptive interfaces and supports for immobilized photoactive

reactive domains.7–9 Compared with slurry photocatalysts, these

by restricted light penetration, transport resistance, nonuniform active-phase deposition, and structural or surface
changes during repeated aqueous operation.10–12

hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and related
interfacial forces.17–22 Loofah-based materials have therefore
been investigated as adsorbents, biocarriers, and structured
supports for water-treatment applications.23–27 Nevertheless,

1. Introduction
Textile dyeing and nishing processes generate persistent
colorants that are oen poorly biodegradable and diﬃcult to
remove by conventional biological treatment.1–3 In light-driven
treatment systems, performance depends not only on semiconductor activity but also on dye-surface aﬃnity, photon
penetration, accessibility of photoactive domains, and interfacial mass transfer.4 These coupled factors have motivated the
development of structured visible-light-responsive materials
that combine photochemical functionality with sorptive accessibility, structural integrity, and straightforward recovery.
Lignocellulosic biomass is an attractive platform for such
structured materials because of its hierarchical porosity, surface
oxygen functionalities, and suitability for monolithic shaping.5,6

Cuprous oxide (Cu2O) is a visible-light-responsive semiconductor with a narrow band gap of approximately 2.0–2.2 eV,
but rapid charge-carrier recombination and the instability of
near-surface Cu(I) species can restrict its performance during
prolonged aqueous irradiation.8,9 Coupling Cu2O with Ag has
been widely investigated to modify its optical and interfacial
charge-transfer behavior.31–35 Although plasmonic enhancement and interfacial carrier transfer are frequently invoked for
Ag-containing semiconductor systems, these eﬀects depend
strongly on particle size, spatial contact, composition,
morphology, and irradiation conditions and therefore cannot
be inferred from decolorization data alone.36 Most Cu2O–Ag
photocatalysts have nevertheless been studied as dispersed
particles, nanoscale suspensions, or conventional coated
substrates, whereas their incorporation into recoverable porous
biomass-polymer monoliths remains comparatively less
explored.37,38
A key materials challenge is therefore to immobilize Cu2O–
Ag-containing domains without excessively compromising pore
accessibility or photon exposure. Structured supports can
facilitate catalyst recovery and limit particle loss, but excessive
or non-uniform coatings may mask active sites, attenuate incident light, and increase transport resistance.39,40 Acrylic-based
matrices oﬀer a practical route for consolidating lignocellulosic scaﬀolds and promoting adhesion between organic
supports and deposited functional phases.41–44 Polymer-assisted
coating strategies can also improve mechanical integrity and
processability, while polar groups at the polymer–water interface may contribute to local dye association.45–50 What remains
unclear is whether Cu2O–Ag-containing domains can be
immobilized within a biomass-polymer monolith while
preserving porous accessibility and visible-light response, and
whether such an architecture can retain suﬃcient active-phase
stability during repeated aqueous operation. The unresolved
issue is therefore not simply photocatalytic activity, but the
balance among active-phase immobilization, interfacial dye
accessibility, photon exposure, and metal retention within
a recoverable structured material.

was constructed through sequential in situ formation of Cu2O–
Ag-containing domains followed by acrylic-assisted immobilization on a three-dimensional loofah scaﬀold. FTIR, XRD, SEMEDX mapping, and UV-vis diﬀuse reectance spectroscopy were

optical characteristics of the material. Dark-adsorption and
photolysis controls were employed to distinguish sorptive
uptake from irradiation-stage photodecolorization, while
apparent pseudo-rst-order rate constants were used only as
comparative kinetic descriptors. Response surface methodology
was applied to dene the operating region, with ANN used as
a complementary nonlinear assessment rather than as an
independent validation tool. Reusability, post-cycle surface
evolution, elemental distribution, and dissolved Cu/Ag release
were further evaluated. The study therefore focuses on the

The collected loofah was air-dried, manually cleaned by
removing the seeds and outer rind, and cut into discs 15–20 mm
in diameter and 5–8 mm in thickness. The discs were treated for
60 min with 0.17% (w/v) lime water containing 4% (v/v) H2O2 at
a liquid-to-loofah ratio of 15 : 1. Aer treatment, the discs were
thoroughly rinsed with deionized water until the wash water
reached pH 6.5–7.0 and then air-dried. The resulting pretreated
three-dimensional loofah scaﬀold was denoted LF-3D.
LF-3D discs (approximately 0.50 g) were immersed in 100 mL
of CuSO4 solution (0.0075 mol L−1) and stirred at 25 ± 2 °C.
Subsequently, 100 mL of NaOH solution (0.015 mol L−1) was
added dropwise to induce in situ precipitation of Cu(OH)2 on
the scaﬀold. A 10 mL portion of ascorbic acid solution
(0.05 mol L−1) was then added dropwise as the reducing agent,
and stirring was continued for 20–30 min. During this stage, the
deposited phase gradually changed from deep blue to yellow–
orange or brick red, consistent with the formation of Cu2O.
Without isolating the Cu2O/LF-3D intermediate, 100 mL of
AgNO3 solution (0.0075 mol L−1) was introduced under lightshielded conditions, corresponding to an overall Cu : Ag molar
feed ratio of 1 : 1. During Ag+ addition, the remaining 10 mL of
ascorbic acid solution (0.05 mol L−1) was added intermittently
to promote reduction and deposition of Ag-containing
domains. Subsequently, 15 mL of the waterborne acrylic
dispersion was introduced as an immobilization/binding phase
to assist retention of the Cu2O–Ag-containing domains on the
LF-3D scaﬀold. The mixture was stirred for an additional 10–
20 min.
The resulting grayish-brown monolith was removed from the
reaction medium, thoroughly washed with deionized water
until the wash water reached pH 6.5–7.0, and dried at 60 °C for
24 h. The nal material was denoted Cu2O–Ag@ACR/LF-3D. The
sequential fabrication route is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Materials and methods

2.1. Materials
Luﬀa cylindrica was collected in Phu Tho Province, Vietnam.
Silver nitrate (AgNO3, $99.8%), copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate
(CuSO4$5H2O, $99%), and ascorbic acid ($99%) were
purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Germany. Calcium hydroxide
(Ca(OH)2) was obtained from a local supplier in Vietnam.
Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) were
of analytical grade and used as received. A waterborne acrylic
polyol resin supplied by Yu-Ta Resin Chemical Co., Ltd, Taiwan,
with a solids content of 70 ± 2% and a hydroxyl value of 70–
75 mg per KOH per g, was used as the immobilization/binding
phase. Synozol Navy Blue K-GF (lmax = 610 nm) was used as the
model reactive dye. Deionized water was used throughout the
experiments.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded in

with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDX) and
elemental mapping using a JSM-6510LV instrument (JEOL,

analyses were performed on selected regions of the fresh and

obtained from the adsorption data at high relative pressure.

with an integrating sphere, with BaSO4 used as the reference,

3.

Results and discussion

3.1. Structural, morphological, and optical characteristics of
Cu2O–Ag@ACR/LF-3D
3.1.1. Structural and optical characterization. The structural and optical characteristics of LF-3D and the Cu2O–Agcontaining materials were examined by FTIR, XRD, UV-vis
diﬀuse reectance spectroscopy, and Tauc analysis, as
summarized in Fig. 2.
The FTIR spectrum of LF-3D shows the characteristic
features of a lignocellulosic scaﬀold, including a broad O–H
stretching band at approximately 3342 cm−1, C–H stretching in
the 2872–3027 cm−1 region, and an intense C–O-related band at
1026–1102 cm−1.17,18,21 Aer incorporation of the acrylic phase,
Cu2O–Ag@ACR and Cu2O–Ag@ACR/LF-3D exhibit a more
pronounced carbonyl absorption at approximately 1726 cm−1
together with enhanced C–O/C–O–C features in the 1200–
1310 cm−1 region. These spectral changes support incorporation of the acrylic component within the immobilized
composite. Features retained in the low-wavenumber region are

© 2026 The Author(s). Published by the Royal Society of Chemistry

consistent with Cu–O-containing domains and support preservation of the Cu2O component aer immobilization.9,32,33
The XRD pattern of LF-3D displays the broad diﬀraction
feature characteristic of semicrystalline cellulose near 2q z 22°.
The Cu2O–Ag-containing sample exhibits distinct reections at
approximately 29.6°, 36.4°, 42.3°, 61.6°, and 73.7°, assigned to
the (110), (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes, respectively, of
cubic cuprite Cu2O.8,31,32 These reections remain detectable
aer acrylic-assisted immobilization and integration with LF3D, although their relative intensities decrease because of the
contribution of the predominantly amorphous organic matrix.
No clearly resolved reections attributable to CuO are observed
within the detection limits of the present measurements, supporting retention of the cuprite phase during composite fabrication. Metallic Ag reections are expected near approximately
38.1° and 44.3° in related Cu2O–Ag systems;8,31,32 however, these
regions are aﬀected by neighboring Cu2O reections and
background contributions. Therefore, metallic Ag cannot be
unambiguously identied from the present XRD data alone.
SEM-EDX and elemental mapping conrm the presence and
spatial distribution of Ag as an element, but do not determine
its oxidation state or crystalline phase.
The diﬀuse reectance spectra show distinct optical
responses following acrylic-assisted immobilization and integration with the LF-3D scaﬀold (Fig. 2C). These diﬀerences may
reect contributions from the Cu2O–Ag-containing domains,
acrylic phase, lignocellulosic matrix, and wavelengthdependent light scattering. The reectance data were therefore transformed using the Kubelka–Munk function to estimate
apparent optical band gaps.
Assuming a direct allowed transition, linear extrapolation of
the corresponding Tauc plots yielded apparent optical band
gaps of 1.67, 2.06, and 1.99 eV for Cu2O–Ag, Cu2O–Ag@ACR,
and Cu2O–Ag@ACR/LF-3D, respectively (Fig. 2D). The apparent
band gap increased following acrylic incorporation and
decreased slightly aer immobilization on the LF-3D scaﬀold.
These values are consistent with visible-light absorption and are
comparable to those reported for Cu2O-containing heterostructures.56,57 For comparison, a Cu2O/Ag-coated biochar
composite was reported to exhibit an optical band gap of
approximately 2.05 eV, compared with approximately 2.19 eV
for the corresponding Ag-free Cu2O-containing material.10
However, the observed diﬀerences should not be interpreted as
direct evidence of changes in the intrinsic electronic structure,
because wavelength-dependent scattering from the acrylic and
porous lignocellulosic matrices may aﬀect the Kubelka–Munk
transformation and Tauc extrapolation. The reported values are
therefore regarded as apparent optical band gaps.
Overall, FTIR supports incorporation of the acrylic phase,
while XRD conrms retention of crystalline Cu2O aer immobilization. Together with the apparent band-gap estimates from
diﬀuse reectance analysis, these results demonstrate that the
Cu2O–Ag@ACR/LF-3D architecture preserves the cuprite phase
while maintaining a visible-light-responsive optical prole.

Before evaluating the eﬀects of the operating variables,
component-resolved visible-light control experiments were
performed using LF-3D, ACR/LF-3D, Ag@ACR/LF-3D, Cu2O@ACR/LF-3D, and the complete Cu2O–Ag@ACR/LF-3D
monolith under otherwise identical irradiation conditions.
The UV-vis spectra aer 180 min of irradiation are compared
with the initial irradiation-stage spectrum in Fig. 5a.
The spectra represent directly measured absorbance values
of two-fold diluted samples without back-correction to the
undiluted samples. All samples retained the characteristic dye
absorption band centered at approximately 610 nm, but markedly diﬀerent decreases in absorbance were observed among
the material congurations. The corresponding Hphoto, 180
values were 7.62, 7.00, 17.00, 63.61, and 94.66% for LF-3D, ACR/
LF-3D, Ag@ACR/LF-3D, Cu2O@ACR/LF-3D, and Cu2O–
Ag@ACR/LF-3D, respectively. The similarly low responses of LF3D and ACR/LF-3D indicate that neither the loofah scaﬀold nor
the acrylic modication alone accounts for the pronounced
visible-light response. Ag@ACR/LF-3D exhibited only modest
photodecolorization, whereas Cu2O@ACR/LF-3D showed
substantially greater activity, indicating that Cu2O provides the
dominant visible-light-responsive contribution among the
tested component congurations. The further increase
observed for Cu2O–Ag@ACR/LF-3D indicates a benecial
contribution associated with Ag incorporation, consistent with
enhanced photocatalytic behavior reported for other Cu2Obased Ag-containing heterojunctions.60 These componentresolved controls establish diﬀerences in visible-light performance but do not, by themselves, demonstrate a specic Cu2O–

eﬀects of initial dye concentration, solution pH, and monolith

monolith.

3.4. Optimization of the photocatalytic process
3.4.1. Box–Behnken design and experimental responses.
The visible-light decolorization eﬃciency, Htotal, of Cu2O–
Ag@ACR/LF-3D was evaluated using a three-factor Box–
Behnken design within the response surface methodology
(RSM) framework. The investigated variables were solution pH
(A, 5.5–6.5), initial dye concentration (B, 65–75 mg L−1), and
monolith dosage (C, 0.65–0.75 g). A total of 17 experimental
runs, including ve center-point replicates, were performed to
estimate pure error and assess experimental repeatability52–54
(Table 2).
Across the 17 runs, Htotal varied from 48.73% to 98.98%,
demonstrating a pronounced dependence on the combined
operating conditions within the investigated design space. The
lowest response occurred at pH 6.5, an initial dye concentration
of 75 mg L−1, and a monolith dosage of 0.70 g, whereas the
highest response was obtained at the center condition of pH
6.0, 70 mg L−1, and 0.70 g. The ve center-point runs yielded
decolorization eﬃciencies of 96.04–98.98%, indicating good
repeatability and providing a reliable estimate of pure error for
subsequent model assessment.
The broad response variation observed over the relatively
narrow factor ranges indicates that decolorization is governed
by coupled and nonlinear eﬀects rather than by any single
operating variable. This behavior justies multivariable
response-surface analysis of the structured Cu2O–Ag@ACR/LF3D system.52,54

the spent surface appeared locally smoother and less densely
decorated, while the underlying porous framework remained
evident (Fig. 8c and d). These observations indicate preservation
of the three-dimensional scaﬀold together with local surface
modication, which may involve partial masking, redistribution, or restructuring of the deposited phase during repeated
aqueous treatment. Similar preservation of luﬀa-derived porous
frameworks aer modication and aqueous use has been
reported.29
Representative EDX spectra showed that both Cu and Ag
remained detectable on the monolith surface aer repeated

fractions varied markedly among the analyzed regions of the
fresh and spent samples, consistent with the heterogeneous
elemental distribution observed before cycling. The EDX data
were therefore interpreted only as local, semi-quantitative
indicators of residual surface composition and not as quantitative measures of bulk metal retention or loss.

working volume, these Cu concentrations correspond to
approximately 10.53, 12.10, and 17.73 mg of dissolved Cu,
respectively. Because the analyzed solutions were obtained from
diﬀerent monolith dosages rather than replicate measurements, the values were considered individually. Moreover, in

4. Conclusions
A recoverable Cu2O–Ag@ACR/LF-3D monolith was constructed
through acrylic-assisted immobilization of Cu2O–Ag-containing
domains on a three-dimensional loofah scaﬀold. The resulting
architecture integrated a porous lignocellulosic framework, an
acrylic immobilization interface, and visible-light-responsive
domains while preserving the recognizable monolithic structure. This combination enabled adsorption-assisted visiblelight photodecolorization, with the apparent kinetic response
reecting the coupled eﬀects of interfacial dye availability,
photon accessibility, and accessible photoactive domains.
Response surface methodology identied optimum conditions at pH 5.94, an initial dye concentration of 68.07 mg L−1,
and a monolith dosage of 0.71 g. Under these conditions, the
predicted decolorization eﬃciency of 98.14% agreed closely
with the experimentally validated value of 96.43%. TOC
decreased by approximately 62% aer 180 min, indicating
