ABSTRACT: As power systems pursue deep decarbonization while retaining dispatchable capacity, retrofitting the existing coalfired boiler fleet with carbon-free fuels such as ammonia is a pragmatic option, but practical implementation is constrained by the
coupled behavior of combustion stability, char burnout, and NO formation. In this work, a Chemkin-based chemical reactor network
(CRN) is developed for a 350 MW pulverized-coal boiler with staged air, combining perfectly stirred reactors for the main furnace
zones with detailed gas-phase kinetics and heterogeneous NO-char/soot reactions. The CRN is validated against staged drop-tubefurnace measurements for pure coal and 20% ammonia cofiring (dry 6%O2), predicting outlet O2, CO2 and CO within ∼10% and
capturing the measured NO rise. With ammonia introduced in the flame zone, simulations for 0−80% blending (20% step) show
near-linear reductions in CO2 and CO (−21%/−73% at 20%; −84%/−98% at 80%), while char consumption is delayed and the
NO/burnout peaks shift downstream. NO varies nonlinearly, increasing from 20% to 60% but decreasing at 80%. Reaction analysis
attributes this trend to competition for H/OH radicals: at high ammonia fractions, NH3 dehydrogenation depletes H/OH, promotes
N2 formation via NHi, and suppresses NO production. At 20% cofiring, injection location is critical: postflame injection minimizes
CO2/CO (−33%/−80%) but increases NO (∼+88%), whereas burnout-zone injection causes a much larger NO penalty (about
+571%). Among the 20% cofiring cases, flame-zone injection yields the lowest predicted NO emission. The outlet NH3 values are
conditional on the ideal-mixing assumptions of the CRN and are not interpreted as quantitative predictions of ammonia slip under
practical high-ratio operation. The results provide a chemical-kinetic interpretation of the effects of blending ratio and injection
location within the modeled conditions. The results relate the predicted outlet species concentrations to nitrogen-conversion
pathways under the prescribed network and operating conditions.

1. INTRODUCTION
With global greenhouse-gas emissions at record highs and the
emissions gap still unclosed, immediate, unprecedented and
sustained mitigation is now essential to limit climate risks and
keep returning to 1.5 °C by 2100 within the realm of
possibility.1 Coal still supplies just over one-third of global
electricity generation, so decarbonizing existing coal boilers
remains a near-term priority.2−4 Ammonia cofiring offers a
potential route to reduce direct CO2 emissions from existing
pulverized-coal boilers by replacing part of the coal-derived
thermal input with a carbon-free fuel.5−7 Its application,
however, requires attention to both fuel conversion and

NO emissions therefore depend on the conditions under which
ammonia reacts with coal-derived species, rather than on the
ammonia blending ratio alone.9−12 Understanding this
dependence is necessary for evaluating ammonia cofiring in
boilers operated with staged air.
At the mechanistic level, NO production in NH3-containing
flames is governed by a radical-sensitive oxidation network in
which NH3 is converted through NH2/NH/N intermediates,
and the competition between N2-forming and NO-forming
channels depends strongly on the local H/O radical pool and
the flame structure.9 Detailed-mechanism analyses identify the
HNO route as a major contributor to NO formation over wide
condition ranges, which makes OH-related reactions and
residence time in high-temperature zones high-leverage
sensitivities for NO prediction and control.9−11,13,14
Previous studies have examined ammonia−coal cofiring
under different blending ratios and air-staging conditions.
Experiments in staged drop-tube furnaces have investigated
combustion characteristics and fuel-nitrogen conversion,
demonstrating the importance of considering ammonia
addition together with the local combustion environment.15−17
Boiler-scale numerical studies have also examined the effects of
ammonia blending ratio and air distribution on combustion
and NO emissions.16−18 These studies provide a basis for
assessing the overall effects of ammonia substitution. At
constant total thermal input, however, increasing the ammonia
share also reduces the supply of coal-derived volatiles and char.
Interpretation of the resulting NO response therefore requires
consideration of both ammonia conversion and the accompanying changes in coal conversion. This motivates an
examination of the temperature, species evolution, and
nitrogen-reaction pathways in individual furnace zones as the
ammonia blending ratio changes.15,19,20
Recent high-fidelity numerical studies have further clarified
the roles of multistream mixing and species transport in
pulverized-coal/ammonia flames. Wen et al. developed a fourfuel-stream flamelet model for large-eddy simulation that
separately represents coal volatiles, char off-gases, ammonia,
and the pilot stream.21 Their subsequent carrier-phase direct
numerical simulation identified distinct coal- and ammoniadominated reaction regions and showed that, for the flame
investigated, NO was formed mainly in the coal-dominated
region, with volatile-N species making an important contribution.22 Later flamelet-based large-eddy simulations and
carrier-phase direct numerical simulations further showed that
differential diffusion can affect local thermochemical quantities
and NOx distributions, while its inclusion improves the
prediction of temperature and NOx mass fractions in the
flamelet framework.23,24 These findings provide a flame-scale
basis for interpreting why changes in ammonia blending ratio
and injection location can alter nitrogen-conversion behavior
in the present zonal model.
Ammonia injection location is another important variable
because it determines the reaction environment encountered
by the injected fuel. Experimental studies using pulverized-coal
furnaces and swirl burners have examined the influence of
ammonia injection modes on combustion and pollutant
emissions.8,25−27 Numerical investigations have likewise
considered different ammonia introduction methods in utility
boilers.8,28,29 Under staged-air operation, shifting ammonia
injection between furnace zones changes its exposure to
temperature and oxygen, its interaction with coal-derived
species, and the available downstream reaction sequence.

Consequently, outlet NO concentrations need to be
interpreted together with ammonia consumption and NO
formation and reduction within the network. Comparing
injection into the flame zone, postflame zone, and burnout
zone provides a means of examining these relationships under
the prescribed air-staging arrangement.15,19,20
Explaining these effects requires a description of the
reactions responsible for nitrogen conversion. Ammoniaderived nitrogen can proceed through competing NO-forming
and N2-forming pathways, while reactions involving char and
soot provide additional routes for NO consumption.25,30,31
Chemical reactor networks offer a tractable approach for
incorporating detailed chemistry into a zonal representation of
boiler combustion.25,32−34 By representing furnace regions as
interconnected ideal reactors, a CRN allows the evolution of
temperature and species to be examined along a prescribed
reaction sequence. Rate-of-production and reaction-path
analyses can then relate changes in NO to the elementary
reactions and nitrogen-conversion routes within selected
zones. This approach is suited to the present comparison of
ammonia blending ratios and injection zones, although spatial
mixing is represented through the prescribed network rather
than resolved directly.
The question addressed here is how changes in ammonia
blending ratio and injection zone alter the balance between
NO formation and removal within a prescribed staged-air
reactor network. Outlet concentrations alone do not identify
the furnace zones or reaction pathways responsible for an
emission change. A consistent comparison of zonal temperature, fuel conversion, and nitrogen chemistry is therefore
needed to connect the overall NO response with the
underlying reactions. The present study focuses on this
relationship for a selected coal and boiler configuration,
considering ammonia blending ratio and injection zone as the
principal variables.
In this study, a CHEMKIN-based CRN is developed for a
350 MW pulverized-coal boiler with staged air, incorporating
detailed gas-phase chemistry and heterogeneous reactions
involving char and soot. At constant total fuel thermal input
and with a prescribed air-staging arrangement, ammonia
thermal shares of 0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, and 80% are first
investigated with ammonia supplied to the flame zone.
Ammonia injection into the flame zone, postflame zone, and
burnout zone is then compared at a thermal share of 20%. The
analysis examines zonal temperatures, char consumption, O2
and NH3 evolution, and outlet CO2, CO, NO, and NH3
concentrations. Rate-of-production and reaction-path analyses
are used to identify the principal reactions governing NH3
consumption and NO formation and reduction, and to explain
their changes with blending ratio and injection zone. The
results provide a kinetic interpretation of the predicted
emission trends under the modeled operating conditions.

2. NUMERICAL SIMULATION METHODS
2.1. CHEMKIN Based Kinetic Computation Method
CHEMKIN provides numerical solvers for detailed chemical kinetics,
formulating the reacting system through “mechanism + conservation
equations” and organizing species, reactions, and property data in
modular files. At boiler scale, it is commonly coupled with a reactor
network (CRN) to resolve key pathways and radical pool evolution
within a tractable computational budget. The overall workflow is
summarized as mechanism/data input → reactor network modeling
→ solution → post processing, as shown in Figure 1.

2.2. Reactor Network Model and Chemical Reaction Model
for Coal-Fired Boilers
2.2.1. Reactor Network Model for Coal-Fired Boilers. When
simulating boiler combustion with CHEMKIN, the three-dimensional
furnace is reduced to a reactor network composed of multiple zerodimensional units based on the thermo-flow field and reaction
features, thereby enabling a tractable 0-D computation.35 A 350 MW
cogeneration boiler is used as the reference unit. The furnace adopts a
π type arrangement, with wall fired tangential main firing and four
corner tangential overfire/burnout air. The total coal feed rate is
166.14 t·h−1; the primary and secondary air temperatures are 343 and
603 K, respectively. Pulverized coal jets are entrained by the primary
air into the main combustion zone. With its lower temperature, the
primary air primarily supplies oxygen for volatile combustion during
the initial firing stage. Air staging is implemented by delivering 30%
and 70% of the secondary air to the flame and post flame zones,
respectively, thereby producing a fuel-rich (oxygen-lean) stage
followed by a fuel lean (oxygen rich) stage-an effective strategy for
NOx reduction. The recirculation branch shown in Figure 3 is
included as part of the prescribed reactor-network configuration. This
study examines ammonia blending ratio and injection location within
this configuration; it does not independently evaluate the effects of
introducing or removing recirculation. Of the total recirculated
stream, 20% is directed to the mixing zone and the remaining 80% to
the flame zone. These percentages describe the allocation of the
returned stream between the two zones, rather than the overall
recirculation ratio. In the burnout zone, the remaining unburned fuel
undergoes further oxidation after the addition of overfire air. The gas
composition at the outlet of the downstream section is taken to
represent the flue gas composition at the boiler back end. The
composition of the recirculated flue gas is not prescribed as a fixed
inlet composition but is obtained from the converged outlet of the
recirculation reactor for each case. Consequently, changes in oxygen
consumption downstream of the NH3 injection point can modify the
composition of the recycled stream and feed back to the mixing-zone
reactor. Accordingly, the boiler is partitioned into three principal
sections the main combustion zone, the burnout zone, and the
downstream section and the corresponding reactor network is shown
in Figure 3.
To simplify the computation, we assume that once injected, coal
volatiles undergo instantaneous devolatilization to the gas-phase; the
devolatilized gases mix homogeneously with the oxidizer. Effects of
ash and gas-phase preheating transients are neglected, so the boiler
combustion is treated as a continuous flow process. Reactors are
assigned according to the boiler zoning; the resulting reactor network
layout is summarized in Table 1. The main combustion section is
represented by five PSRs: C1-R1 (mixing zone), C1-R2 (flame zone),
C1-R3 (post flame zone), C1-R4 (reducing zone), and C1-R7
(recirculation zone). Two additional PSRs are used for the
downstream section, where the second downstream node, C1-R8,
represents the furnace exit. The use of a PSR network follows previous
studies of ammonia cofiring in pulverized-coal boilers, in which
interconnected PSRs were used to investigate the effects of ammonia
injection location and blending ratio on NO emissions.25,35 In the
present study, this representation is adopted to examine detailed
nitrogen chemistry within prescribed reaction zones. A PFR can
describe continuous streamwise evolution under a negligible-axialbackmixing assumption, whereas each PSR represents an idealized

2.1.1. Mechanism and Data Input. Gas-phase mechanism:
provides elementary reactions and thermochemical data (THERMO
polynomials) to evaluate reaction rates and equilibrium properties
both are prerequisites for any calculation. CHEMKIN prescribes strict
formats for mechanism, thermodynamic, and transport data to ensure
consistent coupling with its solvers.
Surface/multiphase mechanism: describes heterogeneous reactions
involving condensed phases such as char/soot (e.g., char/soot NO
reduction, gasification). Required inputs include site density, surface
coverage relations, and rate expressions for surface reactions.
Transport properties (Transport): include diffusion coefficients,
viscosity, and thermal conductivity, which close the energy and
species transport terms; typical databases are based on Chapman
Enskog fits and are supported by CHEMKIN parsers.
2.1.2. Reactor Network Modeling (CRN). Using perfectly
stirred reactors (PSRs) and plug flow reactors (PFRs) as basic
modules, the network is assembled in series/parallel according to
functional zones (mixing, main flame, post flame, downstream, etc.).
Each node is parametrized by volume/residence time and by inlet
composition and temperature, providing an equivalent representation
of “thermochemical subdomains” within the complex furnace. The
advantage of a CRN is that it retains detailed chemistry while
dramatically reducing the burden of flow/radiation solving, making it
well suited for mechanistic attribution and parameter sweeps; this
paradigm has been repeatedly adopted in combustion devices and
boiler studies.30
2.1.3. Solution and Postprocessing. The solver integrates the
steady/transient forms of the mass, energy, species, and equation of
state relations to obtain temperature and species fields for each node.
Rate-of-production (ROP) analysis is used to identify the elementary
reactions contributing to NH3 consumption and NO formation and
reduction, while reaction-path analysis is used to examine nitrogen
conversion among the participating species. In this work,
interconnected PSRs represent the prescribed reaction zones of the
boiler. The network describes the transfer of reacting streams between
these zones and the associated changes in their average
thermochemical states. These analyses are applied to selected reaction
zones to compare the competing pathways leading to NO and N2
under different ammonia blending ratios and injection locations.

2.2.2. Chemical Reaction Model. NH3-coal cofiring involves
complex chemistry among hydrocarbons, nitrogenous species, and a
variety of radicals. Reaction models specifically tailored for NH3
cofiring with pulverized coal are still under development. Beyond gasphase oxidation, gas−solid heterogeneous reactions must be
incorporated for realistic emission predictions. In particular, under
the high-temperature, oxygen -lean zone of coal boilers, the
heterogeneous reaction pathways of NO with NH3, coal char, and
soot remain an active research topic. Because NO abatement is
governed not only by reactions with hydrocarbon derived radicals but

also by heterogeneous reduction on soot/char, these solid−gas
reactions should be explicitly included in the kinetic model.
The calculations employed the detailed nitrogen-chemistry model
developed by Hashemi et al.36 for pulverized-coal combustion. Its gasphase subset, originally assembled from the detailed kinetic
mechanisms of Glarborg and coworkers, contains 97 gaseous species
participating in 778 gas-phase reactions. It integrates hydrocarbon
combustion chemistry, NOx formation chemistry, and heterogeneous
reactions involving coal char and soot, enabling simulation of both
NOx formation and reduction during coal combustion. Hashemi et al.
formulated and compared submodels for char + NO and soot + NO.
They showed that omitting soot/char chemistry leads to overprediction relative to experiments; hence, heterogeneous NO
reactions with char or soot are essential for credible emission
predictions. Wen et al.37 coupled a flamelet model with a quadrature63976

4.2.2. Reaction Path Analysis. Figure 15 summarizes the
NH3-related reaction routes considered in the analysis of
different blending ratios. The dashed lines highlight the routes
discussed for the 80% NH3 case; they should not be
interpreted as reactions that are absent from the mechanism
at lower blending ratios. In all cases, the first step of NH3
oxidation is reaction with O/H/OH radicals to form NH2.
Subsequently, NH2 and NH react with O/H/OH to yield Nbearing intermediates (HNO and N), which are further
oxidized to NO. Concurrently, NO reacts with N and NH to
form N2, and with NH2 to form NNH, which is subsequently
converted to N2. Thus, NH3 oxidation comprises two
intertwined branches: NO formation and NO reduction.
The effect of blending ratio should be considered in terms of
both nitrogen input and the location of fuel conversion. At
constant total thermal input, increasing the NH3 share
increases the input of externally supplied ammonia-N while
reducing the inputs of coal-derived volatile-N and char-N for
the same coal. These changes in nitrogen supply do not
directly determine their respective contributions to outlet NO,
because the nitrogen-containing intermediates participate in
coupled formation and reduction reactions. Moreover, the
NH3 thermal share is not equivalent to its share of the total
fuel-nitrogen input.
The existing results show that the reaction environment also
changes with blending ratio. At 20% NH 3 , the NO
concentration maximum occurs in the flame zone, whereas at
40% and 60% it occurs in the postflame zone. At 80%, NH3
conversion shifts predominantly to the postflame zone, while
the highest NO concentration occurs in the burnout zone.
Figures 13−15 relate these changes to reactions involving
HNO, N, and NHx species and to competing routes leading to
NO and N2. The lower NO concentration at 80% is consistent
with the reported decrease in local NO-forming rates and the
N2-forming routes discussed for this case. These results
support a qualitative interpretation of the changing nitrogen
chemistry, but do not establish source-specific conversion
efficiencies or quantitative pathway shares for the three fuelnitrogen sources.
Figure 16 summarizes the entry routes of fuel nitrogen and
their connections to the nitrogen-reaction network. Here,
ammonia-N refers specifically to nitrogen introduced with the
externally supplied NH3; volatile-N denotes coal-derived
nitrogen represented by HCN and NH3 in the volatile input;
and char-N denotes nitrogen retained in the char component.
These categories distinguish nitrogen sources at the fuel-input
stage.
After entering the gas-phase mechanism, externally supplied
NH3 and NH3 released with coal volatiles are represented by
the same chemical species. The HCN-related and NH3-related
routes also connect through shared nitrogen-containing
intermediates. Consequently, a reaction involving NH3, NHx,
HNO, or NO cannot be assigned exclusively to one original
nitrogen source using the present unlabeled mechanism. Figure

interact with the reaction network, rather than providing a
quantitative partition of the resulting NO or N2 among these

Char requires a further distinction between the conversion
of its bound nitrogen and its participation in heterogeneous
NO reduction. The presence of char in an NO-consuming
reaction does not mean that all nitrogen in the products
originates from char; nitrogen is also supplied by the reacting
NO. Char oxidation and char-mediated NO reduction are
therefore discussed separately. Their inclusion in Figure 16
describes the coupling between solid conversion and gas-phase
nitrogen chemistry, but does not establish the net contribution
of char-N to outlet NO.
Quantitative attribution of product nitrogen to ammonia-N,
volatile-N, and char-N would require an explicit nitrogensource tracking method, which is not included in the present
analysis. The discussion is therefore limited to source entry
routes, local reaction contributions, and their qualitative
changes with blending ratio.
The present reaction-pathway analysis was further compared
with the recent CP-DNS study of Wang et al.43 By
conditioning nitrogen-atom fluxes on the flame index and a
coal/ammonia mixing coordinate, Wang et al. identified
HNO→NO and HONO→NO as important NO-forming
branches, whereas NO consumption proceeded mainly
through N2-, N2O-, and NNH-forming pathways. In particular,
HNO + H → H2 + NO and HNO + O2 → HO2+ NO
contributed substantially to NO formation, while NO + NH2
→ H2O + N2, NO + NH → H + N2O, and NO + NH2 → OH
+ NNH promoted NO consumption. The present ROP results
are qualitatively consistent with this gas-phase reaction picture:
HNO- and N-related reactions make important contributions
to NO formation, whereas reactions involving NH and NH2
provide major gas-phase NO-reduction routes.
Nevertheless, the conclusions of the two studies are
complementary rather than identical. Wang et al. examined a
single early stage turbulent mixing layer and reported larger net
NOx contributions from the nonpremixed and coal-combustion-dominated regions. Char-N conversion and char-surface
reactions were not included in their analysis. In contrast, the
present CRN model covers successive furnace zones under
different NH3 blending ratios and injection locations and
includes heterogeneous char−NO reduction, although its
contribution is smaller than those of the leading gas-phase
reactions. Moreover, the nitrogen fluxes reported by Wang et
al. were conditioned on local combustion regimes and
normalized by the net NH3 consumption, whereas the present
results are based on zone-averaged species-specific ROP values.
Therefore, the pathway percentages cannot be compared
directly. Because nitrogen-source tagging was not applied in
the present model, Figure 16 distinguishes the entry routes and
reaction connectivity of ammonia-N, volatile-N, and char-N
but does not quantify their individual contributions.

temperature shown in Figure 20 suppresses the thermal-NO
contribution, while NHx and N intermediates promote NO
reduction to N2. Although NH3 oxidation produces a local NO
peak in the flame zone, the NO formed at this early stage
subsequently passes through the postflame and reducing zones,

where gas-phase reduction and heterogeneous reduction by the
remaining char can continue. In contrast, postflame injection
introduces NH3 near the temperature maximum, resulting in
rapid NH3 dehydrogenation and the formation of NHx, HNO,
and N intermediates. The resulting NO is formed farther
downstream and therefore undergoes a shorter subsequent
reduction sequence, with less residual char available for
heterogeneous removal. Consequently, postflame injection
produces a higher outlet NO concentration than flame-zone
injection.
From an operational safety standpoint, unburned NH3 in the
tail flue must be avoided. Figure 18 shows the NH3 mole
fraction at the reactor network outlet for different injection
locations; injecting NH3 in the burnout zone yields markedly
higher tail-end NH3 than the other cases. In this configuration,
intense coal combustion in the main section consumes
substantial O2, and the overfire air admitted to the burnout
zone is insufficient for the large NH3 load; consequently,
incomplete combustion products increase and CO, CO2, and
NO concentrations exceed those of the other injection cases.

influence of burnout-zone NH3 injection on an upstream

introduced, the O2 concentrations recover, and the furnace-exit
values remain relatively close among the three cases.

4.4.1. Rate of Production (ROP) Analysis. For each
injection case, the ROP analysis was performed in the reactor
into which NH3 was introduced, namely the flame-zone reactor
for Case 1, the postflame-zone reactor for Case 2, and the
burnout-zone reactor for Case 3. These reactors were selected
because they represent the initial local interaction between the
injected NH3 and the surrounding combustion products and
oxidizer. They also correspond approximately to the regions in
which the strongest NH3 conversion and the local NO peaks
occur. For a target species s, the contribution of elementary
reaction k was evaluated from its species-specific rate of
production, ROPs,k = νs,kqk, where νs,k is the net stoichiometric
coefficient of species s and qk is the net rate of progress of
reaction k. Positive and negative values denote the production
and consumption of the target species, respectively. All
elementary reactions directly contributing to NH3 or NO
were first extracted and ranked according to the absolute
magnitude of their ROP contributions. For each reaction, the
maximum absolute ROP among Cases 1−3 was used as the
ranking index. A common set of reactions was subsequently
plotted for all three cases so that the differences shown in
Figures 24 and 25 arise from the injection conditions rather
than from using different reaction sets.

5. CONCLUSIONS
The results are conditional on the prescribed reactor-network
structure, recirculation setting, and ideal-mixing assumptions.
Because recirculation was not independently varied, its
contribution to the predicted temperatures, species concentrations, and nitrogen-conversion pathways cannot be isolated
from the present calculations. No direct comparison with a
PFR-based representation was performed, and the sensitivity of
the conclusions to reactor type therefore remains unquantified.
The findings should be interpreted as kinetic trends within the
selected network rather than as fully resolved predictions of
boiler flow and combustion.
(a) Holding other conditions fixed, increasing the NH3
blending ratio yields an approximately linear decrease
in CO and CO2 at the reactor network outlet. Relative to
pure coal firing, CO2 falls by 21% and CO by 73% at
20% NH3, and by 84% (CO2) and 98% (CO) at 80%
NH3. These trends indicate source level decarbonization
via reduced coal consumption under NH3-coal cofiring.
(b) NH3 addition alters coal combustion. With higher NH3
fractions, the ignition energy requirement increases and
the overall temperature field declines, suppressing char
consumption and shifting the NO peak, the principal
NH3-conversion region, and the char-consumption
region downstream. At 80% NH3, heat release occurs
in the post flame rather than the flame zone, and the NO
peak moves to the burnout zone. The near-zero outlet
NH3 concentration predicted at the 80% blending ratio
has not been experimentally validated. This result is
conditional on homogeneous mixing and the high zoneaveraged temperature calculated for the postflame
reactor and may underestimate practical ammonia slip
because transport-related limitations are not resolved.
Therefore, the 40−80% cases should be interpreted as
parametric chemical-kinetic results within the CRN
rather than as evidence supporting industrial operation
at these cofiring ratios.
(c) The ROP and reaction-path analyses identify reactions
involving HNO, N, and NHx species as important
contributors to local NO formation and consumption.
Outlet NO increases as the NH3 thermal share rises
from 20% to 60%, but decreases at 80%, accompanied by
changes in the principal reaction zones and competing
NO- and N2-forming routes. These results describe the
coupled nitrogen chemistry of the mixed fuel; they do
not separately quantify the contributions of externally
supplied ammonia-N, volatile-N, and char-N to outlet
NO.

extremely low levels at the furnace exit, implying
essentially complete NH3 burnout. The NO peak
