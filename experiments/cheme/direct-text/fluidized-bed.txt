ABSTRACT: This study investigated the effects of the coexistence of P, Fe, and Na during the fluidized bed incineration of
municipal sewage sludge on agglomeration, defluidization, and heavy metal emissions. The results indicate that increasing the
temperature promotes the melting of eutectic compounds, promoting agglomeration and accelerating defluidization. Conversely,
using smaller bed material particle sizes or increasing the operating gas velocity enhances particle mobility and mixing, delaying
agglomeration. A comparison of the roles of P and Fe revealed that an increase in their content generally extended the defluidization
time. However, high P concentrations weakened the inhibitory effect of Fe, leading to a shorter defluidization time for the P/Fe/Na
ratio of 0.6/5/1 than for 0.4/5/1. Differences in heavy metal emission concentrations were negligible before defluidization; however,
emissions gradually increased once agglomeration began, with Cu and Pb concentrations increasing significantly after defluidization.
The Cu emission concentration was lower than that of Pb because of its higher melting and boiling points. Experimental results show
that Fe reacts with P to form high-melting-point FePO4, extending fluidized bed stability. Low Fe content allows P to form lowmelting-point compounds like NaPO3, increasing agglomeration risks. Therefore, to ensure long-term stability during cocombustion,
operators must maintain an excess Fe/P ratio (Fe > P).

1. INTRODUCTION

Elements such as S, Cl, Si, P, Fe, K, Na and alkaline earth
metals, have been reported to significantly affect agglomeration.1−4 The gradual accumulation of these agglomerates
during operation can result in bed defluidization, causing
unscheduled shutdowns,4−6 which can lead to incomplete
combustion and the release of secondary pollutants.7,8
Researchers posit that agglomeration stems from the intrinsic
tackiness of the bed material combined with interparticle
collisions.9 Accordingly, Sevonius et al.10 categorized bed
particle aggregation mechanisms into three distinct types.

In recent years, the progressive increase in sewerage connection
rates across various municipalities in Taiwan has led to a
significant increase in sewage treatment volumes and the
subsequent production of sewage sludge. Consequently, rapid
and efficient sludge disposal has become critical. Among various
disposal methods, incineration is considered a high-capacity
approach that offers rapid processing. Furthermore, the flue gas
generated during incineration can be utilized for energy recovery
via cogeneration (combined heat and power), whereas the
resulting bottom ash can be recycled as civil engineering
materials, such as sand or aggregates. However, municipal
sewage sludge contains elements such as P, Na, and Fe, which
can induce bed material agglomeration or even defluidization
after fluidized bed incineration, compromising treatment
efficiency and potentially leading to secondary pollution.

Existing research has focused on ash agglomeration during
coal combustion, estimating the influence of various elements on
agglomeration and defluidization by comparing the elemental
composition of postcombustion agglomerates with that of the
raw feedstock. However, a definitive correlation between specific
metal compositions and agglomeration has yet to be established
because of significant origin-based variation in the complex
metal composition of coal. Given that sludge has a high ash
content and a complex matrix containing P, Si, Fe, Ca, Al, and S,
it is highly susceptible to agglomeration and defluidization
during thermal treatment.16 The coincineration of sludge with
more complex waste streams can further exacerbate these issues.
Therefore, this study evaluates the specific impacts of key
inorganic constituents (P, Fe, and Na) on the agglomerationinduced defluidization process in coincineration systems. This
study expands upon the scope of the study conducted by Lin et
al.17 They established the effects of P, Na, and Fe using
simplified waste matrices. Although simulated waste reduces
interference from complex matrices, real municipal sewage
sludge contains intricate matrices, heavy metals, and a high ash
content, which can result in defluidization behaviors distinct
from those observed with simulated waste alone. Previous
investigations utilizing synthetic matrices have provided
fundamental insights into the chemical mechanisms of Na−
P−Fe interactions;17 however, the behavior of these elements in
actual municipal sewage sludge remains poorly understood
because of its intricate ash chemistry and high trace metal
content. Consequently, this study validated the effects of P, Na,
and Fe coexistence during the coincineration of municipal
sewage sludge and simulated waste. Furthermore, the
volatilization and emission behaviors of heavy metals, such as
Cu and Pb, during the process were explored, providing valuable
guidance for industrial-scale sewage sludge incineration.

2.2. Experimental Procedures
The pressure drop across the bed and gas velocity were measured using
a water manometer prior to the tests to determine the minimum
fluidization velocity (Umf) for the experimental process.18 Further, all
experiments were conducted at an operating velocity of 1.3× Umf to
maintain a stable fluidization state. Silica sand was utilized as the bed
material, and its density was approximately 2600 kg/m3. The silica sand
was sieved using ASTM standard sieves, with a primary particle size of
650 μm. The incineration temperature was maintained between 800
and 900 °C, as shown in Table 2.
Prior to each test, the incinerator bed was preheated to the target
temperature and the blower was activated to provide the necessary air
supply. The flow rate was regulated using a rotameter to maintain a
constant fluidization velocity of 1.3 Umf. Once the bed temperature
stabilized, feeding was started at a rate of one package every 20 s.
Agglomeration was monitored through visual observation and by
tracking pressure fluctuations using a manometer to identify the onset
of defluidization.
After agglomeration and defluidization, the fluidization quality of the
sand bed decreases, which may lead to poor combustion performance
and increased heavy metal release. Therefore, in this study, two metals
commonly found in waste, Cu and Pb, were added to the feedstock to
investigate the release behavior of heavy metals during agglomeration
and defluidization. In this study, the “emission concentration”
represents the mass concentration of heavy metals found in fly ash.
The total mass concentration of heavy metals in the flue gas stream
included both particulate-bound metals captured on the quartz fiber
filter and vapor-phase/gaseous metals captured downstream in the acid
impinger solutions. However, the concentration of gaseous heavy
metals in flue gas is relatively low,19 while most heavy metals are
distributed in fly ash through adsorption and nucleation processes.20
Therefore, the analysis of heavy metal dispersion focuses only on heavy
metals in fly ash to discuss the extent of heavy metal emissions. The fly
ash sampling procedure was primarily based on the U.S. EPA sampling
standard method 5. The emission concentration was calculated as the
heavy metal mass per gram of fly ash.
Fly ash was collected from the flue-gas sampling port every 4 min,
with each sampling interval lasting 2 min. Once defluidization occurred,
the event time was noted, and the bed materials were collected after the
furnace had cooled to room temperature. Subsequently, the collected
fly ash was subjected to microwave digestion and analyzed to determine
the Cu and Pb concentrations using inductively coupled plasma
spectrometer (ICP). The agglomerate samples were analyzed using
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDS) to characterize the morphology and elemental
distribution of the agglomerates.

2.1. Experimental Equipment and Feedstock Composition
Municipal sewage sludge sourced from a wastewater treatment plant in
southern Taiwan was used as the primary matrix. Following sampling,
the sludge was dried at 105 °C and stored. The baseline concentrations
of P, Na, and Fe were determined using inductively coupled plasma
(ICP) spectrometry following microwave-assisted digestion of the
sludge samples. Further, phosphoric acid (H3PO4), sodium nitrate
(NaNO3), and ferric nitrate (Fe(NO3)3) were employed as the
respective sources to adjust the elemental ratios of P, Na, and Fe within
the sludge.

Adjusting the excess air ratio during fluidized bed incineration
requires varying the primary airflow. However, this can alter the
operating gas velocity, which is a key fluidization parameter that
significantly affects agglomeration/defluidization. Figure 3a
illustrates the defluidization time distribution under different
operating gas velocities (1.1, 1.3, and 1.5× Umf). The results
indicated that increasing the operating gas velocity significantly
extended the defluidization time.
Increasing the gas velocity promotes the mixing and mobility
of the bed particles. Iwadate and Horio25 and Zhou and Li26
reported that higher fluidization velocities generate greater
interparticle collision forces (fragmentation forces), facilitating
the breakage of agglomerates and reducing their size. Similarly,
Xu and Zhu27 observed that increasing the gas velocity
minimized the agglomerated particle size. Therefore, a higher
gas velocity contributes to the disruption of agglomerates,
reducing the potential for defluidization.
Figure 3b shows the defluidization time for three different bed
material particle sizes. An increase in the bed material particle
size led to a corresponding decrease in the defluidization time.
Given that several parameters were fixed in this study, 650 μm
was selected as the primary particle size, and the gas velocity was
set to 1.3× Umf for the 650 μm bed at 800 °C.
The 775 μm bed particles are the largest and therefore require
a highest minimum fluidization velocity. At the operating gas
velocity 1.3× Umf, the 775 μm particles can be fluidized;
however, their movement is slow, which results in a lower
momentum. As viscous substances accumulate and the particle
size gradually increases, the bed particles transition into the
larger Geldart Group D category. At a fixed fluidization velocity,
the interparticle adhesive forces exceed the aerodynamic forces
exerted by fluid motion, leading to defluidization.24

current experiments. This discrepancy is attributed to the

feedstock consisted primarily of simulated wastes. In contrast,
the present study used real municipal sewage sludge to

Figure 4c shows the effects of simultaneously increasing the P

decrease in defluidization time. Although Figure 4b indicates

counteracts this benefit. A P/Fe/Na ratio of 0.6/5/1 resulted in
a shorter defluidization time than a ratio of 0.4/5/1. This implies
that the effect of high P concentrations weakens or overcomes
the stabilizing effect of high Fe concentrations.
Lin et al.17 reported that increasing the P/Na ratio extended
the defluidization time, suggesting that P exerted an inhibitory
effect on agglomeration. Conversely, the defluidization time
decreased with an increase in the Fe/Na ratio, implying that Fe
might compromise the operational stability. When all three
elements coexisted, the effect of P outweighed that of Fe,
resulting in defluidization times longer than those observed with
Na alone.
In contrast, the results of the present study regarding the
coexistence of these three elements indicate that higher contents
of both P and Fe in the sludge extended the defluidization time.
The impact of Fe observed in this study deviates from previous
findings. While increasing both the P and Fe concentrations

typically leads to a gradual increase in the defluidization time, P
diminishes the stabilizing effect of Fe when both are maintained
at high concentrations. High-temperature thermodynamic
studies by Horiguchi et al.28 and Grimm et al.12 demonstrated
that the addition of Fe preferentially captures reactive P species
to form stable, high-melting-point iron phosphate compounds
(such as FePO4, melting point of ∼1050 °C), thereby
significantly suppressing the melt formation and adhesiveness
of alkali phosphates. As given by the following equation, Fe
preferentially captures volatile P2O5 to form high-melting-point
FePO4.

When P is in stoichiometric excess (e.g., at a ratio of 0.6/5/1),
the surplus P overflows the iron-binding capacity. The excess P
first reacts with K and then with Na to form highly viscous lowmelting-point metaphosphates (e.g., KPO3 and NaPO3)12 and

Figure 5 shows the effect of various temperatures on heavy
metal emissions. The results indicate that higher temperatures
(e.g., 900 °C) yield slightly lower emission concentrations than
800 and 850 °C. Liu et al.29 found that the mechanisms
influencing heavy metal emissions during incineration include
bed material adsorption and the entrapment of heavy metals by
liquid eutectics. The volume of the molten eutectic compounds
increases with temperature. During sludge incineration, these
liquid eutectics come into contact with the sludge, allowing the
heavy-metal-containing ash residues to become embedded
within the molten species and reduce the volatilization of
heavy metals. However, once defluidization occurs, the contact
probability between the bed material and sludge decreases,
increasing heavy metal emissions.

velocities. At 1.5 Umf, the increased mobility of the bed material
