ABSTRACT: Draw solution chemistry is a design parameter in
forward osmosis (FO), governing hydraulic performance, organic
rejection, fouling dynamics, and process economics, yet these
dimensions are rarely evaluated together, particularly under
wastewater feed conditions. This study addresses that gap through
an integrated experimental, modeling, and techno-economic
framework using three draw solutions (NaCl, MgSO4, and sodium
acetate), each prepared at an initial osmotic pressure of 30 bar, and
two feed solutions (deionized water (DI) and real municipal
wastewater (WW)). Water flux (Jw), reverse salt flux (Js), and
chemical oxygen demand (COD) flux (JCOD) were monitored over
4 hours. Water fluxes ranged from 1.8 to 4.0 L m−2 h−1, and
substituting DI with WW reduced Jw by 8.1−46.2% depending on
draw solution chemistry. A dynamic MATLAB finite-difference model coupling concentration polarization with membrane transport
accurately reproduced experimental Jw and Js profiles, with Root Relative Mean Square Error below 1.9% and 6%, respectively. A
phenomenological COD sub-model partitioned organic matter dynamics into three pathways and introduced a dimensionless ratio
PCOD/Kfoul as a selectivity screening metric. Techno-economic analysis identified NaCl as the most economically viable draw
solution, achieving a levelized cost of water of USD 1.04 m−3. Monte Carlo analysis confirmed this advantage was statistically robust
with complete non-overlap of NaCl ’s 95% confidence interval and those of MgSO4 and sodium acetate. MgSO4 demonstrated
superior COD rejection, lower initial fouling accumulation, and more stable flux decay kinetics within the initial-to-intermediate
fouling regime examined, making it preferable where selectivity and effluent quality are prioritized.

1. INTRODUCTION
Forward Osmosis (FO), driven by the osmotic pressure
difference rather than applied hydraulic pressure, has attracted
sustained interest for wastewater treatment owing to its lower
membrane compaction, reduced specific energy consumption,
and potential for integration with renewable energy sources.1−3
The selection of the draw solution (DS) is the central design
variable in any FO system. It simultaneously governs the
osmotic driving force, the reverse solute flux that degrades feed
quality, the concentration polarization (CP) regime, and the
cost of downstream regeneration. Despite this centrality, no
study has yet compared draw solution chemistries across
hydraulic performance, organic rejection, fouling, and economics under real wastewater feed conditions within a single, unified
predictive framework.4−6
Three coupled phenomena constrain FO performance and
complicate DS comparison: Reverse solute diffusion, CP, and
fouling.4−6 Reverse solute diffusion, the leakage of DS
components into the feed solution (FS), reduces the osmotic
driving force and aggravates fouling.7,8 Currently, it is best

Because conventional experimental designs average these

Prior techno-economic assessments of FO have generally
been developed independently of draw-solution-specific transport behavior. Pure transport comparisons across multiple draw
solutions, such as those by Achilli et al.14 and Ge et al.,4 provide
no economic dimension, while existing economic analyses either
focus on a single draw solution or adopt literature-based flux
values rather than experimentally calibrated transport parameters.15−21 No prior study has simultaneously applied a
parameter-level calibration of levelized cost of water against
experimentally resolved Jw, Js, and Kfoul across three chemically
distinct draw solutions under real municipal wastewater feed
conditions. Accordingly, integrating experimental observations
with mechanistic modeling and parameter-calibrated economic
evaluation is essential for a quantitative and actionable draw
solution selection framework under realistic operating conditions.
This study addresses these gaps through an integrated threepillar framework applied to three draw solutions (NaCl, MgSO4,
and sodium acetate) prepared at identical initial osmotic
pressure. Each DS was tested against two FS (deionized water
and wastewater) to isolate the flux reduction and fouling
imposed by a complex feed matrix and to quantify their effects
on water flux (Jw), reverse salt flux (Js), and chemical oxygen
demand (COD) flux (JCOD). Simultaneously, a dynamic
MATLAB model, coupling ICP and ECP with membrane
transport equations was developed to predict the time-resolved
FO performance, thereby enabling mechanistic discrimination
between osmotic driving force losses and membrane-selectivity
limitations. A phenomenological COD sub-model was further
integrated into this framework to quantify organic matter
partitioning among three pathways: feed-side COD concentration enhancement arising from volume reduction by
transmembrane water extraction, transmembrane leakage, and
irreversible fouling deposition. A dimensionless ratio PCOD/Kfoul
was introduced as a single-parameter selectivity screening metric
for draw solution comparison, quantifying the relative
dominance of organic leakage over foulant deposition. Building
on these transport results, the experimentally measured fluxes
and fouling parameters were used to calibrate a technoeconomic framework, from which levelized cost of water
(LCOW) probability distributions were derived via Monte
Carlo uncertainty propagation. This framework establishes, to
the authors’ knowledge, the first parameter-level link between
membrane transport and process economics across multiple
draw solutions under real wastewater feed conditions.

2.2. Feed and Draw Solutions
Deionized water (DI), characterized by a conductivity below 2 μS cm−1,
and real municipal wastewater (WW), with a chemical oxygen demand
(COD) of 460 mg L−1 and an osmotic pressure of 2.5 bar were
employed as feed solutions. The wastewater was sourced from the
secondary clarifier effluent of a local municipal treatment plant, and was
pre-filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter before use. Full
physicochemical fractionation of the dissolved organic matter was
outside the scope of the present study. All mechanistic interpretations
relating to foulant-membrane interactions are therefore presented as
the most probable explanations consistent with the available COD flux
and mass-balance evidence, rather than as directly confirmed
mechanisms. All experiments used wastewater from a single batch,
collected on the same date from the same treatment stage, which
minimizes inter-batch variability.
Draw solutions were prepared to provide an initial nominal osmotic
pressure of 30 bar at 25 °C, using analytical-grade salts: NaCl (35.0 g
L−1), MgSO4·7H2O (71.8 g L−1), and sodium acetate (79.4 g L−1). The
three draw solutions were selected to represent three chemically
distinct classes: a monovalent ionic solute, a divalent ionic solute, and
an organic solute. This class-representative design enables systematic
attribution of performance differences to specific physicochemical
properties, including ion valence, hydrated radius, diffusivity, viscosity,
and molecular character. Other draw solutes were excluded because
they were outside the scope of the present study or incompatible with
the proposed RO-based regeneration framework. Nevertheless, the
proposed modeling and techno-economic framework can be readily
extended to other draw solutes within the same chemical class by
substituting the corresponding transport and economic parameters.
Equal initial osmotic pressure (30 bar for all three draw solutions)
was chosen to isolate draw solute chemistry as the variable under study,
removing osmotic driving force as a confounding factor and giving each
solution the same thermodynamic starting point. Differences in
resulting flux, selectivity, and fouling behavior then reflect how
efficiently each solute chemistry converts that shared driving force
into performance under real operating condition, so that kinetic and
transport differences between solute chemistries emerge directly in the
measured response.
All solutions were pre-filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter
before use. Each of the two feed solutions (DI, WW) was systematically
combined with each of the three draw solutions, yielding 6 distinct
experimental configurations.

measured COD, a blank correction was applied. The COD contribution
of the draw solute at each time point was calculated from the measured
conductivity-derived draw solute concentration and the theoretical
oxygen demand of acetate (1.07 g COD g−1), and subtracted from the
measured draw-side COD to isolate the contribution of permeated feed
organics. No correction was required for NaCl or MgSO4, which
register zero COD by the potassium dichromate method.
A fresh membrane coupon was used for each of the six experimental
configurations to eliminate any possibility of fouling history or
membrane compaction effects between runs. All coupons were taken
from the same manufacturing lot to ensure consistency of intrinsic
transport parameters across experiments. For each configuration, the 4
h experiment was repeated three times. Prior to each run, the
membrane was flushed with DI for 20 min; the same rinse was applied
post-experiment, and the coupon was stored wet until the next run.
The 4-hour experimental window was deliberately selected to
capture the initial-to-intermediate fouling and concentration polarization development phases, and during which comparative draw
solution performance is most clearly discriminated.23,24 Studies
employing CTA membranes with real municipal wastewater as feed
under comparable cross-flow conditions consistently report that the
transition from initial cake-layer formation to advanced irreversible
fouling, characterized by a measurable inflection in the flux decline
curve, occurs between 8 and 24 hours of operation.25,26

is negligible on experimental timescales. Therefore, the

3.2. Numerical Simulations

An explicit finite-difference scheme was implemented in
MATLAB under conditions identical to those of the experiments. Figure 1 provides a detailed flowchart of the FO process
modeling. After parameter initialization, the variables of the feed
and draw solutions were computed sequentially.
The interfacial concentrations were applied under a quasisteady-state assumption using a time step chosen so that the
profiles equilibrate almost instantaneously relative to changes in
bulk concentration. The water flux Jw was then estimated using a
bisection solver iterated to within 1% absolute error. With the
overall mass-balance equations, concentrations, volumes, and
physical properties were refreshed at each time step until the
final experimental time was reached. Solution properties were
estimated using empirical equations depending on solution
concentration and temperature.34 At each time step, the model
yields the reverse solute flux Js, as well as the full concentration
field across the membrane, providing a spatially resolved
description of solute distribution throughout the membrane
system.
The quality of the model was evaluated through the
coefficient of determination R2 and the root mean square error
(RMSE), relative RMSE (RRMSE), and maximum relative error
(MRE), computed between simulated and experimental
profiles.

A COD sub-model was embedded within this finite-difference
framework, with the temporal evolution of VF and VD supplied
directly from the hydrodynamic solver outputs at each time step,
thereby decoupling organic transport estimation from osmotic
pressure computations for the complex wastewater matrix.35
COD temporal evolution was governed by three distinct
mechanisms: (i) apparent concentration of COD in the feed
solution resulting from continuous water extraction across the
membrane; (ii) transmembrane leakage of dissolved organic
from the feed solution (FS) to the draw solution (DS) driven by
the transmembrane concentration gradient; and (iii) irreversible
removal of COD from the liquid phase due to membrane fouling
and adsorption onto the membrane surface or within its porous
structure. The following simplifying assumptions were adopted:
• COD behaves as a lumped scalar parameter integrating all
dissolved organic species grounded in two facts. Firstly,
measurable shifts in organic molecular-weight distributions are unlikely over the 4-h experimental period.
Secondly, the dense CTA active layer restricts permeation
to a specific low-molecular-weight fraction, represented
by the effective parameter PCOD;
• Both FS and DS bulk reservoirs are assumed to be
perfectly mixed in the bulk reservoir at all times;
• membrane properties and operating temperature remain
constant throughout the experiment;
• COD transport across the membrane is governed by an
apparent diffusion mechanism;
• Fouling kinetics follow a first-order dependence on feedside COD concentration, consistent with the initial-tointermediate fouling regime established in Section 2.3.
The coupled ordinary differential equation (ODE) system
(eqs 23−25) was integrated using the built-in MATLAB ODE
solver, and the global COD conservation condition (eq 26) was
enforced as an internal numerical stability diagnostic at each
iteration.
The two model parameters, PCOD and Kfoul, were identified
through a numerical calibration procedure designed to minimize
the discrepancy between simulated and experimentally observed
COD concentrations in both the feed and draw solutions over
time. The identification was carried out by systematically
evaluating the sum of squared residuals (SSR) between model
outputs and experimental measurements across the admissible
parameter space, selecting the parameter pair that yields the
global minimum of SSR. The admissible parameter space was
established using physically meaningful ranges drawn from the
FO organic transport literature: PCOD in [0.005, 0.5] L m−2 h−1
and Kfoul in [0.005, 0.5] L m−2 h−1.6,33,36
To assess parameter identifiability, the SSR surface was
evaluated over the full admissible [PCOD, Kfoul] parameter space
which was dilated by 10% on either side. For all three draw
solutions, the SSR landscape exhibited a single well-defined
global minimum with no evidence of a correlation ridge between
PCOD and Kfoul, confirming that the two parameters are
independently identifiable from the available COD data in
both compartments. It should be noted that PCOD and Kfoul are
empirical, membrane- and feed-specific calibration parameters
rather than intrinsic membrane transport coefficients. Their

4.2. Uncertainty Quantification and Sensitivity Analysis

The deterministic LCOW framework yields point estimates that
are conditioned on the mean experimental flux values but do not
account for the variability inherent in triplicate laboratory
measurements. To address this limitation, a Monte Carlo
uncertainty propagation procedure was implemented to translate experimental flux uncertainty directly into a probability
distribution of LCOW for each draw solution configuration.
At each of N = 10,000 iterations, the time-averaged water flux
Jw and reverse salt flux Js were independently sampled from
normal distributions defined by their experimentally measured
means and standard deviations across triplicate runs. All
remaining techno-economic parameters were held at their
nominal values, isolating the contribution of flux measurement
uncertainty to total LCOW uncertainty. Non-physical samples
were prevented by enforcing a lower bound of 0.01 L m−2 h−1 on
sampled flux values, a constraint that was never active under the
present experimental conditions. A fixed random seed was
applied to ensure reproducibility of the reported results.
For each sampled flux pair, the full LCOW calculation was
executed. The resulting distribution of 10,000 LCOW values
was used to report, alongside each deterministic point estimate,
the mean, standard deviation, and 95% confidence interval (CI)
defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the distribution.
In addition, a one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis was conducted
to quantify the individual response of LCOW to variation in four
techno-economic parameters: membrane unit cost (USD 10−
80 m−2), water flux (1−15 L m−2 h−1), discount rate (2−16%),
and electricity price (USD 0.05−0.35 kWh−1). Each parameter
was varied independently across its defined range while all others
were fixed at their nominal values, yielding a set of LCOW
response curves that identify the parameters to which each draw
solution configuration is most sensitive and quantify the
magnitude of LCOW variation attributable to each. The oneat-a-time approach varies each parameter independently and
therefore does not capture interaction effects between correlated
parameters, such as the covariation of discount rate and
electricity price. This limitation should be considered when
interpreting the magnitude of the sensitivity gradients reported
in Section 5.4.2.

electrostatic exclusion, facilitating relatively high Js values. This
apparent inconsistency with sodium acetate’s comparatively

acetate under WW feed inflates Jsw even though its reverse solute
flux remains numerically lower than that of NaCl.

the 4-h observation period.

overall reduction in water flux when operating with WW. A
lower Jw inherently led to less severe concentration polarization
effects, both dilutive (ECP-DS) and concentrative (ECP-FS,

closer to their respective bulk values. Consequently, the
attenuation of the osmotic driving force due to CP was reduced
in magnitude, resulting in a higher fraction of the bulk osmotic

increase in the effective pressure fraction, it did not translate into
higher Jw because WW simultaneously introduced additional
hydraulic resistance through incipient fouling layer formation
and enhanced ECP-FS, which collectively outweighed any
apparent driving force gain.

from this ECP-DS mitigation mechanism. Consequently, the
increased ECP-FS from WW directly reduced its effective
osmotic pressure from 43.75% to 32.49%, the largest absolute
reduction among all draw solutes. This is consistent with the
greatest Jw decline under WW conditions.
The dominance of ICP in the CP budget observed here for all
three draw solutions is consistent with the broader osmotically
assisted reverse osmosis (OARO) and FO transport literature, in
which ICP is identified as the primary flux-limiting mechanism
in asymmetric membranes with S > 300 μm operating under
high osmotic pressure driving forces.56 The differential ICP
magnitude across draw solutions, with the divalent draw solution
exhibiting the largest ICP fraction, reflects the well-documented
inverse relationship between ion diffusivity and ICP severity in
confined pore geometries, a relationship that has recently been
quantified under multi-electrolyte conditions in both FO and
OARO contexts.47,57 The observation that ECP-FS increased
substantially on switching from DI to WW feed is also consistent
with literature showing real wastewater feeds intensify feed-side
concentration polarization, whether through fouling-enhanced
CP or reduced boundary-layer mass transfer.58

7. CONCLUSION
This study presented a combined experimental, modeling, and
techno-economic investigation of FO for wastewater treatment,
providing a quantitative characterization of water, solute, and
organic matter transport across three draw solutions and two
feed conditions.
The experimental campaign confirmed that the transition
from deionized water to real wastewater feed induced flux
declines ranging from 8.14% to 46.19% over the 4-h
experimental period, attributable to the combined effect of
organic fouling and increased feed-side osmotic resistance, with
NaCl consistently exhibiting the highest water flux. The
MATLAB finite-difference model, incorporating a bisection
solver for water flux estimation and empirical correlations for
solution properties, accurately reproduced the experimental Jw
and Js profiles and the full-transmembrane concentration
profiles, validating the numerical framework as a reliable
predictive tool for FO system simulation under complex feed
conditions.
The phenomenological COD sub-model introduced in this
work allowed the decoupling of organic matter dynamics into
three physically distinct contributions: feed-side COD concentration enhancement, transmembrane leakage governed by
PCOD, and irreversible fouling accumulation governed by Kfoul.
The calibrated parameters revealed that transmembrane leakage
was the dominant pathway for COD loss from the feed liquid
phase under the tested conditions, accounting for 82−89% of
total COD mass transferred out of the feed reservoir, while
irreversible fouling deposition contributed 11−18%. It is
important to note that transmembrane leakage does not
represent effective treatment: COD that permeates the
membrane transfers to the draw solution rather than being
removed from the system. This contamination of the draw
solution has two process implications. Firstly, it reduces the
apparent purity of the regenerated draw solution and may
impose an additional organic load on the RO regeneration unit.
Secondly, true irreversible COD removal from the liquid phase
is governed exclusively by the fouling deposition term, which
was substantially lower than the leakage term across all draw
solutions. The dimensionless PCOD/Kfoul ratio introduced here
provides a practical single-parameter screening metric for draw
solution selectivity. MgSO4 exhibited the lowest Kfoul of 0.04 L
m−2 h−1 and the most balanced PCOD/Kfoul ratio, indicating that
both leakage-driven draw-solution contamination and irreversible fouling accumulation were minimized relative to the other
draw solutes.
The techno-economic analysis identified NaCl as the most
economically viable draw solution for FO wastewater treatment,
achieving a levelized cost of water of USD 1.04 m−3, 40% lower
than the worst-performing configuration. Sensitivity analysis
confirmed that transmembrane water flux is the primary
technical lever governing LCOW, while discount rate and
electricity price represent the dominant external risk factors. The
modeling framework developed here constitutes a transferable,
experimentally grounded tool for draw solution screening and

6. OVERALL PERFORMANCE TRADE-OFF AND DRAW
SOLUTION SELECTION
The comparative analysis revealed a multidimensional trade-off
among the three draw solutions that extended beyond hydraulics
to encompass membrane selectivity, fouling tendencies, and
economic viability. Thus, the optimal selection is inherently
application-specific. The decision framework maps out actions
across three distincts scenarios: Cost-constrained high-throughput water production, Effluent quality-constrained or high-value
resource recovery applications, and Applications with strict
draw-solute environmental or regulatory constraints.
NaCl emerged as the most economically favorable option,
generating the highest values of permeate flux, water recovery,
volumetric concentrate ratio, and COD concentration factor,
thereby reducing the total membrane area required and lowering
the LCOW. These features made NaCl the most suitable draw
solution for maximizing process output and minimizing costs.
However, these performance advantages were obtained at the
expense of high reverse salt flux and greater initial foulant
accumulation within the 4-hour experimental window under
wastewater conditions. COD transfer to the draw solution, the
primary operational challenge, is most appropriately managed
through downstream draw-solution polishing or by accepting
higher organic load on the RO regeneration unit where effluent
quality requirements are moderate.
MgSO4, by contrast, provided the best COD removal
efficiency, the lowest initial fouling accumulation within the 4hour experimental period, and the most stable flux decay kinetics
under the tested conditions. Although MgSO4 required more
membrane area, leading to a higher LCOW, this draw solution
was identified as the most suitable candidate for applications in
which selectivity, fouling resistance, and operational stability are
prioritized over cost minimization, such as high-value resource
recovery or sensitive effluent treatment scenarios.
Sodium acetate was positioned hydraulically midway between
MgSO4 and NaCl but was at a disadvantage structurally driven
by its expensive draw-solute replenishment, which rendered it
uncompetitive. Nonetheless, sodium acetate presents three
deployment scenarios where it may be preferred over NaCl
despite its higher LCOW:
(i) Jurisdictions where NaCl discharge restrictions apply due
to receiving-water salinity limits, since acetate is fully
biodegradable and environmentally benign;
(ii) FO−biological treatment hybrids where low-level reverse
acetate flux into the feed stream serves as a supplementary
carbon source for downstream biological polishing,
turning a process inefficiency into a functional advantage;
(iii) Applications requiring food-grade or pharmaceuticalgrade product water compatibility, where inorganic salt
contamination is unacceptable.
In all other scenarios, the structural LCOW penalty, driven by
acetate’s unit market price, renders it uncompetitive without a

FO system design. The Monte Carlo analysis demonstrated that

Several limitations should be acknowledged. WW characterization was limited to COD and osmotic pressure, which
precludes assessment of how specific feed matrix components
contribute to the observed fouling patterns. Foulant accumulation was quantified through COD mass balance alone,
capturing only the organic fraction of total foulant accumulation.
The 4-h experimental window characterizes the initial-tointermediate fouling regime, during which first-order fouling
kinetics is a valid modeling assumption, but does not capture
biofouling dynamics, cake compaction, or cleaning-cycle
recovery, all of which emerge under extended continuous
operation. Therefore, the comparative fouling rankings reported
should be interpreted as initial-regime indicators that require
validation through longer-duration, multi-cycle experiments
before they can be confidently generalized to practical
continuous operation. It is also acknowledged that the LCOW
values reported for sodium acetate represent an upper bound
under RO regeneration and should be reinterpreted if a lowercost thermal or precipitation route is available for the specific
solute chemistry. Site-specific economic evaluation should
therefore reassess the regeneration route for sodium acetate
before applying these results to a real installation. Performance
was further characterized at a single initial osmotic pressure of 30
bar; the concentration-dependent performance envelope of each
draw solution across the practical operating range was therefore
not mapped, and the comparative rankings reported here should
not be extrapolated to other draw solution concentrations
without further testing.
Future work should extend the experimental campaign to
longer operating duration, validate the PCOD/Kfoul ratio across
alternative membrane types and feed matrices, and assess the
framework at pilot scale under industrially representative
hydraulic conditions.
