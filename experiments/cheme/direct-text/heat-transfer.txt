ABSTRACT: Macaw palm (Acrocomia aculeata) almond oil was
evaluated as a potential renewable heat-transfer fluid (HTF)
candidate for medium-temperature solar thermal applications
through thermophysical and chemical characterization. The oil
was extracted using Soxhlet extraction and characterized by gas
chromatography with flame ionization detection (GC-FID),
Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), ultraviolet−visible
spectroscopy (UV−Vis), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), and
differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). The extraction yield was
15.74 ± 2.85% (w/w), with an acid value of 3.44 ± 0.02 mg KOH·
g−1, density of 0.917 ± 0.006 g·cm−3 at 20 °C, and kinematic
viscosity of 29.75 ± 0.76 mm2·s−1 at 40 °C. Fatty acid composition revealed a predominance of lauric acid (39.36%), followed by
oleic (29.99%) and linoleic acids (14.47%), indicating a mixed saturated-unsaturated profile with implications for viscosity behavior
and oxidative susceptibility. FTIR and UV−Vis analyses confirmed the triglyceride structure and indicated early oxidation markers
associated with unsaturated lipid fractions. Thermal analysis indicated the onset of measurable degradation around 300 °C,
suggesting that continuous thermal-fluid operation should be maintained below this temperature. Compared with other vegetable
oils previously investigated for thermal-fluid applications, macaw palm almond oil presents favorable density and viscosity
characteristics, although oxidative stability and acid value remain engineering concerns requiring pretreatment or formulation
improvements. These findings represent a first-stage screening assessment of macaw palm almond oil as a biobased HTF candidate
and support further investigation involving oxidative aging, thermal cycling, corrosion compatibility, and pilot-scale validation.

Vegetable oils have attracted increasing attention as

In countries such as Brazil, favorable agro-climatic

However, their application in thermal systems remains limited
by challenges associated with oxidation, thermal degradation,
viscosity increase under prolonged heating, and material
compatibility. Several vegetable oils, including soybean, palm,
and jatropha oils, have previously been investigated as
potential thermal fluids, highlighting both the promise and
limitations of this class of renewable materials. Recent research
has increasingly explored environmentally sustainable alternatives to conventional petroleum-derived HTFs, driven by
lifecycle sustainability concerns and stricter environmental
performance requirements. However, oxidative stability and
viscosity behavior at elevated temperatures remain major
technical barriers to the practical implementation of biobased
fluids.5
Within this context, Acrocomia aculeata (macaw palm)
represents an interesting but still underexplored feedstock.
This native Brazilian palm is widely distributed across
subtropical and semiarid regions, exhibits strong ecological
adaptability, and offers attractive oil productivity, reaching
approximately 4,000 kg oil·ha−1 under favorable conditions.8,9
In addition, macaw oil presents a fatty acid composition that
may be relevant for thermal applications, particularly due to its
significant content of saturated and monounsaturated
components, which can influence both fluidity and thermal
stability.10
Despite this potential, the applicability of macaw palm oil as
a heat-transfer fluid remains insufficiently investigated,
particularly regarding its thermophysical and thermal behavior
in the context of solar thermal systems. To the best of current
knowledge, no study has specifically evaluated Acrocomia
aculeata almond oil from the perspective of its applicability as a
renewable heat-transfer fluid for solar thermal systems. Existing
studies involving this feedstock are predominantly focused on
biodiesel production, food applications, or general physicochemical characterization rather than engineering-oriented
thermal fluid assessment. This establishes a clear research
gap regarding its thermophysical suitability for renewable
thermal energy applications.
Therefore, this study presents a first-stage screening
assessment of macaw (Acrocomia aculeata) almond oil as a
potential renewable HTF candidate through physicochemical,
spectroscopic, and thermophysical characterization. Specifically, acid value, density, and kinematic viscosity were

2. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
2.1. Materials
The reagents used for macauba oil extraction included Hexane (100%,
Synth, Brazil) and Anhydrous Sodium Sulfate P.A. (99%, Neon,
Brazil), both of which were used as received without any further
treatment. All apparatus cleaning procedures were performed using
deionized water.

2.2. Oil Preparation and Extraction
Macaw palm (Acrocomia aculeata) fruits were collected at the
Agricultural Sciences Engineering Campus (CECA) of the Federal
University of Alagoas (UFAL), Brazil, at different maturation stages
(green, intermediate, and ripe). After collection, the fruits were
cleaned, peeled, and subjected to drying in a forced-air oven (Solab,
model SL-100, 15,000 W) at 50 °C for approximately 72 h to reduce
moisture content.
Following drying, the almond material was manually separated
using a stainless-steel knife, crushed using a grain grinder (Hamilton
Beach, model 80393BZ220, 150 W), and maintained under controlled
drying conditions at 50 °C until extraction.
Oil extraction was performed using the Soxhlet method, selected
due to its high extraction efficiency for lipid recovery from oleaginous
matrices. Three extraction cartridges were prepared using filter paper
and filled with previously weighed crushed almond samples.
The extraction system consisted of three 1 L round-bottom flasks
connected to Soxhlet extractors and condensers. In each extraction
unit, 125 mL of hexane was used as the solvent for approximately 17 g
of macauba palm almond, resulting in a solvent-to-sample ratio of
approximately 1:7.3 (m/v). The extraction process was conducted for
approximately 5 h at about 70 °C using heating mantles.
Temperature monitoring during extraction was performed using a
digital thermometer (Incoterm, model 9226.16.0.00) to maintain
appropriate solvent reflux conditions.
After extraction, the solvent was removed using a rotary evaporator
(Büchi, model R-300, 100 W) at 60 °C under controlled vacuum for
approximately 10 min.
Residual moisture was removed by adding anhydrous sodium
sulfate (Na2SO4, 99%).
The extracted oil was stored in 50 mL amber glass containers
protected from light with aluminum foil and kept at −20 °C until
characterization.

2.3.4. Density Measurement. A digital densimeter Anton Paar

2.3.5. Statistical and Uncertainty Considerations. All
repeatable measurements were performed in triplicate, and the results
are reported as mean ± standard deviation. This approach was used to
account for experimental variability and to provide an estimate of the
repeatability of the measurements.
For calculated properties, such as kinematic viscosity and acid
value, the variability reported reflects the dispersion observed among
the replicate determinations. Instrumental specifications and the
controlled experimental conditions were also considered when
assessing the reliability of the measurements.
As the present study was designed as a preliminary screening of
macaw palm almond oil as a potential heat-transfer fluid, a formal
propagation of uncertainty for each individual variable involved in the
calculations was not performed. Nevertheless, the use of replicate
measurements and the corresponding standard deviations provides an
appropriate indication of experimental variability for the comparative
and exploratory purposes of this study. A more detailed metrological
uncertainty analysis should be included in future validation studies
aimed at operational qualification of the fluid.

3.1. Physical Properties of Macaw Palm Almond Oil

The extraction of macaw oil in this study yielded 15.74 ±
2.85% (w/w)�a value that is consistent with the literature and
sits between the 6.1% reported by Amaral13 and the 16.5%

expected and can stem from both biological and process
determinants. Biologically, yield is modulated by genotype/
ecotype, fruit maturity stage at harvest, and postharvest
metabolism (e.g., endogenous lipase activity), all of which
are further conditioned by environmental drivers that regulate
plant productivity�particularly soil fertility and nutrient
status, temperature regimes, water availability, and photoperiod.13 From a processing standpoint, reported values are
sensitive to the moisture content and drying history of the
feedstock, particle-size distribution after milling, solvent-tosolid ratio, extraction time and temperature, and the efficiency
of solvent removal during concentration and storage. In this
context, our result (obtained from a total extract volume of
∼30 mL) represents a realistic midrange performance for
macaw almonds under the specified conditions, and it
underscores the importance of clearly stating the mass basis
(wet vs dry) and operational parameters when comparing
extraction yields across studies. From an engineering
perspective, extraction yield is not a direct determinant of

The acid value measured for the oil was 3.44 ± 0.02 mg
KOH·g−1, indicating the presence of free fatty acids resulting
from hydrolytic or oxidative degradation processes. The acid
value which is slightly lower than values reported by
Gonçalves15 (3.49 mg KOH·g−1) and substantially lower
than those of Melo16 (12.24 mg of KOH/g), and (12.8 mg
KOH·g−1) for Amaral.13 Because AV reflects the concentration
of free fatty acids (FFAs) generated by hydrolysis and oxidative
cleavage of triglycerides, lower values typically indicate better
conservation status of the almonds and more controlled
handling/extraction (e.g., minimized moisture/enzymatic
activity and limited thermal/oxidative stress). In practical
terms, a lower AV mitigates risks of corrosion in metal surfaces,
soap formation during contact with alkaline media, and catalyst
deactivation in downstream conditioning or upgrading steps�
each of which can compromise the reliability of heat-transfer
circuits and auxiliary operations.
Amaral13 cites a “standard” acidity on the order of ∼5 mg
KOH·g−1 for acceptable-quality macaw oil, placing our value
below that threshold and reinforcing its suitability for
engineering evaluation. Conversely, elevated AV�as emphasized by Meher et al.17�often signals inadequate fruit
selection, improper postharvest handling, or suboptimal
extraction protocols, conditions that accelerate triglyceride
degradation and can preclude certain applications due to
saponification and deposit formation. For full comparability
and process design, we report density (at 20 °C) and kinematic
viscosity (at 40 °C) alongside AV, since density informs mass/
heat balances and pump sizing, while viscosity governs pressure
drop, heat-transfer coefficients, and start-up behavior; together
with a low AV, these parameters provide a coherent basis for
assessing oil stability and fit-for-purpose performance in
thermal systems.
Building on Galvão,18 the lowest feasible acid values (AV)
are preferred for vegetable oils because AV is a proxy for
hydrolytic/oxidative alteration and, when elevated, can
jeopardize downstream use (e.g., corrosion, soap formation,

catalyst fouling). Reported AVs understandably span a range
rather than a fixed point because they track (i) the degree of
unsaturation (more unsaturation → greater oxidative susceptibility), (ii) seasonality and ecotype of the oilseed (climate and
phenology shift lipid biosynthesis), and (iii) processing/
storage history (drying, moisture control, time-to-extraction,
oxygen/heat exposure). Consequently, literature values should
be interpreted with these sources of variance in mind rather
than as absolute pass/fail cutoffs.
It is important to note that vegetable oils inherently present
higher acid values than engineered synthetic HTFs, and acid
value alone should not be interpreted as a definitive exclusion
criterion. However, for practical thermal-fluid deployment,
pretreatment steps such as deacidification would likely be
necessary.
The measured density was 0.917 ± 0.006 g·cm−3 at 20 °C,
which lies within the range commonly reported for vegetable
oils proposed for thermal applications.
Density directly affects thermal mass transport, pumping
requirements, and volumetric heat-transfer considerations. The
observed value suggests compatibility with general expectations
for organic liquid thermal media.
While this measurement at 20 °C provides a reference
physical characterization, comprehensive thermophysical mapping across the operating temperature range is envisioned for
future thermal scaling and heat-exchanger design in mediumtemperature solar systems.
The kinematic viscosity measured here (29.75 ± 0.76 mm2·

Macaw palm almond oil is lauric-rich, with C12:0 ≈ 39%,

lauric/myristic fractions tend to lower viscosity and melting
range, favoring pumpability at moderate temperatures, while
oleic/linoleic contribute to oxidation susceptibility and thus
inform the need for conditioning (e.g., antioxidants) in thermal
applications.
In this data set, C18 species dominate macaw palm almond
oil, with linoleic (C18:2 ≈ 53.1%) and oleic (C18:1 ≈ 23.0%)
as the principal components, indicating a profile enriched in diand monounsaturated. For lubricant uses, macaw palm almond
oil can therefore be positioned as a C18-rich base stock that is
promising when paired with antioxidant packages and, if
needed, formulation strategies (e.g., blending with high-oleic
oils or partial interesterification) to tune stability without
sacrificing viscosity and film-forming performance.15
Factors such as alkyl-chain length, degree of unsaturation,
and intermolecular interactions govern variations in viscosity.
Unsaturated fatty acids predominantly occur in the cis
configuration, introducing kinks that hinder close molecular
packing; this reduces van der Waals cohesion and, consequently, lowers viscosity relative to analogous saturated
chains of similar length.19,20
The significant lauric fraction observed here distinguishes
macaw palm almond oil from many previously studied
vegetable oils.
Potential engineering implications include advantages such
as improved molecular packing stability; reduced oxidation
sensitivity relative to highly polyunsaturated oils; lower
viscosity compared with long-chain saturated oils. And
limitations include: remaining unsaturated fractions still permit
oxidative degradation; thermal polymerization risk remains
relevant.

molecules.23

3.5. Thermal Stability (TGA)

Figure 3 displays the thermogravimetric (TGA) profile, which
reveals a multistage mass-loss behavior rather than “exothermic
events” (those are captured by DSC). The oil shows an onset
of measurable volatilization/decomposition with ∼10% mass
loss at ∼300 °C, followed by a major degradation stage up to
∼480 °C that accounts for ≈95% of the initial mass, consistent
with thermal cleavage and volatilization of triglyceride
fragments. Above ∼480 °C, the residual ∼5% decreases only
slowly, which is attributable to the thermal decomposition of
polymerized/refractory residues formed during heating.
Collectively, the TGA trace indicates limited thermal stability
above ∼300 °C and a dominant decomposition window
between ∼350−480 °C under the stated experimental
conditions.
Accordingly, the primary deoxygenation and cracking of
macaw palm almond oil are inferred to occur predominantly
within ∼350−450 °C, consistent with the TGA major-loss
window observed here and with literature reports that place
triglyceride thermal fragmentation events broadly between
∼235 and 490 °C. 16,26,27 Mechanistically, this range
encompasses acylglycerol bond scission, β-scission of alkoxy/
alkyl radicals, and decarboxylation/decarbonylation pathways
that yield lighter hydrocarbons and oxygenates, followed by
secondary volatilization and slow decomposition of polymerized residues at higher temperatures.

stage spanning ∼340−480 °C, with the maximum degradation
rate near ∼415 °C, and no evident inflection in the TGA trace

Crude macaw palm almond oil presented a high acidity value

of the absorber tubes because of the formation of free fatty

apply the deacidification process of this oil. Given this,
obtaining a better-quality product depends on greater control
over the collection, handling, and storage processes of macaw
fruit, as well as the oil extraction stage.
Viscosity is a key determinant of CSP viability because it
governs mass flow, pressure drop, pump power, and start-up
behavior; since viscosity falls sharply with temperature, lowerviscosity HTFs generally enable better transport conditions
within a given piping layout. In our measurements, macaw oil
exhibits a kinematic viscosity of 29.75 mm2·s−1 at 40 °C, which
is lower than values cited for silicone oils (≈35−40 mm2·s−1 at
40 °C) and jatropha oil (≈39.72 mm2·s−1 at 40 °C). For
context, literature often reports dynamic viscosities at elevated
temperatures for incumbent HTFs (e.g., Therminol VP-1 at
∼300 °C; solar salt at ∼600 °C), so direct numerical ratios can
be misleading without normalization to the same temperature
and property type (kinematic vs dynamic). With that caveat,
the comparison still positions macaw oil as relatively pumpable
at moderate temperatures, suggesting reduced pressure losses
versus higher-viscosity alternatives at equal conditions, while
emphasizing that meaningful cross-fluid benchmarking must
account for temperature-dependent viscosity curves and the
kinematic/dynamic distinction. It should be noted that
viscosity was experimentally determined at a single reference
temperature of 40 °C in the present study. Therefore, a
complete viscosity−temperature relationship cannot be established from the current data set. Nevertheless, the measured
value provides a useful preliminary indication of the oil
transport behavior. Since viscosity strongly influences pressure
losses and pumping energy requirements, future studies should
evaluate this property over the intended operating temperature
range to support hydraulic design, circulation modeling, and
more accurate assessment of pumping requirements in heattransfer systems.
Fluids with high density and thermal capacity values are the
most suitable for working HTF and for thermal storage, as they
increase the system’s efficiency. Macaw palm almond oil
density was 0.917 g/cm3 at 20 °C, consistent with thermal
fluids presented in Table 2. The calorific capacity of macaw
palm almond oil was not determined in this work. However,
Amaral13 verified that macaw almond oil’s calorific value was
4739 kcal/kg, revealing the high calorific value of the species in
comparison to other oilseeds such as soybean and sunflower oil
(2200 and 1700 kcal/kg, respectively).
Thermal conductivity and maximum operating temperature
are pivotal HTF parameters because they directly control heat
pickup within absorber tubes and subsequent heat exchange
(typically to high-pressure water/steam). Higher thermal
conductivity improves the internal heat-transfer coefficient
and reduces temperature gradients, while a higher allowable
bulk temperature enables greater thermodynamic efficiency�
provided the fluid remains chemically and thermally stable (no
cracking, oxidation, or excessive vapor pressure) and materialscompatible. Together, these properties largely determine
HTF’s suitability for service in CSP loops and should be

considered alongside viscosity, specific heat, and corrosion risk
when selecting fluids for plant operation.
Hoffman et al.12 state that increasing temperature can
reduce thermal conductivity. This work could not determine
the thermal conductivity of macaw oil because of limitations in
the reproducibility of the analyses. Pure macaw palm almond
vegetable oil had an irregular operating temperature at
temperatures <330 °C with a loss of 10% of its initial mass,
a lower condition than other fluids commonly applied in CSP
systems. However, its temperature exceeds the temperatures
obtained, for example, in plants with a linear Fresnel reflector
where they reach a temperature of 250 °C.
A more in-depth investigation is required to achieve this
condition, especially concerning low lower-limit temperatures
and high upper-temperature limits at low pressures, aiming at
safety and greater efficiency in the conversion of heat into
electricity in the system. Halogenated salts can operate at high
temperatures (<1000 °C), but they have a high melting point,
making their application unfeasible.12 Such factors can also be
seen in other HTFs.
The melting temperature is another important factor for
verifying the technical-economic viability of the system.
Despite the high melting temperature of macaw oil (Table
2), it was lower than the temperature of molten salts (between
65 and 400 °C). Solar salt has a melting and operating
temperature of 223 and 550 °C, respectively, which raises
freezing/solidification concerns. This fact increases the cost of
the plant due to the need for protection systems against fluid
solidification in solar field absorber tubes and thermal storage
tanks.
Regarding the oxidative stability of macaw oil, two stages of
chemical degradation of deoxygenation and cracking were
identified, relating events of thermal fragmentation reactions of
triglycerides between 235 and 490 °C. Therefore, this
sensitivity can alter chemical and physical properties compared
to other thermal fluids. The use of corrosion-inhibiting
additives and antiwear agents can benefit in controlling oil
mass degradation caused by thermal stress. Therefore, a
cautious investigation of its oxidation stability and thermal
aging is recommended for further conclusions for solar
applications.
In this sense, the applied experiments expand and strengthen
the benefits of macaw palm almond oil in the chain of oils
commonly used in solar power plants, partially replacing
synthetic and mineral oils, and increasing environmental gains
as they are renewable and biodegradable resources. Engineering implications: deploying macaw oil as HTF in CSP requires
(i) deacidification to curb corrosion risk, (ii) antioxidant
packages to delay oxidation under cyclic heating, and (iii)
materials compatibility checks (seals, elastomers, steel alloys).
Phenolic antioxidants and passivators can extend oil lifetime at
250−320 °C; blending with higher-stability fluids is another
pathway to tailor viscosity and oxidative resilience without
process redesign.
Therefore, the present findings support classification of
macaw palm almond oil as a promising renewable screening
candidate, not a validated HTF replacement.

4. CONCLUSIONS
Although several vegetable oils (soybean, jatropha, palm,
rapeseed) have been investigated as biobased HTF candidates,
no previous study has specifically reported the thermophysical
screening evaluation of macaw palm almond oil for this
application.
Macaw palm almond oil (Acrocomia aculeata) was evaluated
through physicochemical and thermophysical characterization
as a potential renewable heat-transfer fluid (HTF) candidate
for medium-temperature solar thermal applications, with a
practical operating limit below 300 °C to prevent thermal
breakdown, volatilization, and deposit formation under realworld system conditions.
The oil exhibited an extraction yield of 15.74%, acid value of
3.44 mg KOH·g−1, density of 0.917 g·cm−3 at 20 °C, and
kinematic viscosity of 29.75 mm2·s−1 at 40 °C. Its fatty acid
profile, dominated by lauric, oleic, and linoleic acids, together
with FTIR, UV−Vis, and thermal analyses, indicates
physicochemical characteristics compatible with preliminary
HTF screening. However, this study represents a first-stage
screening assessment rather than operational HTF validation.
Critical issues such as long-term oxidative stability, thermal
cycling performance, corrosion compatibility, heat transfer
efficiency, circulation behavior, and techno-economic feasibility remain unresolved. In addition, the observed acid value
and susceptibility to oxidation suggest that fluid conditioning
or pretreatment may be required.
Despite these limitations, macaw palm almond oil offers
sustainability-related advantages, including renewable sourcing
and biodegradability, expanding the portfolio of biobased
candidate materials for solar thermal applications. Future
studies should focus on accelerated aging, thermal performance
validation, corrosion testing, process simulation, lifecycle
assessment, and pilot-scale implementation.
