Controlled Humidity in the Presence of a Static Magnetic Field

ABSTRACT: This study investigates the effects of relative humidity and a static
magnetic field on the deposition patterns of evaporating sodium chlorate droplets on
silicon substrates. Experiments were conducted at three controlled humidity levels (11,
33, and 59%), under both zero-field conditions and in the presence of a static
magnetic field of (290 ± 5) mT. Deposition patterns were classified as single crystals,
coffee-ring structures, or irregular formations. The results show that deposition
morphology strongly depends on relative humidity: irregular patterns are most
frequent at low humidity, coffee-ring structures dominate at intermediate humidity,
while higher humidity promotes single-crystal formation. Comparison of experiments
performed with and without an applied static magnetic field indicates that, within the
investigated field strength, evaporation dynamics governed by relative humidity
dominate over any magnetic-field-induced effects on crystallization and deposition
morphology. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) revealed only minor variations in
lattice constants, with no clear correlation to the applied magnetic field. These results indicate that humidity-controlled evaporation
dynamics are the dominant factor governing deposition morphology, whereas no measurable effects of the applied magnetic field
were observed under the investigated conditions.

INTRODUCTION
The evaporation of sessile droplets containing dissolved salts is
a phenomenon of broad scientific and technological interest,
with applications ranging from inkjet printing and coating
technologies to biomedical diagnostics.1−4 Droplet evaporation
is governed by the coupled processes of heat and mass transfer,
internal fluid flow, and solute transport, which together
determine the evolution of concentration fields, crystallization,
and the final deposit morphology.5 As a droplet evaporates, the
solute redistributes within the droplet and forms a solid
deposit on the substrate. This redistribution is controlled by
the combined effects of fluid flow inside the droplet, diffusion,
and solute transport, which determine the final deposition
morphology. Previous studies have shown that evaporationinduced fluid motion can affect the distribution of dissolved
species and the characteristics of dried deposits in different
droplet systems.6,7
The formation of deposition patterns in evaporating droplets
has also been extensively studied within theoretical frameworks
of evaporative lithography, where transport processes and
boundary conditions determine the final deposit morphology.8
Salt crystallization during droplet drying is influenced by
multiple factors, including solute concentration, substrate
wetting, temperature, relative humidity, and the internal flow
dynamics within the droplet.9−13 Recent studies have further

Among the external perturbations capable of modifying

findings highlight the sensitivity of evaporation-driven

processes within the droplet and motivate the exploration of
other external fields capable of influencing deposition behavior.
External fields, particularly magnetic and electric fields, have
recently attracted attention because of their ability to influence
droplet evaporation and crystallization.18,19 Magnetic fields
have been reported to influence crystal nucleation, growth, and
deposit morphology in magnetic fluid and colloidal systems,18,19 whereas their effects in diamagnetic aqueous salt
solutions remain less understood.
A static magnetic field may represent a weak external
perturbation capable of modifying transport processes in
aqueous systems, including heat and mass transfer and fluid
motion, which may subsequently influence crystallization
behavior and deposition morphology.20,21 Therefore, investigating effects of magnetic field during droplet drying provides
an opportunity to evaluate whether such weak perturbations
can influence crystallization when coupled with evaporationdriven transport processes.
The crystallization process is strongly affected by ambient
conditions, particularly relative humidity, which controls the
evaporation rate, the development of supersaturation, and the
time available for crystal nucleation and growth, thereby
influencing the resulting deposition patterns and crystal
morphology.10,12,14 Sodium chlorate was selected as a model
system because it crystallizes in a simple cubic structure,
enabling investigation of how external factors influence
crystallization pathways and deposition morphology.
In this study, sodium chlorate droplets were evaporated
under three controlled relative humidity levels (RH ≈ 11, 33,
and 59%), both in the absence and presence of a static
magnetic field of B = (290 ± 5) mT. The evaporation
temperatures (T) were selected within a narrow range around
the saturation temperature (Ts) to generate both positive and
negative values of ΔT = Ts − T, enabling assessment of the
influence of both the magnitude and the sign of ΔT on
crystallization. The resulting deposits were classified using
optical microscopy, while representative single crystals were
further characterized by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD).
The objective of this work was to evaluate the influence of
relative humidity, the temperature difference ΔT, and an
applied static magnetic field on the crystallization behavior and
deposition morphology of sodium chlorate droplets, with
particular emphasis on their relative contributions to pattern
formation.

Experimental Setup
Droplets of 5 μL were deposited onto 5 mm × 5 mm silicon substrate,
which were pre-equilibrated at the evaporation temperature (T) for
30 min. Droplet drying experiments were conducted in a controlledhumidity, double-walled glass container (internal volume 1 L)
connected to a thermostated water bath with a pump. Thermostatic
water circulated through the space between the glass walls to maintain
a constant temperature within ±0.1 °C. All experiments were
conducted under controlled relative humidity conditions, utilizing
three fixed humidity points (11, 33, and 59%) of binary saturated
aqueous solutions to regulate the humidity in the droplet drying
environment.23 Saturated solutions of lithium chloride, magnesium
chloride, sodium bromide were employed to maintain the relative
humidity in the capped glass container at 11, 33, and 59%,
respectively.

After substrate equilibration, 5 μL droplets were dispensed. The
samples were then returned to the container and maintained in a
controlled environment for 2 h to ensure complete evaporation.
Experiments were performed at evaporation temperatures ranging
from 28−34 °C, for both zero-field and magnetic-field conditions at
each of three humidity levels.

Sodium chlorate (p.a. purity) was dissolved in deionized water to
prepare a saturated solution at the saturation temperature, Ts = (31.0
± 0.1) °C. The solution was kept at this temperature for 3 days to

DISCUSSION
Previous studies have highlighted various factors controlling
salt crystallization patterns.10,25 Basu and Mukherjee emphasized the role of salt concentration,25 whereas Shahidzadeh et
al. demonstrated that wetting properties can outweigh
evaporation rate and substrate thermal conductivity.10 In the
present study, all experiments were conducted using identical
silicon substrates and solutions prepared at the same initial
concentration, for both experiments performed with and
without an applied magnetic field. Consequently, substrateand concentration-related effects can be considered negligible
under the present experimental conditions, indicating that
these factors are unlikely to be responsible for the observed
differences in the final deposition patterns.
The experimental results presented in Tables 2−4 and
Figure 3 show that the distribution of deposition patterns
depends primarily on relative humidity. At low relative
humidity (RH ≈ 11%), irregular patterns are the most
frequent deposits, while coffee-ring structures become
dominant at intermediate relative humidity (RH ≈ 33%). At
higher relative humidity (RH ≈ 59%), the fraction of singlecrystal deposits increases, indicating that slower evaporation
favors crystal growth within the droplet. Increasing evaporation
temperature tends to increase the fraction of irregular deposits
at RH ≈ 33%, whereas at RH ≈ 59% this effect is reduced due
to slower evaporation and longer drying times.11
The transition between coffee-ring and single-crystal
deposition patterns can be explained by the interplay between
humidity-dependent capillary flow and Marangoni convection.
At lower relative humidity, higher evaporation rates generate
strong outward capillary flow toward the pinned contact line,
leading to solute accumulation at the droplet perimeter and
promoting coffee-ring formation.26,27 In this regime, Marangoni convection is comparatively weak and cannot effectively
redistribute the solute within the droplet.11−13,25,28,29
At higher relative humidity, reduced evaporation weakens
the outward capillary flow, decreasing the efficiency of solute
transport toward the contact line. Under these conditions,
surface-tension gradients caused by temperature and concentration variations may contribute more effectively to
Marangoni circulation within the droplet, promoting internal
mixing and redistribution of dissolved species.11−13,25,28,29 This
can reduce preferential solute accumulation at the droplet edge
and increases the probability of nucleation and crystal growth
in the droplet interior.11−13,15 Therefore, the final deposition
morphology is governed by the competition between
evaporation-induced capillary flow, which promotes peripheral
accumulation and coffee-ring formation, and Marangoni
circulation, which can counteract this accumulation by
enhancing internal solute redistribution.26,27 Crystallizationdriven flow effects in evaporating aqueous saline droplets have
also been reported, indicating that the coupling between
evaporation, solute transport, and crystal growth may further
influence the resulting deposition patterns.30

Powder X-ray diffraction analysis revealed only minor
variations in the lattice constant under the investigated
experimental conditions (Table 6). The observed values
remain within a narrow range and do not exhibit any
systematic dependence on either the applied magnetic field
or the evaporation conditions, including the temperature
difference ΔT between the saturation and evaporation
temperatures. These results indicate that neither the applied
magnetic field nor the investigated evaporation conditions
cause systematic changes in the crystal lattice of sodium
chlorate.
Understanding how droplets evaporate and how crystals
form during drying is important for processes in which the final
distribution and morphology of deposited materials are
relevant, including printing, coating, and surface modification
technologies.1−4,8,9,29 Although sodium chlorate was used as a
model system, the mechanisms governing evaporation-driven
transport and crystallization discussed here are relevant to a
broader class of solution-based deposition processes. In
addition, the absence of a measurable effect of the magnetic
field under the investigated conditions suggests that, within the
range of parameters studied, evaporation conditions and mass
transport processes have a stronger influence on the final
deposition morphology. These findings contribute to a better
understanding of the relative importance of environmental
factors and external fields in influencing crystallization during
droplet drying.

influence the probability of forming single-crystal-type deposits

the crystals formed during droplet drying remain relatively

Although relative humidity exerts the strongest influence on
the deposition morphology, the experimental results summarized in Tables 2−4 also allow assessment of the effect of the
temperature difference, ΔT = Ts − T, between the saturation
temperature of the initial solution and the evaporation
temperature. Within the investigated range (ΔT = ±3 °C),
the relative fractions of single-crystal, coffee-ring, and irregular
deposits show no consistent trend that could be attributed to
ΔT alone. This behavior can be interpreted by considering the
temperature dependence of sodium chlorate solubility.
Since sodium chlorate exhibits a positive temperature
dependence of solubility,22 the temperature difference (ΔT)
between the substrate and the saturation temperature affects
the initial supersaturation state of the deposited droplet.
According to the classical theory of crystallization from
solution, supersaturation is the primary thermodynamic driving
force for nucleation and crystal growth; therefore, temperatureinduced changes in solubility may influence the onset of
crystallization.31
When a saturated solution prepared at 31 °C is deposited
onto a substrate maintained at 28 °C, the decrease in
temperature reduces the equilibrium solubility, producing an
initially supersaturated state that may promote crystal
nucleation before significant solvent evaporation occurs. In
contrast, deposition onto a substrate at 34 °C increases the
equilibrium solubility of sodium chlorate, resulting in an
initially less saturated solution. Under these conditions,
crystallization begins only after evaporation increases the
solute concentration above the saturation limit, while the
evolution of supersaturation is further governed by the coupled
heat and mass transfer processes occurring during droplet
evaporation.5

experimental results indicate that this effect becomes less
important as evaporation proceeds. Continuous solvent loss

CONCLUSIONS
The evaporation of sessile sodium chlorate droplets was
investigated under controlled relative humidity, evaporation
temperature, and static magnetic field conditions. The results
indicate that relative humidity is the dominant factor
controlling deposition morphology and crystallization behavior. Higher relative humidity reduces the evaporation rate,
weakens capillary-driven outward transport, and promotes
single-crystal formation, whereas lower humidity favors coffeering and irregular deposition patterns.
For the investigated temperature differences (ΔT = ±3 °C),
the influence of ΔT on the final deposition morphology was
not pronounced.
The application of a static magnetic field of (290 ± 5) mT
does not produce a measurable effect on deposition
morphology or the relative fraction of single-crystal formations.
Powder X-ray diffraction reveals minor variations in lattice
parameters; however, no clear influence of the applied
magnetic field is observed, suggesting that the observed
variations are related to crystal growth conditions rather than
to a systematic magnetic-field effect.
Comparison of experiments performed with and without an
applied static magnetic field of (290 ± 5) mT shows that the
magnetic field does not produce a detectable change in
deposition morphology.
