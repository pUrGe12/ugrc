ABSTRACT: Beyond conventional removal-efficiency metrics, this
investigation reveals a mechanistic divergence between two
prominent photocatalysts in ozone activation. Both (Pd/Cu)
bimetallic g-C3N4 and rutile TiO2 enable efficient antibiotic
degradation and antibiotic resistance genes inactivation; however,
they rely on fundamentally different reaction pathways. (Pd/
Cu)1%C3N4 predominantly generates reactive oxygen species
(ROS), including singlet oxygen (1O2) and superoxide radical
anion (O2●−), whereas (Pd/Cu)1%TiO2 targets specific sequences
like intl1 bypassing conventional ROS pathways. In addition, our
research highlights a critical design trade-off and operational
paradox in advanced oxidation processes: while a low bimetallic
loading (0.1 wt %) optimizes the initial chemical degradation kinetics of both antibiotics and antibiotic resistance genes during active
treatment, a higher loading (1.0 wt %) is strictly indispensable for suppressing long-term bacterial regrowth and preventing the
biological resuscitation of resistance markers during post-treatment storage. Moreover, this study challenges conventional
methodological paradigms by demonstrating that diagnostic scavengers are not inert probes but reactive species that accelerate
ozone (O3) depletion and alter mass-transfer behavior, potentially confounding mechanistic interpretations. In complex wastewater
matrices dominated by dissolved organic matter, such competitive interactions necessitate rigorous scavenger validation to ensure
reliable translation of laboratory findings to scalable purification systems.

INTRODUCTION
In recent years, there is a rising concern regarding the quality
of water and the efficacy of the currently used water treatment
procedures. It is estimated that only 63% of the world’s
wastewater is collected, even excluding agricultural runoff,
while 52% of wastewater is treated at all.1 In addition, there is a
growing problem of so-called contaminants of emerging
concern (CEC), pollutants which are hard to remove by
conventional water treatment facilities. This group includes
multiple substances such as hormones, personal care products,
pesticides, antibiotics, and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs).
ARGs are especially dangerous because by incorporating
antibiotic traits into bacteria at the gene level, they directly
influence the effectiveness of medical therapies.2 In the EU
summary report (2022−2023) it was shown that due to the
presence of ARGs in wastewater, hazardous pathogens like E.
coli bacteria started to develop antibiotic resistance, including
resistance to critically important antimicrobials (CIAs).3
Therefore, there is a great need to limit the concentration of
both antibiotics and ARGs in discharged effluents.

To combat this problem, multiple strategies have been
widely developed. Recent reports show a great interest in the
utilization of advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) for CEC
removal.4 AOPs include various techniques like Fenton and
Fenton-like processes, electro-peroxone, anodic oxidation,
photocatalysis, and ozonation.5 Depending on the selected
method and wastewater characteristics, different reactive
oxygen species (ROS) like superoxide radicals (O2●−),
hydroxyl radicals (●OH), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), or
singlet oxygen ( 1 O 2 ) play a decisive role in water
decontamination.5,6 However, although AOPs in general
prove to be very effective in antibiotic degradation, there are

doping is particularly interesting because it exhibits a lower

losing two electrons in contrast to silver or gold.22 However,
palladium, as a noble metal, is rare and expensive, and
therefore there is a movement toward bimetallic systems, like

understanding the impact of the Pd/Cu additive is essential
for optimizing the design of photocatalytic ozonation systems
tailored to specific water treatment applications.
In this work, we compared the effect of modification of TiO2
and C3N4 by bimetallic Pd/Cu for both antibiotic and ARG
removal. To obtain full information, we studied both ARGs
and integron markers as well as rDNA in secondary effluent.
The aim of this study was to elucidate the mechanistic
divergence and ozone mass-transfer behavior in photocatalytic
ozonation systems employing bimetallic Pd/Cu-modified TiO2
and C3N4. Specifically, the study sought to i) compare the
pathways of ozone activation and pollutant transformation; ii)
assess the role of reactive oxygen species versus non-ROS
mechanisms in the degradation of antibiotics and ARGs; iii)
evaluate the relationship between catalyst loading, degradation
kinetics, and post-treatment biological inactivation; and iv)
critically examine the influence of scavenger compounds and
dissolved organic matter on ozone consumption and
mechanistic interpretation under environmentally relevant
conditions. This work presents the first comprehensive
mechanistic comparison of bimetallic Pd/Cu-modified rutile
TiO2 and C3N4 for the photocatalytic ozonation of antibiotics
and resistance genes in complex wastewater matrices.
Challenging standard mechanistic assumptions, we demonstrate that radical scavengers are not passive probes but active
participants that fundamentally alter ozone decomposition
kinetics, necessitating a reevaluation of ROS roles via ozone
consumption monitoring. The investigation reveals a distinct
mechanistic divergence where C3N4 relies predominantly on
ROS-driven pathways for gene inactivation, whereas TiO2
exhibits a unique resistance to scavenging effects, suggesting
a direct charge-transfer mechanism for recalcitrant targets like
intl1. Furthermore, we identify a critical operational trade-off,
establishing that while low metal loadings (0.1%) optimize
initial degradation rates, higher loadings (1%) are indispensable for preventing post-treatment bacterial regrowth.

ii) AOPs may, in some cases, introduce ARGs into pathogenic
bacteria.4 Thus, selecting the appropriate AOP method for a
specific wastewater is a key issue in the water treatment

Photocatalytic ozonation, an innovative AOP process that
merges photocatalysis and ozonation, has garnered significant
attention as a potent technique for degrading organic
contaminants in water. The prominent aspect of this technique
is its high efficiency in pollutant degradation with a limited
amount of byproducts compared to traditional water treatment
methods. In the typical photocatalytic ozonation process, a
photon is absorbed by the photocatalyst, which leads to
electron−hole separation. Then, ozone absorbs these newly
created electrons/holes, and, after a series of reactions,
produces various ROS.7−9 The key parameters influencing
this process are an appropriate band gap, a low recombination
rate, and ozone and pollutant adsorption capabilities.7,9
Therefore, among the plethora of available photocatalytic
materials, titanium dioxide (TiO2) and graphitic carbon nitride
(g-C3N4 or simply C3N4) have emerged as frontrunners due to
their exceptional photocatalytic properties. Both materials
exhibit different decontamination behavior while used in the
photocatalytic ozonation process. The photocatalytic activity
of TiO2 primarily involves the generation of electron−hole
pairs upon light absorption with simultaneous adsorption of
ozone molecules, followed by redox reactions that lead to the
formation of surface-bound ozone species, which are later
transformed into ROS. These interactions result in large
amounts of ROS in the form of hydroxyl radicals, bound
hydroxyl radicals, and/or positive holes. The ozone species and
newly created ROS can react with organic pollutants or
participate in redox reactions, contributing to the degradation
process.6,10 The degradation activity depends on the crystal
structure of TiO2, and it is believed that although rutile
generates more hydroxyl radical species, they are strongly
bound to the catalyst, leading to overall poorer performance.6
On the other hand, C3N4, due to the higher-lying conduction
band (−1.3 V in comparison to −0.1 V vs NHE for C3N4 and
TiO2, respectively), exhibits a different reaction pathway in
photocatalytic ozonation. When exposed to ozone molecules,
first O2●− species are formed, which consequently react with
O3 causing formation of O3●− which further are transformed
into hydroxyl radicals; in addition, during photocatalytic
oxidation, ●OH are formed from O2●− and H2O2.11,12 All of
these ROS play a crucial role in oxidizing organic pollutants,
facilitating their degradation into harmless byproducts.
Despite their differences in ROS production, both catalysts
share some similarities. TiO2 and C3N4 are known for their
reasonable cost, abundance, chemical photostability, semiconductive character, and the possibility to tailor their
properties according to needs.13−15 Unfortunately, both
materials are also characterized by high recombination rates
of electrons/holes. In addition, in the case of TiO2, the band
gap is ∼3.2 eV,8 which limits the utilization of sunlight to ∼5%
(the percentage of UV in the sunlight spectrum reaching
Earth’s surface16). On the other hand, C3N4 possesses a highlaying valence band which hinders its catalytic activity.17

adopted to enhance the both of these materials’ photocatalytic
activity. Previous works describe the successful construction of

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Various techniques, including XRD, SEM, TEM, photoluminescence (PL), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS),
EDX, UV−Vis/DRS and Raman, were employed to assess the
optical characteristics, structural morphology, and chemical
compositions. The obtained results for these techniques are
elaborated upon in the following sections. The catalytic activity
of catalysts has been investigated toward ARGs inactivation as
well as sulfamethoxazole removal. The mechanism of action
has been also checked leading to find the differences between
investigated catalysts.

This study evaluates photocatalytic ozonation efficacy for inactivating
antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs: blaTEM, ermB, qnrS, tetM), the class

23S rDNA in secondary effluent. Samples were collected weekly from
the Karlsruhe-Neureut WWTP (Germany), with ARG/ARB

reaction (qPCR) protocols detailed in our prior publications.24,27

were investigated toward sulfamethoxazole (SMX) degradation. SMX
concentrations were measured using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II

matches with the morphology observed via SEM and HAADFSTEM imaging. As expected for both TiO2 and C3N4 supports,
the incorporation of bimetallic Pd/Cu nanoparticles led to a
decrease in the Brunauer−Emmett−Teller (BET)-specific
surface area (Table S2). The BET surface area of pristine
TiO2 dropped from 10.5 m2/g to 5.6 m2/g for the (Pd/

apparent shift toward larger diameters (from 20.4 to 49.8 nm)
following bimetallic loading. A similar trend of decreasing
surface area and increasing average pore size was observed for
the C3N4-based photocatalysts at higher metal loadings. Rather
than indicating a structural expansion of the semiconductor
support, this shift is attributed to a selective pore-blocking
mechanism. The photodeposited Pd/Cu nanoparticles preferentially nucleate within or physically block the smaller
mesopores of the pristine support. Consequently, these narrow
channels become inaccessible to nitrogen gas, which artificially
shifts the mathematical average of the remaining accessible
pore volume toward larger, interparticle macroporous voids.
The observed decrease in specific surface area accompanied by
an increase in average pore diameter is well known in the
literature for supported metal nanoparticles.51,52 It indicates
that during the photodeposition process, the bimetallic Pd/Cu
nanoparticles preferentially nucleate within or physically block
the smaller mesopores of the support. Consequently, these
smaller pores become inaccessible to nitrogen gas during the
physisorption measurement, which reduces the total specific

surface area and artificially shifts the mathematical average of
the remaining accessible pore size distribution toward larger,
interparticle macropores.53

inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy

confirming its safety for environmental discharge.
Catalytic Activity

In this study, we examined how bimetallic TiO2- and C3N4based nanoscale catalysts influence photocatalytic ozonation
treatment. Titanium dioxide (TiO2) in the rutile form and
graphitic carbon nitride (C3N4) have been chosen as
investigators since TiO2 has benefits such as low solubility
and high-surface area54 and the appropriate band gap of C3N4
is 2.7 eV that can be easily modified for achieving high
photocatalytic efficiency.55,56 The materials can currently
comprise a variety of compositions, morphologies, and
topologies (spheres, nanotubes, nanowires, nanorods, powders,
thin films).57 It is well known that morphology is important for
microscale materials, since it is related to the proficiency of
contaminants removal, and nanoscale materials are highly
effective due to their multidimensional properties, such as
small size and diverse morphology.58,59 In the catalyst
ozonation process, nanoscale catalysts outperform microscale
ones in catalytic activity and reaction efficiency due to their
high surface-to-volume ratios and abundant, evenly distributed
active sites. These features enhance ozone adsorption and
activation, enable rapid mass transfer, and promote efficient
ozone decomposition, resulting in faster reaction kinetics and
superior practical performance.60 Therefore, the nanoscale
bimetallic TiO2 and C3N4 catalysts have been employed for
SMX and AMR degradation. One of the most important

nanoparticles result in a higher rate of contamination

that the 1% Pd−1% Cu (wt %) bimetallic catalyst was one of
the most potential candidate in nitrate reduction.74 Furthermore, according to,75 TiO2-based catalysts with the least
amount of metal may diminish the scavenging impact of

recombination phenomenon. However, the outcomes of our

Although 0.1 wt %-doped catalysts indicated the best
performance, the dependence was different for SMX removal
in MQ water. Moreover, according to the results of the
regrowth research, the 1.0 wt % Cu/Pd-doped catalysts
exhibited the highest inhibitory performance on post-treatment

area is noticeably reduced after Pd/Cu modification, the
enhanced efficiency of the bimetallic samples toward
taxonomic and ARG deactivation cannot be ascribed to an
increase in physical adsorption sites. Instead, this behavior
highlights a critical operational and biological design paradox.
Lower metal loadings (0.1 and 0.5 wt %) provide a highly
active, highly dispersed distribution of ultrasmall cocatalyst
nanoparticles on the support surface, optimizing initial
electron−hole pair separation and localized surface plasmon
resonance (LSPR) effects.73 However, while these dispersed
sites excel at the rapid primary chemical degradation of
dissolved compounds, they generate a milder, highly diffuse
oxidative environment that inflicts only sublethal cellular and
membrane injuries on robust bacterial populations. During
subsequent post-treatment dark storage, surviving bacteria are
capable of repairing their damaged membranes and resuscitating. To achieve complete, sustained disinfection and prevent
post-treatment regrowth, a higher density of active metal
cocatalyst sites (1.0 wt %) is strictly indispensable. The
localized concentration of Pd/Cu cocatalysts at 1.0 wt % drives
a rapid, massive burst of highly localized reactive species that

inflicts irreversible lipid peroxidation and complete physical
lysis of the bacterial cell wall, thereby permanently destroying
the cellular machinery required for DNA repair.

removing ARB and ABGs living in municipal wastewater

the presence of ozone-resistant microorganisms that are hardly
removed even when the ozone dose is increased. The purpose

pollutants. The transferred ozone dose (TOD) was measured

water matrix, and other parameters all influence transfer
yield.76 To put it another way, the ozone mass transfer yield
was calculated as the ratio of transferred to injected ozone
doses.

In this study, we sought to unravel the intricate mechanisms
and reaction pathways underlying Pd/Cu bimetallic TiO2- and
C3N4-based photocatalytic processes. To investigate the role of
ROS in photocatalytic degradation, as a first step the
photocatalytic oxidation of SMX using (Pd/Cu)1%TiO2 and
(Pd/Cu)1%C3N4 was carried out in the presence of ROS
scavengers. The trapping experiments were carried out by
using different scavengers to detect the involved species. It is
well known that t-BuOH reacts with OH radicals (7.6 × 108
M−1 s−1),78 therefore it was selected to capture •OH.
Ammonium oxalate (AO) was used as a hole scavenger (h+)
under visible light.79 Sodium azide (NaN3) was selected due to
its high reactivity toward singlet oxygen. However, it should be
noted that NaN3 is efficient in singlet oxygen quenching (2 ×
109 M−1 s−1) but also may quench effectively hydroxyl radicals
(1 × 109 M−1 s−1).80 1,4-benzoquinone (p-BQ) was applied as
a superoxide radical ion (O2•−, 1.1 × 109 M−1 s−1).81 However,
p-BQ can also react with ●OH with the rate constant 1.2 × 109
M−1 s−1.82 Additionally, the reaction was carried out in the
presence of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase as O2•−
and H2O2 scavengers, respectively.83

provokes a significant reduction of the degradation efficiency

(Pd/Cu)1%C3N4 is employed, the oxidation mechanism is
dominated by the superoxide radical anion. Due to the
insufficient valence band potential of C3N4, direct water
oxidation to hydroxyl radicals is thermodynamically unfavorable. Nevertheless, O2●− can participate directly in oxidation
reactions, act as a precursor for 1O2 formation, and contribute
indirectly to trace hydroxyl radical generation via localized
metal-mediated pathways. These include in situ H 2 O 2
formation followed by Cu(I)-driven Fenton-like reactions
occurring at Pd/Cu surface sites. The results confirm that
H2O2 is also present in the system during both photocatalytic
processes. However, its presence is strongly related to the

the role of ROS can be overlooked or underestimated.100 Thus,
the ozone consumption during scavenging experiments was
investigated. A rigorous kinetic analysis based on film theory

was applied to decouple chemical ozone consumption from
physical mass transfer dynamics. The catalytic decomposition
of dissolved ozone at the bimetallic catalyst surface creates a
steeper local ozone concentration gradient at the gas−liquid
boundary layer. This concentration gradient acts as a driving
force, increasing the apparent volumetric mass transfer
coefficient (kLa).99 However, our ozone-consumption profiles
(Figure 12) reveal a critical methodological caveat: traditional
radical scavengers (t-BuOH, NaN3, p-BQ) are not passive
observers. Instead, they directly participate in the reaction by
accelerating ozone decomposition and blocking the active
catalytic sites of the bimetallic composites. Consequently,
apparent increases in ozone consumption during scavenging
assays must be interpreted as a combined effect of mass
transfer enhancement and scavenger-induced ozone depletion,
rather than purely representing bulk reactive oxygen species
(ROS) production. This methodological challenge is highlighted when comparing our scavenger-consumption profiles
with contemporary literature,103 which relies heavily on
diagnostic scavengers and EPR spin-trapping. While EPR
provides a snapshot of radical presence, it does not account for
how these organic additives alter the physical mass transfer and
decay kinetics of gaseous ozone. As shown in our work, the

As can be seen in Figure 12a,b, sodium sulfite increased
ozone consumption during both catalytic processes, leading to
the same inhibition of ROS mechanisms. Therefore, based on
that finding, a similar influence on ARGs’ photocatalytic

abundance.
The consumption of ozone in the presence of NaN3 behaved
differently for both catalytic processes. As mentioned before,
NaN3 can react with 1O2 and ●OH at a similar reaction rate,
while it reacts very slowly with O3. The consumption of ozone
during the reaction with NaN3 is therefore expected.104
However, the difference in the ozone profile suggests that
during (Pd/Cu)1%C3N4 photocatalytic ozonation the consumption of NaN3 is related to 1O2 and ●OH, while in the case
of (Pd/Cu)1%TiO2 those ROS are present in smaller amounts
and are less responsible for the catalytic performance. This is
clearly seen in Figure 12c,d, where a higher abundance of 16S
rDNA, blaTEM and intl1 is observed for the reaction with (Pd/
Cu)1%C3N4, while only an influence on 16S rDNA can be
observed when the process is carried out with (Pd/Cu)1%TiO2.
The experiments with t-BuOH were performed only with (Pd/
Cu)1%TiO2 photocatalytic ozonation, leading to increased
ozone consumption; however, the only visible effect on 16S
rDNA can be seen during that experiment. Surprisingly, the
scavenging experiment with p-BQ during (Pd/Cu)1%C3N4
photocatalytic ozonation caused a slight inhibition in ozone
consumption. It can be related to blocking the active sites of
the catalysts,91 or it can indicate that ozone is not taking part in
the scavenging reaction. The influence of p-BQ on 16S rDNA,
blaTEM, and intl1 is visible and can also be related to O2●− and

Notably, when focusing on TiO2-based photocatalytic
ozonation, the scavengers had limited influence on the
elimination of intl1, indicating that ROS such as hydroxyl
radicals, singlet oxygen, and ozone might not be the primary
drivers of intl1 deactivation; however, in the case of 16S rDNA,
the ROS influence is noticeable. Considering intl1, it is
plausible that alternative mechanisms involving the participation of holes, electrons, or other mechanical pathways
should be considered. This can be supported by XPS analysis,
where palladium existed primarily in a metallic, zero-valent
state, acting as an electron sink to facilitate direct charge
transfer. Therefore, this suggests a mechanism driven by direct
hole or electron transfer rather than a bulk radical attack.

The proposed divergence in the reaction pathway, where
C3N4 operates via a dominant bulk-phase ROS mechanism
(1O2 and O2•−) while TiO2 relies on a direct surface-bound
charge-transfer pathway, is strongly supported by both our
previous photoluminescence data24 and current XPS observations. In our previous work, the PL spectra of bimetallic TiO2−
CuPd showed a near-complete quenching of excitonic
recombination, proving that photodeposited Pd acts as a
powerful Schottky electron trap. This is chemically validated
by our XPS results (Figure 5), which reveal that palladium
exists predominantly in its zero-valent metallic state (Pd0, up to
75%) on the TiO2 support, compared to only 17% on the
C3N4 support. On TiO2, the Pd0 nanoparticles act as highly
efficient electron sinks, establishing a Schottky barrier at the
metal−semiconductor interface that drives rapid charge
separation. This spatial separation allows photogenerated
holes (h+) to accumulate at the TiO2 surface, where they
can directly oxidize adsorbed organic targets and deactivate
recalcitrant genetic sequences like intl1 via direct charge
transfer, bypassing the necessity of bulk radical intermediates
and rendering the process remarkably resistant to conventional
chemical scavengers. This model of surface-confined direct
charge transfer aligns with state-of-the-art semiconductor
heterojunction theories,105 which demonstrate that localized
metallic junctions create a powerful internal electric field that
guides photogenerated carriers directly to adsorbed substrates,
minimizing energy-loss through bulk solution recombination.
Conversely, in the context of C3N4-based photocatalytic
ozonation, the scavengers employed exhibited a considerable
reduction in catalytic activity. Specifically, sodium azide and
benzoquinone demonstrated pronounced impacts on the
removal of the 16S rDNA gene and intL1, respectively.
Thus, it can be inferred that superoxide ion radicals, ozone,
and singlet oxygen collectively contribute to the elimination of
ARGs, thereby elucidating the more efficient removal of ARGs
observed with the C3N4 catalyst compared to that with TiO2.
This implies that in C3N4-based treatment processes, ROS
plays a dominant role in pollutant removal, whereas other
factors may underlie the removal mechanisms mediated by
TiO2-based AOPs. These findings can be also supported by
XPS analysis, where in the case of C3N4-based catalysts
palladium was predominantly oxidized (Pd4+), while copper
existed largely as Cu+, creating a redox-active surface conducive
to the generation of ROS.
Overall, our results underscore the notion that distinct AOP
conditions may exhibit diverse treatment mechanisms, with
scavenger agents proving to be instrumental in unveiling the
major influences in photocatalytic ozonation. However, further
research is warranted to ascertain the primary hydroxyl radicals
involved in the treatment processes. Moreover, as can be seen,
the 1 wt % Cu/Pd catalyst treatment approach performed
badly in terms of removing ARGs, which is also reflected in the
worst boosting of ozone transfer. Nevertheless, both 1 wt %
Cu/Pd catalysts were effective in terms of preventing bacterial
regrowth. However, regarding the efficiency toward blaTEM
including bacterial regrowth (Figure S6), the abundance for
both catalytic ozonation processes was similar. This might be
explained by the mechanism which states that because the two
catalysts had distinct mechanisms of action, ROS did not play a
significant role in the deactivation of the ARGs.

CONCLUSIONS
This study successfully elucidated the distinct mechanisms and
efficiencies of bimetallic Pd/Cu-modified TiO2 and C3N4
catalysts in the photocatalytic ozonation of sulfamethoxazole
(SMX) and antibiotic-resistant genes (ARGs). Both bimetallic
catalysts significantly enhanced degradation rates compared to
those of ozonation alone, with (Pd/Cu)C3N4 generally
exhibiting higher pseudo-first-order rate constants than (Pd/
Cu)TiO2.
The investigation of reaction mechanisms using radical
scavengers revealed fundamental differences between the two
materials. For C3N4-based photocatalytic ozonation specifically, superoxide radicals, singlet oxygen, and ozone play a
dominant role in pollutant elimination. Conversely, TiO2based processes exhibited a different behavior, where ROS
appeared less critical for the deactivation of specific targets like
intl1, suggesting the involvement of alternative pathways such
as direct hole or electron interactions.
Furthermore, the study highlighted a trade-off between the
removal efficiency and regrowth inhibition. While lower metal
loadings (0.1 and 0.5 wt %) demonstrated the highest
performance for the initial removal of ARGs, the 1 wt % Pd/
Cu loading was necessary to effectively prevent bacterial
regrowth after treatment. Additionally, the efficiency of both
systems was influenced by the water matrix; the presence of
EfDOM in secondary effluents increased the required ozone
dose by competing for oxidants and affecting the ozone
decomposition profiles.
Ultimately, (Pd/Cu)0.5%C3N4 demonstrated the best overall
performance in enhancing ozone mass transfer and removing
ARGs driven by its capacity to accelerate ozone consumption
through a variety of ROS. These findings underscore that while
both catalysts are effective, their optimal application depends
on the specific target pollutants and the necessity of controlling
bacterial regrowth with C3N4 showing particular promise for
ROS-driven remediation strategies.
