Enhanced catalytic reduction of 4-nitrophenol
using magnetic Ni–Ag bimetal supported on
reduced graphene oxide
Yue Li, Jiaxin Li, Fatang Tan,

* Wei Wang

and Xueliang Qiao

Designing a catalyst with high activity and good recyclability for the reduction of 4-nitrophenol (4-NP) is
a challenge of great signiﬁcance. Herein, we report a one-step synthesis of highly eﬀective and
magnetically recyclable Ni–Ag bimetal supported on reduced graphene oxide (Ni–Ag/rGO), carried out
via the co-reduction of Ag+, Ni2+, and graphene oxide (GO) using hydrazine hydrate as a reducing agent.
The morphologies and structures of the prepared Ni–Ag/rGO composites were investigated using XRD,
SEM, and TEM. The catalytic performance of the obtained Ni–Ag/rGO catalysts with diﬀerent contents of
rGO (0, 0.1, 0.5, and 1 wt%) was also studied. The results showed that the Ni–Ag/rGO catalyst with
0.5 wt% rGO exhibited higher catalytic activity, with a reaction rate constant of 0.8585 min−1, which is
3.4 times that of the catalyst without GO. The enhanced catalytic activity of the Ni–Ag/rGO catalyst with
0.5 wt% rGO can be attributed to the improved dispersity of Ni–Ag bimetallic nanoparticles (NPs) and

Introduction

Environmental pollution, especially water pollution, has
intensied with the rapid development of the economy,
industry, and agriculture. Tremendous amounts of wastewater
containing organic pollutants are discharged from many
industries, such as textiles, paper, iron-steel, petroleum, pesticides, and pharmaceuticals,1 which can harm human beings
through biomagnication in the food chain.2 Among these
organic pollutants, 4-nitrophenol (4-NP), as one of the most
toxic pollutants, has attracted the attention of many
researchers. In recent years, great eﬀorts have been made to
reduce toxic 4-NP to 4-aminophenol (4-AP), which is a ne
organic chemical intermediate for the production of analgesics,
antipyretic drugs, photographic developers, antioxidants,
petroleum product additives, etc.3–7 Reduction of 4-NP to 4-AP by
sodium borohydride (NaBH4) is an eﬀective method to treat
wastewater containing 4-NP, which is theoretically feasible in
terms of thermodynamics, but restricted by kinetics without
suitable catalysts.8 The conversion of 4-NP to 4-AP essentially
corresponds to the multi-step hydrogenation–reduction of nitro
groups. The degradation of prevalent toxic industrial contaminants, such as nitrobenzene and azobenzene, follows identical

hydrogen-involving pathways. All these processes rely on the
active sites of the catalyst to facilitate electron–proton transfer
and unsaturated-bond scission.9,10 Given this shared reaction
mechanism, catalysts capable of eﬃcient 4-NP hydrogenation
should, in terms of structural and electronic modulation, also
work for the hydrogenative breakdown of other nitrogencontaining pollutants, including nitrobenzene and azobenzene. Hence, materials with outstanding hydrogenation
activity can not only treat 4-NP-contaminated wastewater but
also have broad application potential for the remediation of
various nitro- and azo-based organic contaminants.
In the last decade, NPs of precious metals such as gold (Au),
silver (Ag), and platinum (Pt) have been extensively explored due
to their potential application in catalysis.11–14 Many studies have
indicated that the performance of NPs strongly depends on
their size and shape.15,16 Smaller particles have a larger specic
surface area and more active sites on the surface, and hence
exhibit superior activity.17 Unfortunately, small-sized nanocatalysts easily agglomerate owing to the high surface free
energy, which greatly reduces their catalytic performance.18,19
In order to prevent the agglomeration of nanocatalysts,
supports with a certain stability, larger specic surface area, and
good adsorption capacity, including mesoporous silica (mSiO2),20 magnesium oxide (MgO),21 titanium dioxide (TiO2)22
and graphene oxide (GO), have been introduced to achieve good
dispersion of metal NPs.23 Supports like m-SiO2 and MgO are
able to increase the specic surface area and provide additional

adsorption capacity. Nevertheless, the supports occupy a certain
weight of the catalyst, and therefore, the relative amount of
eﬀective catalyst in the composites is reduced, and the catalyst
performance per unit mass decreases signicantly. Introducing
TiO2 can provide enhanced photocatalytic activity; however, it
has been largely limited by the photo source in practical
application. Therefore, an excellent support should not only
prevent the agglomeration of NPs but also enhance catalytic
activity when added in a small amount, leading to high catalytic
eﬃciency and stability of the catalyst during long-term
utilization.
Graphene is a prevalent support for NPs due to its unique
thermal, mechanical, and electrical properties and large surface
area.24,25 The functional groups (such as hydroxyl, epoxy groups,
and carboxyl groups), which provide abundant active sites for
metal NPs and the various contaminants in aqueous solution,26,27 can eﬀectively prevent the agglomeration of NPs. The
catalytic reduction of 4-NP is mainly a process of electron
transfer, which plays a signicant role in the catalytic reaction.
The excellent conductivity of graphene could accelerate electron
transfer and improve catalytic activity.24 Additionally, metal NPs
supported on reduced graphene oxide (rGO) could generate
a strong metal–support interaction (SMSI) and enhance the
electron transfer at the metal/graphene interfaces.28 This electron transfer could adjust the electronic states of the metal NPs
to bring about unexpected catalytic enhancements.
Single-component noble-metal catalysts are limited by their
prohibitive cost. Since only one active component is in eﬀect,
their reaction kinetics cannot be optimized through the electronic synergy between dissimilar metals. For this reason,
researchers generally introduce a secondary metal into noblemetal systems. The electronic interaction at the bimetallic
hetero-interface improves catalytic activity and endows the
material with magnetic responsiveness for convenient magnetic
separation and recovery. For instance, Abdullah et al. fabricated
Ni@Mg co-doped Zn(O,S) solid-solution photocatalysts. Their
work conrmed that synergistic Ni and Mg doping greatly
increases the surface oxygen-vacancy concentration and accelerates photogenerated-carrier separation and interfacial charge
transfer. The modied catalyst delivers improved performance
across multiple redox reactions, including photocatalytic
hydrogen evolution, organic hydrogenation, heavy-metal
reduction, and organic-dye degradation.29 Xia and co-authors
prepared a Fe@Ag bimetallic NP catalyst, which exhibited
superior recycling performance and could be magnetically
recovered readily from the solution without ltering.30 However,
Fe is easily oxidized, and the leakage of Fe ions might result in
secondary pollution during utilization. Compared with ironbased materials, metallic nickel exhibits superior oxidation
and corrosion resistance. Additionally, its intrinsic catalytic
activity toward 4-NP hydrogenation and non-toxic properties
also make Ni environmentally friendly.31–34 As demonstrated by
Abdullah et al., nickel-based cocatalysts, including oxyhydroxides, phosphates and phosphides, can eﬀectively reduce the
energy barrier of the four-electron water oxidation reaction.
Such materials can capture photogenerated holes and inhibit
charge carrier recombination, thereby signicantly accelerating

the kinetic process of photoelectrocatalytic oxidation.35 Metallic
Ag can modulate semiconductor defects and band structures
through lattice doping, which suppresses carrier recombination
and optimizes interfacial electron transport eﬃciency. Saragih
et al. conrmed that the substitution of Cu sites in quaternary
selenide lattices with Ag can substantially eliminate Cu–Zn
antisite defects that act as charge recombination centers. This
strategy improves carrier mobility and eﬃciently promotes
interfacial electron transfer.36 When Ni and Ag are integrated
into a bimetallic system, a built-in electric eld is generated at
the heterogeneous interface. This eld regulates the directional
migration of electrons and induces prominent bimetallic
synergism, endowing the Ni–Ag catalyst with distinctly
enhanced catalytic performance compared with monometallic
Ag nanoparticles.
However, most currently reported Ni–Ag bimetallic catalysts
rely on cumbersome fabrication procedures involving multistep impregnation and high-temperature calcination. Herein,
we propose a facile method for the synthesis of a Ni–Ag/rGO
catalyst via the co-reduction of Ag+, Ni2+, and GO using hydrazine hydrate as a reducing agent. The addition of 0.5 wt% GO
improves the catalytic activity of the catalyst greatly by preventing agglomeration and accelerating electron transfer,
achieving a rate constant of 0.8585 min−1, which is higher than
most of the previously reported results. In addition, due to the
stability of Ni, the catalyst could be magnetically recovered and
recycled for use.

2 Experimental section
2.1

Materials

Natural ake graphite was purchased from Tianjin Dengfeng
Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Nickel nitrate hexahydrate
(Ni(NO3)2$6H2O), silver nitrate (AgNO3), sodium hydroxide
(NaOH), hydrazine hydrate (N2H4$H2O, 85%), anhydrous
ethanol, sodium borohydride (NaBH4) and 4-nitrophenol (4C6H5NO3) were all purchased from Sinopharm Chemical
Reagent Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Deionized (DI) water was
used in all of the experiments. All the reagents used in this work
were of analytical grade and were used without further purication or treatment.
2.2

Synthesis of Ni–Ag/rGO

In this study, Ni–Ag/rGO nanocomposites were successfully
synthesized through a novel co-reduction method. First, the GO
used in this work was obtained from natural ake graphite
according to the modied Hummers' method.37 To prepare Ni–
Ag/rGO nanocomposites, 16.6 mg rGO was dispersed in 20 mL
DI water and sonicated for 30 min. Then, 1 mmol Ni(NO3)2$6H2O and 1 mmol AgNO3 were dissolved and mixed thoroughly
in 50 mL DI water, followed by the addition of diﬀerent volumes
(0, 0.2, 1, 2 mL) of rGO solution to obtain diﬀerent rGO weight
fractions (0.0, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0 wt%). Aer 5 min of stirring,
5 mol L−1 NaOH solution was added dropwise until the pH of
the mixed solution reached 14, and then 150 mL hydrazine
hydrate (85 wt%) solution was added to the above solution as

Fig. 3 shows the Ni 2p XPS spectra of Ni–Ag/rGO and Ni–Ag.
Each spectrum consists of three peaks. Note that the binding
energies (BEs) of both Ni 2p peaks of Ni–Ag/rGO exhibit a positive shi of 0.5 eV compared to those of unsupported Ni–Ag.
This positive shi suggests that some electrons have been
transferred from the Ni–Ag metals to the rGO support, indicating the electronic modication of the Ni–Ag NPs by rGO.44
This interfacial charge-transfer behaviour originates from the
Fermi-level diﬀerence between the metallic phase and carbon
support. Since metallic Ni and Ag exhibit higher Fermi levels
than rGO, free electrons spontaneously ow from the highenergy Ni–Ag bimetallic phase to the low-energy conjugated
carbon framework of rGO once an intimate hetero-interface
contact is established, until Fermi-level equilibrium is
attained for both phases.45,46 The built-in electric eld formed at
the Ni–Ag hetero-interface reduces the barrier for internal
electron transport and continuously supplies abundant free
electrons. Additionally, rGO possesses an intact sp2-conjugated
plane with outstanding electron conduction and delocalisation
capacity. It can rapidly capture electrons transferred from the
metal and eﬀectively suppress charge-carrier recombination.47

Catalytic mechanisms of Ni–Ag/rGO

It is well known that the reduction of 4-NP by BH4− is governed
by two aspects. One is the eﬃcient adsorption of reactants, and
the other is the rapid hydrogen transfer from BH4− to 4-NP over
the catalysts.55 Here, the reduction of 4-NP catalyzed by Ni–Ag/
rGO nanocomposites was carried out via a similar mechanism. Firstly, Ni–Ag bimetal captures the electrons from BH4−
ions and then transfers the electrons to rGO sheets, making the
catalysts electrophilic.56 In addition, the solution becomes
slightly alkaline due to the generation of BH4− from NaBH4,

4 Conclusion
In summary, a facile co-reduction process for the synthesis of
Ni–Ag/rGO nanocomposites was proposed using rGO as
a support for the Ni/Ag bimetal. Catalysts with diﬀerent weight
fractions of GO were synthesized, and the eﬀect of the amount
of rGO on the catalytic performance was studied. Ni–Ag/rGO
nanocomposites with a moderate amount of rGO support prevented the agglomeration of the NPs and signicantly improved
the catalytic activity of the catalyst. Owing to the SMSI eﬀect and
uniform dispersion induced by rGO, the Ni–Ag/rGO catalyst
with 0.5 wt% GO showed the highest rate constant towards the
reduction of 4-NP, which was 3.4 times that without GO. The
enhanced catalytic activity of the Ni–Ag/rGO catalyst with
0.5 wt% GO could be attributed to the improved dispersion of
the Ni–Ag bimetallic NPs and acceleration of electron transfer
between the catalyst and rGO. The Ni–Ag/rGO catalyst also
exhibits good magnetic recyclability and stability, with only 8%
loss aer 10 cycles, providing a potential solution for treating 4NP in practical applications.
