ABSTRACT: Carbide slag (CS), a Ca-rich industrial residue, was
investigated as a calcium source for enhancing steam gasification of
sewage sludge (SS) and mitigating heavy metal risks in bottom
residues. Gasification performance and heavy-metal behavior across
Ca/C ratios and temperatures were assessed using apparent
retention, Community Bureau of Reference (BCR) sequential
extraction, the toxicity characteristic leaching procedure (TCLP),
and the risk assessment code (RAC). At 700 °C, increasing Ca/C
from 0 to 0.75 raised the H2 volume fraction from 57.73 to 68.02
vol %, H2 yield from 207.40 to 332.85 mL g−1, and cold gas
efficiency from 28.09 to 42.24%. CS addition increased the
apparent retention of Zn, Pb, and Cd and was accompanied by
redistribution from the F1/F2 fractions toward the F3/F4 fractions
and by lower TCLP leaching concentrations; the RAC risk levels of Zn and Cd decreased from high to medium. However, CS was
largely ineffective for As immobilization because its apparent retention, BCR fraction distribution, and TCLP leaching behavior
changed only slightly, while As remained at medium RAC risk. At Ca/C = 0.50, increasing temperature from 650 to 800 °C
increased H2 yield and cold gas efficiency but decreased the H2 volume fraction and H2/CO ratio. Higher temperature reduced the
potential mobility and TCLP leachability of metals retained in the collected bottom residues but decreased the apparent retention of
Zn, Pb, Cd, and As. Overall, under the investigated conditions, Ca/C = 0.50 and 700−750 °C provided a representative compromise
between hydrogen-rich gas production, CS dosage, operating-temperature requirements, and the environmental response of the
collected bottom residues.

1. INTRODUCTION
Municipal sewage sludge is a representative waste generated
during urban wastewater treatment. It is characterized by high
moisture content, high ash content, and complex composition.
Sewage sludge contains recoverable organic matter, nitrogen,
and phosphorus, while also concentrating potentially hazardous heavy metals such as Zn, Cu, Pb, Cr, Ni, Cd, and As.
Improper disposal can therefore cause resource loss, heavy
metal release, and secondary pollution risks.1,2 Compared with
landfilling, composting, and direct incineration, gasification
converts the organic fraction of sludge into combustible gases
such as H2, CO, and CH4 under oxygen-deficient or oxygenlimited conditions, while most inorganic constituents are
retained in solid residues. Gasification is therefore considered
an important thermochemical route for sludge volume
reduction, energy recovery, and inorganic resource recovery.2−4 However, sludge gasification should be evaluated not
only in terms of syngas quality and energy conversion
efficiency but also in terms of heavy metal stability and
leaching risk in the gasification residues.

Carbide slag is a large-volume alkaline industrial solid waste
produced during acetylene or poly(vinyl chloride) production
using the calcium carbide route. Carbide slag typically contains
Ca(OH)2, CaCO3, CaO, and other calcium-based constituents
and is characterized by high calcium content, high alkalinity,
and low cost. As a waste-derived calcium source, carbide slag
has the potential to replace part of commercial CaO in
sorption-enhanced gasification. Han et al.14 used bauxitemodified carbide slag for calcium-looping gasification of
sewage sludge and found that it increased H2 yield and
gasification efficiency, mainly through in situ CO2 capture by
calcium-based components and gasification enhancement. In
coal and biomass systems, carbide slag has also been shown to
promote steam gasification, enhance hydrogen-rich gas
generation, and exhibit potential for CO2 capture and
multicycle gasification.15−17 These studies indicate that carbide
slag has a sound basis for process intensification in steam
gasification systems. However, sewage sludge differs from coal
and common biomass because of its high ash, high
phosphorus, and heavy metal contents. The role of carbide
slag should therefore not be interpreted only from the
perspective of gas-phase product regulation. Its effects on
heavy metal stabilization and environmental risk in gasification
residues also require further evaluation.
The environmental behavior of heavy metals is an important
consideration during the thermochemical conversion of sewage
sludge. During gasification, some semivolatile elements may
migrate to fly ash, condensates, or the gas phase as temperature
increases, whereas others remain in bottom residues. Their
environmental risk depends on chemical speciation and
leaching behavior. Ewald et al.18 investigated entrained-flow
gasification of sewage sludge and found that Hg, Tl, Cd, Pb,
As, Zn, Cu, and Ni volatilized to different extents, while
inorganic constituents such as P and K were mainly enriched in
gasification ash. Further research has shown that temperature,
reaction atmosphere, and additives affect heavy metal
evaporation and retention during sludge gasification.19 Other
studies have emphasized that the environmental risk of heavy
metals in sludge pyrolysis or gasification residues should not be
assessed only by total concentrations but should be evaluated
using chemical speciation, leaching concentrations, and risk
indices.20 Minerals and Ca-containing additives, including
CaO, montmorillonite, calcium aluminosilicates, and calcium
phosphates, have been shown to affect heavy metal

volatilization, speciation transformation, and leaching risk
during sludge thermal treatment.21−24 BCR sequential
extraction, TCLP leaching tests, and RAC risk assessment
are commonly used to evaluate the potential mobility and
environmental safety of heavy metals in sludge thermaltreatment residues.25 It is therefore necessary to clarify whether
carbide slag can simultaneously enhance hydrogen-rich gas
generation and decrease the active fractions and leaching risks
of heavy metals in gasification residues.
Current studies on carbide slag-enhanced sludge gasification
mainly focus on H2 generation and CO2 capture, whereas a
systematic understanding of the apparent retention, speciation
transformation, and leaching risks of heavy metals in
gasification residues remains limited. In particular, direct
experimental evidence is still lacking on whether hydrogenrich gas production and heavy metal stabilization can be
synchronously regulated under different carbide slag dosages
and gasification temperatures. Accordingly, this study used
municipal sewage sludge and carbide slag as feedstocks and
selected the Ca/C molar ratio and gasification temperature as
the main variables. Gas composition, H2 yield, total gas yield,
and cold gas efficiency were investigated during carbide slagassisted sludge steam gasification. The apparent retention rates,
BCR speciation distributions, TCLP leaching concentrations,
and RAC risk levels of Zn, Cu, Pb, Cr, Ni, Cd, and As in
gasification residues were further evaluated. The objective was
to reveal the response patterns of hydrogen-rich gas generation
and heavy metal stabilization in carbide slag-assisted sludge
steam gasification and to provide a basis for energy recovery
and environmental risk control during the coutilization of
sewage sludge and carbide slag.

2. MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1. Feedstocks
Municipal sewage sludge was collected from the dewatered sludge
stream of the Shenyang North Wastewater Treatment Plant. The asreceived dewatered sludge had a moisture content of approximately
80 wt %. After transport to the laboratory, visible impurities were
removed. The sample was then dried in a forced-air oven at 105 °C
for 24 h, ground, sieved to 0.10−0.15 mm, sealed, and stored for
subsequent use. The prepared sample was denoted as SS. At the time
of characterization, SS had a measured moisture content of 4.75 wt %.
Therefore, the proximate and ultimate analysis results and the lower
heating value reported in Table 1 are all expressed on an air-dried
basis (ad), rather than on an as-received or dry basis. Proximate
analysis was conducted using a proximate analyzer (TGA2000, Las
Navas, Spain). The C, H, N, and S contents were determined using an
elemental analyzer (CE-440, Exeter Analytical, USA), and the O
content on the same basis was calculated by difference. The lower
heating value was measured using an oxygen bomb calorimeter (Parr
6300, Parr Instrument, USA). The proximate analysis, ultimate
analysis, and lower heating value of SS are listed in Table 1.

sample was dried at 105 °C for 12 h to remove free water, ground,

2.2. Steam Gasification Experiments
Steam gasification of sewage sludge was carried out in a laboratoryscale fixed-bed reaction system consisting mainly of a gas supply unit,
steam generator, tube furnace, condensation and purification unit, and
online gas analyzer, as shown in Figure 1. The quartz-tube reactor had
an inner diameter of 22 mm and a total length of 1200 mm, with a
400 mm isothermal zone. Deionized water was continuously delivered
into the steam generator using a peristaltic pump to produce stable
steam. N2 was used as the carrier gas and internal standard gas. The
outlet gas was condensed and purified before entering the online gas
analyzer for continuous detection of H2, CO, CO2, and CH4.
The Ca/C molar ratio was defined as the molar amount of total
elemental Ca in CS relative to the molar amount of C in SS. The CaO
value of 90.54 wt % reported by XRF represents the CaO equivalent
of total elemental Ca rather than the actual content of the CaO phase.
XRF does not distinguish Ca present as CaO, Ca(OH)2, CaCO3, or
other Ca-bearing phases. Therefore, the Ca/C ratio used in this study
should be regarded as a feed-composition parameter based on total
elemental Ca rather than as the molar ratio of reactive CaO available
for CO2 capture. Accordingly, CaCO3 was not separately excluded
from the Ca/C calculation.
To evaluate the responses of SS steam gasification performance and
heavy metal stabilization to CS dosage, Ca/C molar ratios of 0, 0.25,
0.50, and 0.75 were investigated at 700 °C. For each experiment, 10.0
g of the same air-dried SS was used to maintain a constant SS carbon
input. The corresponding CS masses were 0, 3.70, 7.39, and 11.09 g,

shaking. After extraction, the samples were filtered through glass fiber

The concurrent increase in H2 and decrease in CO2 are
consistent with possible in situ CO2 capture by the calciumbased components in CS. In sorption-enhanced steam
gasification, the removal of CO2 may favor the forward
water−gas shift reaction and thereby promote H2 formation.9−11,14 Nevertheless, CO is simultaneously generated
through char−steam gasification and secondary reforming of
volatile or tar-derived species and consumed through the
water−gas shift reaction. Changes in total gas production and
the balance among these pathways may therefore result in
small nonmonotonic variations in the CO volume fraction
even when the H2 volume fraction and H2/CO ratio increase.
Accordingly, the observed gas-composition changes are
interpreted as the coupled response of possible CO2 capture
and concurrent gasification and reforming reactions rather than
as evidence that CO2 capture alone controlled the H2/CO
ratio. Because CaO-to-CaCO3 conversion, char conversion,
and tar yield were not quantified, the relative contributions of
these pathways could not be resolved in the present study.
From the perspective of overall gasification performance,
increasing CS dosage was accompanied by changes in gas
composition and increases in gas production and energy
recovery under the present experimental configuration. As the
Ca/C ratio increased from 0 to 0.75, the H2 yield increased
from 207.40 to 332.85 mL g−1, the total gas yield increased
from 359.26 to 489.34 mL g−1, and CGE increased from 28.09
to 42.24%. The increase in H2 yield indicates enhanced

reflects improved chemical energy recovery in combustible
gases. Similarly, Lin et al.28 found that lime addition and
temperature regulation significantly affected H2 generation and

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Effect of Ca/C Ratio on Sewage Sludge Steam
Gasification Performance

The gasification performance at target Ca/C molar ratios of 0,
0.25, 0.50, and 0.75 at 700 °C is shown in Figure 2. As
described in Section 2.2, the SS mass and operating conditions
were kept constant, whereas increasing Ca/C increased the CS
charge and total solid loading. Although uniform mixing,
sample spreading, and placement within the same isothermal
zone were used to reduce physical differences, changes in layer
thickness, thermal inertia, porosity, and local gas−solid contact
cannot be fully excluded. Therefore, the following results
represent the overall response to increasing CS dosage under
the present reactor configuration rather than chemically
isolated effects of Ca alone.
As the Ca/C ratio increased from 0 to 0.75, the H2 volume
fraction increased from 57.73 to 68.02 vol %, whereas the CO2
volume fraction decreased from 23.69 to 14.26 vol %. The H2/
CO ratio increased concurrently from 4.19 to 5.27. This
increase does not require a monotonic decrease in the CO
volume fraction because the H2/CO ratio is determined by the
relative changes in both components. In the present experiments, the H2 volume fraction increased by 10.29 percentage
points, whereas the CO volume fraction fluctuated within a
narrow range of 12.78−13.79 vol %. Therefore, the progressive
increase in H2/CO was governed mainly by the enrichment of
H2 relative to the comparatively stable CO fraction. Moreover,
the CO volume fraction is a normalized composition
parameter and does not, by itself, represent the absolute
amount of CO produced. The CH4 volume fraction also
remained nearly constant at 4.66−4.81 vol %, indicating a

active fractions, the proportions of Zn, Pb, and Cd decreased
from 56.29, 53.04, and 64.01% to 34.97, 35.46, and 43.08%,
respectively, indicating markedly reduced potential mobility.
Studies on sludge pyrolysis and thermal treatment have
reported that heavy metal environmental risk depends not
only on total concentrations but also on chemical speciation.
Higher proportions of active fractions such as acid-soluble
species generally correspond to greater potential mobility and
environmental risk.20,25
The BCR speciation changes of Cu, Cr, and Ni were
relatively moderate. Under Ca/C = 0, the F4 fractions of Cu,
Cr, and Ni were already 39.37, 58.24, and 49.11%, respectively.
When the Ca/C ratio increased to 0.75, their F4 fractions
further increased to 45.67, 63.43, and 55.42%, while their F1
fractions decreased to 5.26, 2.56, and 4.89%, respectively. This
shows that CS still exerted a stabilizing effect on Cu, Cr, and
Ni, but because these metals already had relatively high stablefraction proportions without CS addition, the scope for further
transformation was limited. The F1 fraction of As decreased
only from 23.34 to 20.15%, and the F4 fraction increased from
35.09 to 38.03%, much less than the changes observed for Zn,
Pb, and Cd. This result further indicates that As stabilization in
the gasification residues responded weakly to CS addition. In a
related Fenton−CaO-conditioned sludge system, Jin et al.22
reported that As behavior during thermal treatment and steam
gasification was influenced by changes in oxidation state,
interactions with Ca-, Fe-, and Al-containing components, and
the gasification atmosphere. These literature-reported processes may help explain the weak response of As observed here,
although they were not directly verified in the present residues.

The TCLP leaching concentrations of heavy metals in
gasification residues under different Ca/C ratios are shown in
Figure 5a. As the Ca/C ratio increased, the TCLP leaching
concentrations of all target heavy metals decreased, indicating
that CS addition reduced environmental risk from the
perspective of leachable release. The leaching concentrations
of Zn, Pb, and Cr decreased from 2.432, 0.718, and 0.187 mg
L−1 to 1.765, 0.526, and 0.139 mg L−1, respectively,
corresponding to decreases of 27.43, 26.74, and 25.67%. The
leaching concentrations of Cu, Ni, and Cd also decreased to
0.285, 0.210, and 0.088 mg L−1, respectively. In comparison,
the As leaching concentration decreased only from 0.316 to
0.285 mg L−1, a decrease of 9.81%, consistent with its limited
BCR speciation change. These results show good correspondence between decreased BCR active fractions and decreased
TCLP leaching concentrations. However, the environmental
implications of BCR and TCLP are not identical. BCR mainly
characterizes potential heavy metal mobility, whereas TCLP
reflects actual leaching release under specific extraction
conditions.
The RAC risk levels are shown in Figure 5b. Under Ca/C =
0, Zn and Cd were classified as high risk, Pb and As as medium
risk, and Cu, Cr, and Ni as low risk. As the Ca/C ratio
increased to 0.25, both Zn and Cd decreased from high risk to
medium risk. When the Ca/C ratio further increased to 0.50
and 0.75, Zn, Pb, Cd, and As remained at medium risk,
whereas Cu, Cr, and Ni remained at low risk. This result
indicates that CS addition had a pronounced risk-reduction
effect for highly active metals such as Zn and Cd. However, As
remained at medium risk under all Ca/C ratios and should
therefore be a key element in subsequent environmental safety
control of gasification residues.

of CS differed substantially among heavy metals. Zn, Pb, and
Cd were the most sensitive to changes in the Ca/C ratio, as

3.3. Effect of Gasification Temperature on Sewage Sludge
Steam Gasification Performance

At Ca/C = 0.50, the effect of gasification temperature on
sewage sludge steam gasification performance was further
investigated. The gas composition, H2/CO ratio, H2 yield, total
gas yield, and CGE at different temperatures are shown in
Figure 6.
Overall, gasification temperature had a pronounced
influence on sludge steam gasification. As the temperature
increased from 650 to 800 °C, the total gas yield increased
from 413.03 to 553.45 mL g−1, the H2 yield increased from
282.35 to 344.47 mL g−1, and CGE increased from 36.66 to
44.79%. These results indicate that increasing temperature
improved the conversion of sludge organic matter and char
into gaseous products and increased the energy output of
combustible gases. Previous studies on sludge steam gasification have shown that higher temperatures favor volatile
release, secondary tar cracking, and char gasification reactions,
thereby increasing gas yield and cold gas efficiency.6,28 The
continuous increases in total gas yield and CGE in this study
therefore indicate that temperature elevation promoted overall
gasification conversion of sludge at Ca/C = 0.50.
The H2 volume fraction and H2 yield showed different
trends as temperature increased. The H2 volume fraction
gradually decreased from 68.36 vol % at 650 °C to 62.24 vol %
at 800 °C, whereas the H2 yield increased from 282.35 to
344.47 mL g−1. This is because the H2 volume fraction reflects
the relative H2 content of the product gas, whereas H2 yield
represents the actual amount of hydrogen produced per unit
mass of sludge. As temperature increased, the total gas yield
increased markedly, and the CO and CO2 volume fractions

Regarding gas composition, the CO volume fraction

such as C + H2O → CO + H2 and C + CO2 → 2CO proceed
more readily, thereby promoting CO formation. Higher
temperatures may also weaken the CO2 capture advantage of
Ca-based components and allow part of CO2 to participate in
the reverse water−gas shift reaction to generate CO. Previous
studies have shown that CaO-based sorbents can increase H2
concentration in gas products through in situ CO2 capture, but
this effect is influenced by reaction temperature and the
thermodynamic conditions of CaO carbonation.11,13 Therefore, the decreasing H2/CO ratio at higher temperature may
reflect coupling between intensified gasification reactions and
weakened CO2 capture capacity in the high-temperature range.

temperature. On the one hand, increasing temperature can
promote further cracking, reforming, or conversion of oxygencontaining organics and tar intermediates in sludge, increasing
CO and CO2 formation. On the other hand, CO2 capture by
Ca-based components may be limited at higher temperatures,
allowing more CO2 to remain in the gas phase. The CH4
volume fraction decreased from 5.95 to 3.47 vol %, indicating
that higher temperature favored CH4 steam reforming or
thermal cracking and its further conversion into small-molecule
gases such as H2 and CO. Lin et al.28 also found that
temperature and lime addition significantly affected gas
composition and gasification characteristics during steam
gasification of lime-dried sewage sludge.

Overall, increasing temperature had a dual effect on SS
steam gasification at Ca/C = 0.50. On the one hand, higher
temperature increased total gas yield, H2 yield, and CGE,
indicating enhanced overall gasification conversion and energy
recovery efficiency. On the other hand, increasing temperature
decreased the H2 volume fraction and H2/CO ratio and
reduced the contribution of Ca-based CO2 capture to H2
enrichment in the product gas. If H2 yield, total gas yield, and
CGE are used as the main evaluation indices, 800 °C showed
better performance. However, considering the H2 volume
fraction, H2/CO ratio, CO2 capture effect, and heating-energy
demand, 700−750 °C may provide a more balanced operating
range under the present conditions. Because tar yield, char
conversion, and CaO/CaCO3 conversion were not quantified
in this work, the effect of temperature on specific reaction
pathways still requires further validation. As gasification
temperature affects not only gas production but also apparent
retention and speciation distribution of heavy metals in
gasification residues, the following section analyzes the
influence of temperature on heavy metal stabilization and
environmental risk.

4. CONCLUSIONS

carbide slag (CS) dosage was accompanied by enhanced
hydrogen-rich gas production during sewage sludge steam
gasification. As the Ca/C ratio increased from 0 to 0.75, the H2

yield from 207.40 to 332.85 mL g−1, and the cold gas efficiency

with possible CO2 capture by Ca-bearing components and the
coupled effects of gasification, reforming, and water−gas shift
reactions. Because the gains in H2 yield and CGE diminished
above Ca/C = 0.50, this ratio was selected as a representative
compromise for subsequent temperature experiments, considering the additional CS dosage relative to the incremental
gasification improvement.
