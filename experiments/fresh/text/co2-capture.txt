ABSTRACT: Gas hydrate-based carbon capture and storage (CCUS)
technologies represent a promising approach to mitigating climate change by
efficiently capturing and storing carbon dioxide (CO2). This review examines
the technical and economic aspects of gas hydrate-based CCUS, including the
latest developments related to CO2 hydrate formation studies. With special
attention to flue gas decarbonization, this paper discusses the challenges faced
and the focus areas that need improvement in advancing and scaling up
hydrate-based CCUS. The review also provides a systematic approach to
understanding the techno-economic aspects of CCUS plants and concisely
discusses the technical opportunities and the associated cost dynamics within
various CCUS technologies. The review also discusses the integration of
hydrate-based capture with broader decarbonization strategies, underscoring
the critical role of supportive policies and technological advancements in scaling
up these solutions for global impact.

1. INTRODUCTION
Carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) are vital
strategies for mitigating carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions and
combating climate change. Gas hydrate-based carbon capture
is a promising approach within the CCUS framework, offering
a potentially efficient and long-term storage solution for
captured CO2. In the context of CCUS, gas hydrate-based
carbon capture involves the formation of CO2 hydrates, where
CO2 molecules are trapped within a lattice of water molecules
under high pressure and low temperatures.1−4 One of the key
advantages of gas hydrate-based carbon capture within the
CCUS framework is its potential for long-term storage. Once
the CO2 is captured and converted into hydrates, it can remain
stable for extended periods, reducing the risk of leakage of the
CO2 into the atmosphere.
Techno-economic analysis (TEA) is essential for evaluating
gas hydrate-based CO2 capture technologies, focusing on their
technical potential and economic feasibility within the broader
context of carbon capture and utilization (CCU). This
evaluation method meticulously examines both the attainable
benefits and the challenges associated with these technologies,
offering a systematic approach to assessing their commercial
viability. Gas hydrate-based CO2 capture involves complex
processes that have garnered significant interest owing to their
climate change mitigation potential. The technical assessment
within TEA explores the operational intricacies and efficiency
of various CCUS technologies, evaluates the technical

2. PROCESS OF HYDRATE-BASED CARBON CAPTURE
Gas hydrate nucleation is a sophisticated microscopic process
that begins with the initial aggregation of hydrate nuclei and
continues with their expansion to a critical size that supports
sustained growth. This complex sequence is influenced by
various thermodynamic and kinetic factors and is characterized
by stochastic and time-dependent properties.
A critical metric in assessing hydrate nucleation is the
induction time, which measures the period from the onset of
gas−water cluster formation to the point where these clusters
stabilize into a critical size. Induction time is a vital indicator of
the occurrence of nucleation and is subject to influence by
several variables, including chemical composition, system
configuration, and mixing conditions.13−16 The shorter
induction time suggests more favorable thermodynamic and
kinetic conditions for rapid hydrate formation.
The stochastic nature of hydrate formation is attributed to
the system’s metastability, defined as the ability of a
nonequilibrium state to persist in a seemingly stable condition
over prolonged durations. This metastability is crucial in
hydrate nucleation, as it allows for an understanding of the
variability and unpredictability in the timing and rate of
nucleation events.
In the nucleation phase, water molecules assemble around
guest atoms or molecules to form either incomplete or
complete crystalline embryos. These embryonic formations are
dynamic, continuously undergoing formation and dissolution
due to localized fluctuations in mass, temperature, and
pressure. This introduces a degree of stochasticity that is
dependent on free energy, rendering the nucleation process
fundamentally probabilistic.17−20
Upon attaining a critical size, the hydrate nuclei are capable
of surmounting the associated free energy barrier, thus marking
a pivotal transition to further growth or aggregation. This
overcoming of the energy barrier is a crucial juncture in the
nucleation phase.
Several factors significantly influence this phase transition.
Among these, the critical nucleus size denotes the minimum
size at which a hydrate nucleus becomes thermodynamically
stable and can facilitate further growth. The energy barrier
represents a thermodynamic threshold that the system must
surpass to transition from a metastable state to a more stable
configuration.21−26 The driving force for nucleation is often
linked to the chemical potential difference between phases,
which may be altered by gradients in temperature, pressure,

2.2. Gas Hydrate Nucleation Process. Gas hydrate
nucleation process is a microscopic phenomenon that refers to
the conformation and growth of hydrate nuclei into a critical
size for additional growth. It is characterized by the
determination of induction time (the time at which nucleation
occurs, which includes the formation of gas−water clusters and
their growth into stable bases with a critical size). Hydrate
nucleation is a stochastic process and time-dependent that can
last from hours to days depending on the composition, outfit,
and mixing conditions, etc. This stochastic nature of hydrate
formation results from the degree of metastability (the
capability of a nonequilibrium state to persist for a long
time). During hydrate nucleation, water particles gather
around the guest atoms, forming incomplete or complete
crystal embryos. These embryos form and shrink continuously
due to changes in local mass, temperature, and pressure. This
medium makes hydrate nucleation a free-energy-dependent
and stochastically arbitrary process. Upon attaining a critical
size, the hydrate cores overcome the free energy barrier and
move to the succeeding stage, which involves additional

Research on the CO2 hydrate formation kinetics with the
use of various additives has been ongoing due to its application
in carbon capture. There are two types of additives, generally
termed as promoters, which include kinetic and thermodynamic promoters. Thermodynamic promoters are utilized in
hydrate formation as they shift the equilibrium, which means
they reduce the high pressure to lower required for hydrate
formation. In contrast, kinetic promoters (commonly “surfactants” or “amino acids”) alter the interfacial properties during
gas/liquid contact, due to which increased hydrate formation
rates are achieved. These kinetic promoters have no effect on
the phase equilibrium curve.63 The thermodynamic and kinetic
promoters can be used together, which is termed the
synergistic effect of promoters on hydrate formation.
Thermodynamic promoters shift equilibrium conditions to
milder temperature−pressure regimes, while kinetic promoters
accelerate hydrate formation rates by improving interfacial
characteristics and reducing induction times. This results in
significant enhancement of hydrate formation kinetics, lowering the required pressure, improving hydrate selectivity, and
reducing overall energy costs.
Thermodynamic promoters (THPs) substantially reduce the
hydrate formation pressure or increase the hydrate formation
temperature, delivering milder conditions for hydrate formation. There are two types of THPs. The first group are the
one that consists of small molecules enwrapping the water
concavities as guest atoms.64,65 Tetrahydrofuran (THF) is a
prominent hydrate-forming guest that has been studied
extensively as a thermodynamic promoter for different guest
gases like methane, hydrogen, and CO2 hydrates.63 It is a
water-miscible cyclic ether that has the capability of forming a
hydrate on its own by enwrapping the large cages of sII. Other
considerably researched THPs include 1,4-dioxane, propylene
oxide, cyclopentane, propane, neohexene, acetone, and
methylcyclohexane. Still, the maximum of these chemicals is
largely unpredictable and hence are delicate to use.47
Meanwhile, the alternate group is organic phosphonium salts
and nonvolatile organic quaternary ammonium salts that
served as ionic hydrate formers. These can form semiclathrate
hydrates (SCHs) at ambient pressure and temperature.47
These are a unique group of clathrates that can form hydrate
by being blended with the host cavities, replacing the water
molecules.
Kinetic hydrate promoters are additives that allow a faster
rate of hydrate formation by reducing the induction time,
increasing the gas uptake as well as enhancing hydrate growth
kinetics. The main advantages of kinetic hydrate promoters are
that they do not occupy the water cages and are used at low

2.3. Gas Hydrate Growth Process. Following the
completion of hydrate nucleation, hydrate growth takes
place. This growth process depicts the growth of stable
hydrate nuclei into solid hydrates. The growth of hydrate
depends upon temperature, pressure, agitation, interfacial area,
and degree of subcooling. When there is a sudden sharp drop
in the pressure caused by the consumption of gas molecules, it
shows hydrate formation and growth, and the constant
pressure in the system depicts the completion of the hydrate
formation process. In addition to this, a sudden rise in
temperature of the system represents hydrate formation as it is
an exothermic process, while a constant temperature marks the
completion of the hydrate formation process.47 There are key
kinetic parameters that are studied for hydrate growth, which
include
1. Hydrate formation rate (growth rate): quantifies how
quickly hydrate crystals grow after nucleation, typically
measured as gas consumption rate (mol/min) or the rate
of crystal mass increase.
2. Gas uptake: represents the rate and total amount of gas
molecules entrapped within hydrate cages.
3. Degree of subcooling (ΔT): defined as the temperature
difference between equilibrium hydrate formation
temperature and operating temperature; greater subcooling enhances hydrate growth kinetics.
4. Activation energy (Ea): represents the energy barrier to
hydrate crystal growth. It can be experimentally
determined using Arrhenius-type kinetic modeling
from rate data.

the t90 remained to be about 15 min. In addition, the
recyclability of the 0.2 wt % L-methionine system is also good.

3. GAS HYDRATE REACTORS
The most commonly utilized reactor setup for lab
experimentation is a continuous stirred tank reactor for
hydrate formation. They’re the formal choice for closed
system, small-scale batch processes, due to the simplicity of
operation. Nevertheless, stirrer-tank reactors are not recommended for large-scale hydrate formation, as numerous
specialized challenges may arise during the scale-up,
fabrication, and operation of this type of reactor. The use of
a stirrer for agitating the gas and water phases is the first and
most important issue in operating stirrer-tank reactors.44
Although mixing of gas and liquid phases is necessary for
hydrate formation, veritably fast mixing will help the
accumulation of the hydrate core, which is essential for the
nucleation part. Hence, stirrer-tank reactors suffer from long
induction time.45

The alternate common type is bubble column reactors. Like
stirrer-tank reactors, the dominant phase is liquid water, which
has a high heat capacity. Thus, the heat of hydrate formation
can be removed more efficiently from reactor walls, and a
stable thermal profile can be maintained in this type of reactor.
Likewise, the movement of bubbles in the water phase agitates
the system. Yet, supplying atomic, uniform bubbles in the
entire column cross section and length is not a simple task.
Thus, bubble columns cannot give enough contact area for
continued output of gas hydrate, unless they are equipped with
a stirrer system, which has its own downsides, as mentioned
earlier.44 Still, some hydrate reactors are designed and reported
in the literature based on bubble column technology.83−85
The third common type of gas hydrate reactor is the spray
tower. By scattering water into a continuous gas phase region,
the gas/water interfacial area can readily be increased.
Likewise, as the tiny water drops have a large speed in the
gas phase of the reactor, the necessity of a mechanical stir
system is ruled out for this type of reactor. Consequently, the
design of the reactor is simple, and scaling up can be done
readily.
However, as the heat capacity of the gas phase is small and a
large portion of the spray tower is occupied by the gas phase,
the heat of hydrate formation may not be removed throughout
the reactor walls efficiently. This would easily increase the
reactor temperature. By increasing the temperature, the
system's supersaturation condition, which is essential for
crystallization, would decrease, and this would result in
decreasing the rate of hydrate formation or even stopping
it.44 The simplicity in both fabrication and operation of any
reactor is essential in developing hydrate-based technology,
especially for the separation. Thus, spray towers seem to be the
best option for industrial applications. However, the problem
of heat transfer should be resolved for this purpose. The

This study investigates the micro mechanism of CO2 hydrate formation in seawater with solid promoters, using in situ Raman analysis and
macroscopic experiments. It explores how solid promoters influence the hydrate formation, focusing on the transformation from weak to strong
hydrogen bonds and the role of hydroxyl groups in enhancing this process.
The study explores the kinetics and mechanisms of CO2 capture using semiclathrate hydrates with TBAB as a promoter. It emphasizes enhanced
capture efficiencies and the influence of TBAB on the structural transitions of hydrates, which are crucial for improving CO2 sequestration in
industrial applications.
The study evaluates the kinetics of hydrate formation for methane and carbon dioxide gas mixtures in the presence of SDS. It found that SDS
enhances the rate of hydrate formation significantly, offering insights into effective strategies for gas separation and storage.

The paper explores the kinetics of hydrate formation and dissociation for CO2 and CH4 in the presence of THF under varying conditions. It
demonstrates enhanced formation rates and improved dissociation control, highlighting potential applications in carbon capture and storage (CCS)
technologies.
The study introduces a novel phase change heat removal method using ice granules and normal alkanes to enhance the CO2 capture through hydrate
formation. This approach significantly increased the hydrate growth rate and gas uptake, highlighting the potential for improving CO2 capture
efficiency.
The study investigates the impact of amino acids on CO2 hydrate formation and dissociation kinetics in porous media with brine. L-methionine
showed the best results, enhancing CO2 storage capacity and stability compared to other amino acids and the conventional promoter SDS.

This review explores the role of carbon nanotubes as kinetic nanopromoters in gas hydrate formation, particularly for flammable gases like methane
and hydrogen, and for CO2 capture. It discusses the promoting efficiency of both unmodified and chemically modified CNTs, their impact on
hydrate formation rate, and the synergy when combined with other promoters.
The study explores the use of low-dose amino acids as promoters for methane and carbon dioxide hydrate formation. It finds that L-methionine
enhances CO2 hydrate formation while L-phenylalanine shows varying effects based on gas composition. The study highlights the potential of amino
acids to improve hydrate formation kinetics and storage capabilities in gas capture technologies.

The study examines the effect of R-134a and SDS on CO2 hydrate formation under static conditions. It demonstrates that the combination of these
additives decreases the induction time for hydrate formation, enhances the hydrate growth rate, and improves the water conversion into hydrate,
thus potentially increasing CO2 capture efficiency.

The study investigates the kinetics of CO2 hydrate formation and storage efficiency in different porous media. The results show that smaller pore sizes
significantly enhance both the rate of hydrate formation and the density of CO2 storage, suggesting practical implications for CO2 sequestration
technologies.
This study investigates CO2 hydrate formation using three different promoters under varying pressure conditions to enhance CO2 capture and cold
thermal energy transport. The effectiveness of each promoter is assessed in terms of CO2 capture performance, formation conditions, and hydrate
structure characteristics.

The paper reviews the effects of porous media on the formation of gas hydrates, particularly focusing on the phase equilibrium and hydrate formation
rate. It discusses the influence of porous media properties such as particle size, surface roughness, and hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity on hydrate

simulation time by 90%.145

reservoir simulations.146

11.4. Risk Analysis and Uncertainty Quantification.
Incorporating ML into risk analysis improves the reliability of
hydrate-based storage systems. Chen et al. utilized multivariate
adaptive regression splines (MARS) to develop monitoring
frameworks for CO2 sequestration.151 The model enabled
optimal well placement and early detection of migration
anomalies under uncertain geological scenarios.151
Amar et al. constructed an ensemble of RBFNNs optimized
via bioinspired algorithms to predict CO2 solubility in saline
aquifers.152 Their work achieved high accuracy (R2 > 0.996)
across a range of NaCl concentrations and pressures, providing
essential inputs for hydrate formation simulations in deep
aquifers.152
Rahimi et al. reviewed the role of feature selection in
enhancing model robustness, highlighting that ML models
trained with irrelevant features often misrepresent risk
factors.153 They recommended techniques such as recursive
feature elimination (RFE) and Lasso regularization for
identifying influential geological and operational variables.153

Gu et al. used a deep learning framework to evaluate CO2/

hydrate-promoting surface development.155

designing low-pressure, low-temperature hydrate adsorbents.156

12. CONCLUSIONS
By effectively capturing and storing carbon dioxide (CO2) over
an extended period of time, gas hydrate-based carbon capture
and storage (CCUS) technologies represent a promising
solution to the urgent problem of climate change. Recent
developments in CO2 hydrate formation research, the technoeconomic aspects of CCUS plant operations, and the
incorporation of hydrate-based capture into more comprehensive decarbonization plans have all been methodically
reviewed in this review. Notably, the study also examined the
new models and simulations of CO2 hydrate formation
processes using machine learning (ML) and Artificial
Intelligence (AI) techniques, highlighting their potential to
speed up design optimization and lower experimental costs.
Although hydrate-based CCUS has clear benefits, such as high
selectivity and the potential for energy-efficient operations, a
number of technical, financial, and computational issues must
be resolved before its full potential can be realized. Gas
hydrate-based CCUS, enhanced by data-driven methods, has
great potential to help achieve global carbon reduction goals
with continued innovation, strategic policy support, and
integration into current energy systems.
