1. Introduction
Ammonia (NH3) is a cornerstone of modern society, serving as
an indispensable precursor for nitrogen fertilizers and
emerging as a highly promising zero-carbon fuel for energy
storage. However, synthesizing NH3 by breaking the highly inert
N^N triple bond remains one of the most energy-intensive
challenges in chemical manufacturing. For over a century,
global production has been dominated by the Haber–Bosch
process, which catalytically combines N2 and H2 under extreme
temperatures (400–650 °C) and pressures (200–400 atm).1
Consequently, this energy-intensive process accounts for
approximately 2% of global energy consumption and 1.8% of
global CO2 emissions, primarily due to the steam-reforming of
methane for H2 production.2 Despite its high energy eﬃciency
at large-scale production, the process still relies on centralized,
continuous operations, presenting a signicant barrier to integrating intermittent renewable energy sources.
In nature, biological N2 xation, mediated by nitrogenase
enzymes in soil microbes, generates ammonia under ambient
conditions, serving as the primary natural nitrogen source for
ecosystems.3 Inspired by nature and driven by the proposed
ammonia economy roadmap,4 electrochemical ammonia

In this review, we provide a comprehensive overview of the

The ability of metallic lithium to chemically reduce N2 was
rst demonstrated by Fichter et al. in 1930,14 who electrodeposited Li from LiBr/LiCl dissolved in ethanol solvent under
1000 bar N2 atmosphere. Despite the extreme conditions, only
10% of FE toward NH3 was achieved due to competing HER
from the excessive reaction between metallic Li0 and the protic
solvent. This early work was largely overlooked for six decades
until Tsuneto et al. revitalized the approach in the 1990s by
replacing ethanol with tetrahydrofuran (THF) – an aprotic
solvent with high N2 solubility – while using only small amount
of ethanol.15,16 This solvent engineering strategy dramatically
suppressed excessive HER, improving FE to ca. 60% under 50
bar N2 with LiClO4 electrolyte.15 Motivated by these prior
studies, in 2017, McEnaney et al. demonstrated multi-step NH3
synthesis via high-temperature molten LiOH electrolysis.17
Thereaer, Lazouski et al. reported a continuous Li-NRR system
where primary steps occurred in the same batch cell to
primarily investigate mechanistic insights.18 At the same time,
Andersen et al. successfully validated the Li-NRR for ammonia
electrosynthesis by proposing a rigorous protocol of nitrogen

The SEI represents an interfacial layer that forms spontaneously when electrolyte components undergo reductive reactions at the lithium-coated electrode.19,20 This dynamic,
permeable layer consists of both organic and inorganic species
and acts as a selective barrier rather than a simple passivation
layer.10,21 In Li-NRR, the presence of nitrogen and a proton
carrier results in a distinct SEI structure and chemistry
compared to those observed in lithium-ion batteries.21–23 An
eﬀective SEI layer for Li-NRR must satisfy competing demands:
facilitating nitrogen and proton transport to the reactive
lithium surface while suppressing parasitic pathways including
hydrogen evolution, unproductive lithium accumulation, and
continuous electrolyte degradation.24,25 Since N2 electroreduction takes place within the SEI layer, this interfacial region
governs the mass transport of key reactants (Li, N2, H+),
heterogeneous charge-transfer kinetics, and N2 activation.
Insights from lithium battery research reveal that while the SEI
is electronically insulating, it conducts lithium ions, creating
conditions where mass transport through the layer, rather than
surface electrochemistry, oen limits overall reaction rates.19
When transport becomes unbalanced – i.e. lithium ion ux
exceeds nitrogen supply – an accumulation of detrimental
byproducts such as bulk lithium deposits or lithium hydride
leads to a deterioration of Li-NRR performance.18,25

2.1.1 Lithium electrolytes. Critically, the chemical nature
of the lithium electrolyte directly determines SEI composition
and thus performance through its impact on ionic conductivity,
electrochemical stability, and nitrogen solubility. Understanding this intricate relationship has driven the evolution
from early LiClO4 systems toward high-performance electrolytes. Early Li-NRR research predominantly employed LiClO4
due to its commercial availability and decent solubility in
organic solvents.9,25,26 However, systematic investigation by

concentration was higher than 0.6 M, attributed to reduced
nitrogen solubility and diﬀusivity, shiing the system to

by chlorinated species (LiClOn, LiCl) and Li2CO3, which
provided insuﬃcient selectivity and stability.

Beyond traditional LiClO4 systems, Li et al. signicantly
advanced the Li-NRR system by utilising highly porous copper
electrodes and a LiBF4-based electrolyte to achieve record-high
current densities.28 By using electrodes fabricated via the
hydrogen bubble template method to increase the electrochemical surface area by 300 times, they achieved an unprecedented ammonia production rate of 2.5 ± 0.1 mmol s−1 cm−2 at
a current density of −1.0 A cm−2 with a 71% FE. Furthermore,
the system exhibited exceptional selectivity at a current density
of −0.1 A cm−2, reaching a FE of 95% ± 3%. They suggested that
LiBF4 induces the formation of a compact, uniform, and LiFenriched SEI, which stabilizes lithium plating and eﬀectively
suppresses parasitic electrolyte decomposition. Operando
grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering by Deissler et al.
conrmed that LiBF4 electrolytes form SEI layers with high
concentrations of crystalline LiF along with lithium ethoxide,
contrasting sharply with the chlorinated species formed by
LiClO4.29 This LiF-rich SEI composition originates from
decomposition of BF4− anion at reducing potentials, forming
a dense, ionically conductive but electronically insulating layer
that eﬀectively suppresses undesired side reactions while
maintaining adequate Li+ conductivity.
Despite improvements observed in LiBF4 systems, other
uorinated imide-based lithium electrolytes have proven to
provide enhanced Li-NRR performance. Du et al. achieved
unprecedented performance metrics of almost 100% FE and
stabilized NH3 YR of 150 nmol s−1 cm−2, with stable operation
over 4 days, using high-concentration LiTFSI electrolyte in THF
at 15 bar N2.11 Post-electrolysis analysis revealed that the highconcentration LiTFSI electrolyte enables the formation of
a compact SEI comprising LiF, Li3N and organic components.
This dense ionic layer at the electrolyte–electrode interface
suppresses reductive degradation of electrolyte and solvent
while facilitating the transfer of Li+, N2, and proton carriers.
This work suggests that a thin, nanometer-scale SEI layer could
provide suﬃcient ionic transport, while a bulk interface containing LiF, polysuldes, and Li3N formed in the region of the
electrolyte–electrode–gas phase boundary resulted in unproductive charge consumption, thereby reducing NH3 FE and YR.
Among lithium electrolytes examined in batch cells, LiTFSI
electrolyte displays exceptional performance over other traditional lithium electrolytes such as LiPF6,11,28 LiOTf, LiFSI,11 and
LiBF4,18,30 demonstrating that electrolyte selection is not only
about increased ionic conductivity, but fundamentally about
engineering SEI formation.
2.1.2 Proton carrier. In Li-NRR systems, the proton carrier
supplies protons to lithium nitride (Li3N), promoting the
chemical conversion to ammonia. An ideal proton carrier must
deliver protons eﬃciently while maintaining electrochemical
stability at the high anodic potentials. Following the pioneering
work by Tsuneto et al., ethanol (C2H5OH) has been widely used
in most early Li-NRR studies.16
Early Li-mediated NRR studies generally assumed that an
adequate ethanol concentration is required to protonate Li3N to
release NH3, while acknowledging that ethanol can also parasitically react with plated Li0 to form lithium ethoxide and
evolve H2.26 Rather than acting solely as a reactant in Li3N

both of which decrease the overall FE.

cathodic polarisation beyond the Li0/+ potentials. Historically,
THF has been the most prevalent choice, enabling many of the
highest-performing benchmark systems.11,28,38 However, THF
suﬀers from two critical limitations: a relatively low boiling
point (66 °C), which leads to unproductive solvent loss into the
gas phase, and a low dielectric constant (7.5 at 25 °C), limiting
the bulk ionic conductivity. Identifying alternative solvents that
improve upon these metrics while maintaining the requisite
electrochemical stability has proven challenging. To date, chain
ethers such as diethylene glycol dimethyl ether (diglyme; DG)
and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) represent the most successful
advancements.13,39,40 While their dielectric constants are
comparable to THF, chain ethers exhibit signicantly lower
volatility and, crucially, superior resistance to polymerization
induced by solvent oxidation—a major degradation pathway
that limits the longevity of THF-based electrolytes in continuous
operation.
Beyond serving simply as a passive reaction medium, the
physicochemical properties of the solvent fundamentally
dictate mass-transport dynamics, interfacial chemistry, and
overall performance and energy eﬃciency in Li-NRR.13,39,40 In
particular, solvent polarity aﬀects the solubility and diﬀusivity
of dissolved N2, with chain ethers like diglyme (DG) demonstrating superior N2 carrying capacity compared to cyclic ethers
like THF.13 Furthermore, the solvent directly determines the Li+
solvation structure. Molecular dynamics simulations have
revealed that chain ethers promote anion-rich primary solvation
shells, which preferentially undergo reduction to form a robust,
inorganic-rich SEI, thereby suppressing excessive solvent

2.1.4 Injection of additives. While the bulk solvent and
lithium salt establish the primary matrix of the electrolyte,
several research groups have explored whether SEI composition
could be further tuned through trace electrolyte additives while
maintaining the same traditional electrolyte system. Li et al.
found that small amounts of oxygen (0.8 mol% O2) added to
LiClO4/THF electrolyte under 20 bar N2, previously believed to
induce a detrimental eﬀect, actually improved FE to approximately 78 ± 1%.10 This counterintuitive improvement occurs
because O2 molecules activate SEI modication, shiing its
composition to inorganic-rich interfaces containing Li2O and
LiOH (Fig. 3). This inorganic-dominant layer possesses superior
Li+ conductivity and improved mechanical stability, as
compared to the organic-rich interface, thus protecting the
cathode from side reactions. Subsequent investigations
employing gas chromatography-mass spectrometry and nuclear
magnetic resonance spectroscopy indicated that low O2
concentration hindered electrolyte degradation pathways,
including poly(tetrahydrofuran) formation, thereby improving
the FE of N2 towards NH3.10,43
Spry et al. demonstrated that the controlled introduction of
a small concentration of water (36 mM) into LiClO4/THF solution leads to enhanced NH3 FE, contrasting the general belief
that the presence of water is purely detrimental to Li-NRR
performance (Fig. 3).44 With optimal water concentration, the
highest FE of 27.9 ± 2.5% was obtained at ambient pressure
without a gas diﬀusion electrode (GDE). A transformed SEI
composition from chlorinated-based species (LiCl, LiClOn) to
Li2O-dominant regime, measured by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), resulted in the enhanced performance because
this interface with low lithium diﬀusivity suppresses excessive
lithium plating while allowing favourable transport of N2 and
protons at eﬃcient rates.44 This work emphasises the extreme
sensitivity of the Li-NRR system, in which diﬀerent experimental parameters result in improved performances through
minor variations in electrolyte composition.
Jeon et al. demonstrated that modifying the copper cathode
with trace amounts of lithiophilic silver (Ag) signicantly
enhances ammonia FE.45 With in situ Ag electrodeposition in
a LiTFSI-based electrolyte, this system delivered an NH3 yield
rate of 163 nmol s−1 cm−2 at a high FE of ca. 85%. This study
suggests that Ag facilitates the formation of Li–Ag alloy and
a LiF-dominant SEI, which helps reduce overpotential for
lithium plating. Importantly, this improvement is dependent
on the presence of LiF in the electrolyte, as the addition of
AgOTf exhibited unchanged performance in LiClO4-based
system.

2.2. Operational parameters: controlling driving force and
mass-transport
2.2.1 Nitrogen pressure eﬀects. Beyond the critical role of
proton carriers, nitrogen availability represents an equally
important factor governing Li-NRR performance. The solubility
of N2 in non-aqueous electrolytes is inherently pressuredependent, and numerous studies have revealed substantial
performance improvements under elevated N2 pressures.
Specically, Tsuneto et al. observed increased performance with
increasing pressure up to 50 bar N2.15 A similar eﬀect was also
experienced by Lazouski et al.18 with linear increase of FE when
enhancing N2 from 0 to 1 bar, driven by enhanced N2 solubility.
A kinetic model developed by Andersen et al. to correlate the
mechanism of Li-NRR and relevant performance proposes two
possible mass-transfer limitations: either nitrogen, or proton
transfer.25 Based on computational data, they observed that low
pressure (1 bar) experiments proceed in the nitrogen-limited
regime, while high pressure (up to 20 bar) operate in or near
a proton-limited region. This regime transition explains why the
sharp FE increase observed between 1 and 10 bar was not
experienced when the pressure elevated to 20 bar, as the electrolyte reached nitrogen-saturated plateau. Therefore, various
studies were also conducted 15–20 bar condition to overcome
mass-transport limitation and achieve the highest
performances.5,11,25,34,48

2.2.2 Mass-transport eﬀects. Beyond optimizing the static
properties of the electrolyte, dynamic regulation of the hydrodynamic boundary layer—through forced convection and
viscosity tuning—represents a critical but underexplored aspect
in Li-NRR. While proton and nitrogen supply are optimized in
Li-NRR systems,18,25 the diﬀusion of nitrogen and protons to the
Li-deposited cathodic surface can become rate-limiting steps in
Li-NRR. This point is particularly relevant for phosphoniumbased shuttles, where high viscosity and molecular size introduce a mass-transport limitation that cannot be inferred from
interfacial chemistry alone.
Despite their stability advantages, the high viscosity associated with the [P6,6,6,14][NTf2] salt raised the question of how LiNRR performance changes when the phosphonium cation
structure is varied. Following the emergence of highconcentration LiTFSI electrolytes capable of delivering nearly
quantitative faradaic eﬃciency with ethanol, substituting
ethanol with the more robust [P6,6,6,14]+ in LiTFSI led to
a marked performance decrease, consistent with a hypothesis of
insuﬃciently rapid transport of the bulky proton shuttle and
therefore slower eﬀective proton delivery. This motivated
a systematic study of phosphonium cations with shorter alkyl
chains together with re-optimization of LiTFSI concentration.
Counterintuitively, Nguyen et al. found that the bulkiest
[P6,6,6,14]+ cation still delivered the highest FE and YR under
forced convection, while Li-NRR performance metrics
decreased as the phosphonium cation size decreased.49 Postelectrolysis characterizations revealed that short-alkyl-chain
proton shuttles induced the formation of organic-rich SEI
layers, whereas bulky phosphonium cations favored the deposition of inorganic Li-based species (LiF, Li3N, polysulphides),
which suppressed unproductive Li+ electroreduction and electrode passivation. Under the optimized condition of 1.5 M
LiTFSI + 0.1 M [P6,6,6,14]+, a long-term operation with FE of ca.
78% was maintained for 72 h. Notably, this study also demonstrated the profound sensitivity of the Li-NRR to uid dynamics.
They emphasise that the eﬀects of changing the length of alkyl
chains in the phosphonium cations are not limited to altering
the mass-transport characteristics of the proton shuttle, but
also inuence the kinetics of the Li-NRR through modications
to the composition of the solid electrolyte interphase. Therefore, controlling mass transport in Li-NRR extends beyond just
chemical optimization, requiring precise control over uid
dynamics to decouple the kinetics of key processes.
2.2.3 Potential eﬀects. The applied potential serves as
a critical parameter in determining the chemical composition
and structural morphology of the SEI in Li-NRR.50–53 Izelaar et al.
systematically identied three distinct potential regimes using
a reliable LixFePO4 reference electrode to correlate specic SEI
properties and corresponding FE.51 In the low potential regime
(around −3.2 V vs. SHE), the SEI is thin, porous, and predominantly organic, consisting largely of lithium ethoxide. This
organic-rich interface is particularly ineﬀective at regulating the
transport of Li+ and H+, leading to high rates of HER and low
faradaic eﬃciency. Their nding aligns with previous works,
which indicated that a passivated SEI layer composed of

induces a poor FE towards NH3.10,11,28,49
As the potential shis to more negative values (from −3.7 V
down to −4.6 V vs. SHE), the SEI transitions to a thicker, denser
morphology enriched in inorganic species, particularly LiF
originating from LiTFSI anion decomposition. A signicant
overpotential is required to overcome the kinetic barrier for
uorine-based salt decomposition, explaining why LiF formation is not observed at low overpotentials. Post-mortem XPS
analysis by Izelaar et al. revealed a strong correlation between
the LiF concentration on the SEI and the resulting FE directly
supporting the mechanistic hypothesis advanced by Chorkendorﬀ and coworkers via rst-principles simulations.28,51 Their
calculations suggest that Li+ transport rate through LiF is
signicantly lower than through Li2CO3, while the N2 and H+

residence time of freshly deposited Li0 and thereby facilitating
N2 dissociation before further electron consumption occurs.
Notably, operando Raman spectroscopy has further conrmed
that the SEI is not static but dynamically restructures during LiNRR, with ethanol-driven formation of LiOEt and Li3N playing
an important role in enabling N2 transport and activation at the
electrode surface.50
At extreme potentials exceeding −4.0 V, they found that
while ammonia FE reaches a maximum, the system suﬀers from
signicant current instabilities, which are attributed to
a dynamic cycle of continuous SEI thickening and mechanical
breakdown.51 This dynamic degradation undermines long-term
operational stability and highlights the delicate balance
between driving suﬃcient LiF formation and maintaining
a mechanically coherent interface. To mitigate this issue,
Andersen et al. proposed a potential cycling protocol for Li-NRR
that improved FE and stability of the system to 37 ± 1% and
125 h, respectively.25 The mechanism behind this improvement
was further elucidated by Blair and colleagues using in situ
neutron reectometry. They observed that resting the cell at
open-circuit voltage (OCV) decreases thickness of lithium/SEI
layer, conrming that partial consumption of lithium metals
for either ammonia production or solvent decomposition
occurs during relaxation periods.21
Collectively, these studies establish that applied potential
functions not only as a thermodynamic driving force, but as
a precise tuning parameter for interfacial chemistry, where
achieving high Li-NRR performance metrics requires careful
optimization of applied potential to control SEI properties.

3.2. Coupling Li-NRR with OER
While Li-NRR coupled with HOR process presents an important
interim solution for the distributed green ammonia production,
the most economically and technologically desirable anode
process for ammonia electrosynthesis is the oxidation of water
to protons. Transitioning toward the direct use of water as
a sustainable proton source, Hyung Kim et al. developed
a biphasic electrolyte system that separates an organic solvent
catholyte from an aqueous anolyte.57 From a reactor engineering perspective, this hybrid architecture utilizes the AEM as
a selective interfacial barrier to mediate proton transport while
mitigating the crossover ux of water and ions into the anhydrous Li-NRR environment. Although a cross-linked AEM
signicantly suppressed water permeability compared to cationexchange membranes, enabling Li-NRR at an FE of ca. 60%, it

permeation of water during extended operation triggered the
accumulation of LiOH within the SEI, which passivated the
cathode surface and limited stable operation to 50 hours,
during which the ammonia FE declined progressively. In
another work, Miao et al. utilized polyoxometalates (POMs) as
electron-coupled proton buﬀers to link water electrolysis
directly with Li-NRR.58 POMs serve as proton shuttles that
receive protons from an aqueous anode and deliver them to the
cathode while suppressing the HER. This approach achieved an
ammonia YR of ca. 9 nmol s−1 cm−2 and a FE of 54.2% under

cells are unlikely to become practical due to the unavoidable
and continuous loss of the lithium electrolyte anion to the
aqueous anodic phase and cross-over of water into the organic
cathodic electrolyte solution. These crossover phenomena are
intrinsic to the use of polymer exchange membrane, which
cannot fully block ionic or solvent species driven by concentration and osmotic gradients.
Metal membrane designs represent a more elegant solution
by leveraging the unique property of palladium (Pd) to
completely avoid liquid electrolyte crossover. In contrast to
polymer exchange membranes, Pd membranes exhibit exclusive
selectivity for atomic hydrogen transfer while providing a robust
physical barrier to both organic and aqueous electrolytes.59 This
eﬀectively prevents electrolyte crossover, thereby oﬀering
higher reusability, compared to polymer membranes. Specically, this unique permeation mechanism operates via
a sequence of steps: protons are reduced at the aqueous surface
to form palladium hydride (PdHx), then diﬀuse across the
lattice structure, and are oxidised at the organic-facing Pd
surface to release protons to the organic electrolyte.59,60 Bemana
et al. employed a single Pd foil as both palladium hydride
(PdHx) cathode coupled to aqueous OER on the front side and
Li-NRR cathode coupled to organic solvent oxidation on the
back side.61 Their batch system produced ammonia at a modest
level of 5% FE and 0.6 nmol s−1 cm−2. These modest performance metrics are more likely correlated to mass-transport
limitations in traditional batch cells. Building on this foundation, Stephens and colleagues advanced the PdHx concept to
a two-electrode ow cell where PdHx serves as a passive

This conguration achieved a FE of 36 ± 4% at a current density
of −6 mA cm−2 over 6 h, representing the most advanced
demonstration of Li-NRRjOER coupling in a continuous ow
cell to date. The transition from static batch10,11,18,25,62 to

a specic membrane chemistry is inseparably correlated to the
hydrodynamic conditions of reactors. This reinforces the critical importance of integrated cell design over the sole optimization of membrane materials. Ultimately, leveraging the
selective proton transfer properties of Pd membranes within
a ow-cell conguration establishes a robust strategy for
advanced Li-NRR, providing a viable pathway toward scalable
green ammonia synthesis.

4. Current challenges of Li-NRR
Despite the signicant progress made in optimizing electrolyte
composition and SEI engineering, the Li-NRR process faces
both fundamental and technological challenges that must be
addressed for industrial-scale viability.
4.1. Energy eﬃciency limitation
The most pressing concern is the inherently low energy eﬃciency (EE) of Li-NRR systems (Table 1).13,63,64 Currently reported
EE ranges from 1–17% under realistic operating conditions,
with a theoretical thermodynamic maximum ceiling of only
27.8%—a fundamental limitation set by the electrochemistry of
lithium electrodeposition.13 This poor energy eﬃciency can be
attributed to three factors: (1) the extreme negative potentials
required for lithium electrodeposition (typically −3.2 to −4.6 V
vs. SHE), leading to enormous overpotentials; (2) energy losses
across the dynamic SEI; and (3) additional potential drop
required to drive Li0/+ electroreduction via SEI. In comparison,
the Haber–Bosch process achieves high energy eﬃciency of ca.
60% under optimized conditions. To make Li-NRR economically competitive for distributed ammonia electrosynthesis, the
EE must be improved to above 40%, which requires new
approaches beyond electrolyte optimization.
To circumvent the thermodynamic limitations of lithium,
alternative redox mediators possessing lower negative reduction potentials have been investigated via experimental and
simulation-based approaches.65–67 Computational screening
studies employing DFT have identied key thermodynamic
descriptors for a viable mediator: metal nitride formation
energy, standard reduction potential, stability of surface
nitrogen vacancies, and adequate salt solubility in non-aqueous
electrolytes.67 Based on these criteria, calcium and magnesium
are the most promising candidates among the alkaline earth
metal group, as both are capable of electrodeposition in organic
electrolytes and satisfy the thermodynamic requirements for N2
activation.65,67 While Na and K oﬀer similarly favourable
reduction potentials on paper, both are impractical mediators
due to their extreme reactivity with organic solvents and the
absence of stable electrodeposition conditions in non-aqueous
media.67 Nonetheless, despite their thermodynamic promise,
Ca- and Mg-mediated systems currently lag behind the optimised Li-NRR in terms of ammonia yield rate and long-term
stability, underscoring that the shi to alternative mediators
requires systematic optimization of reaction environments
including electrolyte composition, SEI chemistry, and proton
donor selection.66,68
4.2. Electrolytejsolvent decomposition
Understanding solvent and electrolyte degradation in Li-NRR
requires distinguishing between cathodic and anodic
processes, as each proceeds through diﬀerent chemistry and
inuences the system stability under diﬀerent conditions. At
the cathode, the highly negative potentials required for Li
deposition drive partial reductive decomposition of the lithium
salt anion and, to a lesser extent, THF. However, owing to its

wide cathodic electrochemical stability window, THF itself
undergoes only minimal reductive decomposition under standard Li-NRR operating conditions—its kinetic stability against
cathodic reduction is in fact higher than that of common anions
such as TFSI−. Rather than being purely detrimental, this
controlled partial decomposition of the salt anion and solvent
at the onset of operation is mechanistically benecial via the
SEI layer (see Section 2). At the anode, in contrast, degradation
is far more severe and represents a stability issue in Li-NRR
systems. Specically, THF—the standard solvent due to its
high N2 solubility—undergoes oxidative polymerisation at high
positive anodic potentials (>3 V vs. Li0/+).43,49 This anodic polymerisation generates byproducts that increase electrolyte
viscosity, reduce ionic conductivity, and hinder ion transport.49
The cumulative result is system degradation and performance
loss aer prolonged operation, which is commonly experienced
in high-pressure batch cells.27,34 Recent advances using diglyme
continuous-ow cells have achieved longer operation (300 h).13
The ether backbone of diglyme lacks the strained ring structure
of THF and thus exhibits markedly superior resistance to anodic
ring-opening polymerisation, though it presents challenges
including higher viscosity and cost relative to THF.13
Despite its status as a “redox mediator,” lithium is not
completely recovered aer each reaction cycle. Multiple degradation pathways of lithium ions could occur simultaneously: (1)
side reactions between electrodeposited Li0 and the organic
electrolyte/solvent,31 forming stable lithium compounds (LiH,
Li2O, Li2CO3, etc.) that are not electrochemically regenerated;
(2) precipitation and encapsulation of lithium within thick,
poorly conducting organic-rich SEI layers;31 and (3) mechanical
loss through electrode fracturing and particle detachment
during cell cycling.69 Over extended operation, these cumulative
losses necessitate periodic electrolyte replacement, complicating system design.

4.3. Current density limitations
High current densities up to 1 A cm−2 have been demonstrated in
small-area batch cells (<1 cm2) using high-surface-area porous
electrodes.28 However, even in state-of-the-art continuous-ow
reactors with gas-diﬀusion electrodes (GDE), mass transport of
N2 and proton carriers remains insuﬃcient for N2 electroreduction operated at high current densities.12,26 Achieving the
industrial target of >−300 mA cm−2 current density with largearea electrodes therefore represents a fundamental kinetic challenge. Advanced continuous ow systems still operate at
a current density below −60 mA cm−2 when using large-area
electrodes (25 cm2 geometric area), far below the −300 mA
cm−2 target.12,13,26 This scaling limitation arises from the rate
mismatch between electrochemical Li+ reduction and chemical
N2/proton transport to the freshly deposited lithium surface.
According to Faraday's law, the Li electrodeposition rate is
directly proportional to applied current,18 whereas N2 and proton
transport are governed by convection in bulk solutions and
diﬀusion through the SEI with certain mass-transfer coeﬃcients
that are independent of current density.25 At low current densities
(<10 mA cm−2), the Li0 deposition rate is slow enough that

dissolved N2 molecules can diﬀuse through the SEI and react
with freshly deposited lithium to form Li3N before subsequent
protonation. However, at high current densities (>100 mA cm−2),
lithium is deposited at a much faster rate than those of N2 and
proton diﬀusion. This rate mismatch at high current densities
creates several detrimental eﬀects. First, excessive Li0 that cannot
completely react with N2 owing to insuﬃcient N2 mass transport
instead undergoes unproductive side-reactions with the
electrolyte/solvent molecules, thereby thickening the SEI with
organic decomposition products. Second, the protonation step is
limited by sluggish proton diﬀusion through a thick SEI, despite
prior Li3N formation. Additionally, the rapid Li electrodeposition
at high current densities generates dendritic lithium deposits
that fracture the SEI layer, inducing uctuating current density
and non-selective electron consumption, thereby suppressing the
ammonia FE. Achieving uniform current distribution, SEI
composition, and N2/proton supply simultaneously across largearea electrodes therefore remains an unsolved engineering
challenge for practical Li-NRR scale-up.
4.4. Product separation
Unlike the Haber–Bosch process, which produces gaseous
ammonia at high pressure,1 electrochemical systems struggle to
eﬃciently isolate NH3. In most batch systems, ammonia remains
dissolved in the organic electrolyte, which requires complicated
post-electrolysis distillation or gas-stripping procedures to obtain
ammonia product. Recent breakthroughs using diglyme solvent
and optimized GDE ow reactor achieved ca. 98% ammonia
release to the gas phase, representing a major step forward.
Although GDE ow cells remain feasible for scale-up, these
systems require sophisticated control of experimental parameters to prevent typical electrolyte ooding at the electrode–electrolyte–gas interface.26 Precise balance of gas ow rates and ow
dynamics of electrolytes, electrode wettability through optimal
electrode area, and cell architecture should be optimized to
achieve stable operation in specic systems.

5. Conclusions and future
perspectives
Li-NRR has progressed rapidly in the last ve years, emerging as
a promising route for distributed green ammonia electrosynthesis. This review highlights how electrolyte engineering—
through proton carriers, N2 pressure, lithium salts, additives,
and applied potentials—controls the SEI to deliver unprecedented metrics. These include FE approaching 100% and
continuous operation reaching 300 hours. Furthermore, the
deployment of advanced in situ and ex situ analytical techniques
to characterize the cathodic SEI has profoundly expanded our
fundamental understanding of these dynamic electrochemical
systems.
Despite these remarkable achievements, translating
outstanding lab-scale performances to industrial application and
meeting U.S. Department of Energy targets requires overcoming
signicant engineering hurdles. As discussed, the extreme
negative potentials inevitably drive solvent decomposition, which

Review
coupled with severe kinetic mass-transport mismatches,
currently prevent stable operation at high current densities.
Furthermore, the low energy eﬃciency, prohibitive electrolyte
costs, and the complexities of scaling up ow reactors represent
steep fundamental barriers to practical applications.
To bridge the gap between fundamental research and practical application, future eﬀorts must prioritize cross-disciplinary
solutions. Key research directions for high-performance Li-NRR
should include the screening of more robust, cost-eﬀective
solvent/electrolyte systems, the design of advanced ow cell
capable of delivering suﬃcient mass-transport across large-area
electrodes, and the development of eﬃcient product separation
protocols. Importantly, to address kinetic mismatches in these
systems, future mechanistic studies should transition beyond
traditional batch cells to utilize hydrodynamic electrodes that
provide a precisely-control and uniform mass transport of key
chemical species (viz. Li+, N2, H+) for decoupling kinetics of the
key processes in a Li-NRR system. Moreover, exploring other
alternative mediators with less negative reduction potential
represents a great importance of improving overall energy eﬃciency of the redox-mediated ammonia electrosynthesis. Last but
not least, integrating Li-NRR with OER via proton-selective
membranes remains a critical frontier for achieving truly
sustainable ammonia production.
As global interest in decarbonization drives a growing
demand for sustainable chemical manufacturing, the rapidly
advancing eld of Li-NRR oﬀers ample scope for future breakthroughs. By systematically summarizing the evolution of LiNRR from early proof-of-concept works to state-of-the-art
innovations, we anticipate that this review could provide strategic guidance for the development of robust, scalable devices.
With continued advancements, Li-NRR is expected to reduce
global reliance on the centralized Haber–Bosch process, establishing a strong foundation for decentralized, renewablepowered green ammonia production.
