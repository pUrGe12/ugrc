Sequential Solvent Extraction Reveals Lithofacies-Dependent Pore
Accessibility, Wettability, and Hydrocarbon Partitioning in OrganicRich Lacustrine Shales

Shengyu Yang, Tao Wang, and He Cheng
Cite This: ACS Omega 2026, 11, 49899−49913

ABSTRACT: Understanding the reservoir characteristics of shale
oil formations is an essential workflow in petroleum exploration
and development because it supports petroleum-reserve evaluation
and guides production activities. Few studies have examined the
coupled effects of pore structure and oil composition on reservoir
characterization, which limits the accuracy of reservoir evaluation.
In this work, three representative dolomitic shale and silty shale
samples from the Kongdian Formation, Bohai Bay Basin, were
studied with X-ray diffraction, petrographic microscopy, and μ-Xray fluorescence to determine mineral compositions and
sedimentary textures. In conjunction with sequential solvent
extraction, pore structure, wettability, and oil composition were
characterized using mercury intrusion porosimetry, nitrogen physisorption, contrast variation-small angle neutron scattering, gas
chromatography, and saturated hydrocarbon, aromatic hydrocarbon, resin, and asphaltene fractionation. After solvent extraction of
the as-received core samples, the porosity of the two dolomitic shale samples increased only from 5.36% and 6.34% to 6.46% and
7.28%, in contrast to the dramatic increase from 2.96% to 20.4% for the silty shale. CV−SANS results show a slight increase in wateraccessible pore space after solvent extraction, but water-accessible pores still account for less than 30% of the total pores. Oil
composition gradually changed from light to heavy fractions, and the presence of light oil indicated that oil charging occurred in both
dolomitic shale and silty shale. In addition, a rule-of-thumb extraction duration for pore-structure characterization is proposed as a
reference for future studies.

1. INTRODUCTION
Following the successful commercial production of shale gas in
North America over the first two decades of the 21st century,
shale oil exploration has attracted increasing attention.1,2
Major North American shale oil plays (Eagle Ford, Avalon and
Bone Spring, Bakken, Niobrara, and Monterey/Santos) are
predominantly marine deposits with high spatial continuity and
relatively limited heterogeneity.1,3 In contrast, China’s shale oil
resources are dominantly lacustrine in origin, exemplified by
the Qingshankou Formation (Songliao Basin), Kongdian and
Shahejie Formations (Bohai Bay Basin), Lucaogou Formation
(Junggar Basin), Yanchang Formation (Ordos Basin), and
Funing Formation (Subei Basin).4−6 Intensive tectonic
activities have further segmented these deposits into isolated
sags. Consequently, the combination of restricted lacustrine
depositional areas and structural fragmentation necessitates
more meticulous reservoir evaluation prior to economic
lacustrine shale oil production in China.
Laboratory analyses of core samples constitute a critical step
in reservoir evaluation workflows. Shale gas samples mainly

Soxhlet extraction may not remove all oil from accessible

to the strong adsorption and restricted diffusion of shale oil in

Given the low porosity and extremely low permeability of the
rock matrix, the study of shale oil reservoirs from lacustrine
deposits has become very challenging.19,26−28
In addition to solvent-extraction efficiency, reservoir
characterization studies often overlook the origin of the oil.
Although shales (mudrocks) are commonly regarded as typical
self-sourcing and self-storing rocks, substantial evidence
indicates that the light oils were expelled out of the shale
and charged into adjacent layers, while heavy oils tend to be
retained in the shale matrix.29−31 Consequently, some of the
shale layers may contain oils charged from neighboring
layers.32 Therefore, tracing the origins of oil can help to
predict and locate the geological and engineering sweet spots
of interlayered siliceous bands or shales that contain light
hydrocarbons, which have good porosity, permeability, and oil
mobility.

Currently, coupled research on pore structure and oil
composition during solvent extraction remains limited, with
even fewer studies using sequential solvent extraction.23,33−37
Therefore, this comprehensive work integrates petrophysical
and geochemical analyses to address this gap in the Kongdian
Shale of the eastern Bohai Bay Basin and proposes a welljustified workflow. Basic sample information, including mineral
composition, organic richness, thermal maturity, and sedimentary texture, was first determined using X-ray diffraction,
total organic carbon, pyrolysis, thin section observation, and μXRF scanning. Changes in pore structure during sequential
solvent extraction were directly measured using mercury
intrusion porosimetry (MIP) and nitrogen physisorption
(NP). The wettability of pore spaces was evaluated using
contrast variation-small angle neutron scattering (CV−SANS).
Changes in oil composition were measured using gas
chromatography (GC) and saturated hydrocarbon, aromatic
hydrocarbon, resin, and asphaltene (SARA) fractionation. The
results improve our understanding of how solvent extraction
modifies pore structure and oil composition, thereby
improving the accuracy of shale-oil reservoir evaluation.

polished with sandpaper for μ-XRF measurements using a Bruker M4

In addition to the sequential solvent extraction, two supplementary
extraction designs were conducted to evaluate the hydrocarbons
remaining after the 60 day conventional extraction. In the first design,
approximately 10 g of the residual solid samples after 60 days of
extraction were collected and ball-milled to <300 mesh. The
pulverized samples were then subjected to an additional 14 day
conventional solvent extraction using the same DCM/MeOH
mixture, and the oil obtained from this step was named D60-Powder.
Subsequently, on day 200 after the initial extraction, the extracted
pulverized residues were further treated by accelerated solvent
extraction (ASE) using a Thermo Fisher ASE 350 system at 130 °C
and 1500 psi for 30 min, and the oil collected from this step was
named D200-Powder-ASE. In the second design, the residual 20−35
mesh samples after the 60 day sequential extraction were directly
subjected to ASE on day 200 after the initial extraction without
further pulverization. The ASE conditions were the same as those
used for the pulverized samples, and the extracted oil was named
D200-ASE.

2.2. Solvent Extraction
In this study, several solvent extraction methods were used for
different experiments. Cubic samples were soaked in a beaker
containing a dichloromethane/methanol solvent mixture at a volume
ratio of 93:7. The DCM/MeOH mixture of 93:7 (v/v) was selected as
a conventional and reproducible solvent system that can extract both
nonpolar to moderately polar hydrocarbons and part of the polar
compounds associated with mineral and organic surfaces.39−41 The
beaker was placed in a water bath at 40 °C for 14 days to remove the
oil in the samples as a batch. The mixed fluids were changed every 2−
3 days to ensure a high solvent extraction efficiency.
A total of 50 g of 20−35 mesh particles of each sample was
prepared for sequential solvent extraction (Figure 2). The particle
samples were placed in a cellulose cylinder filter and extracted with a
mixed fluid of dichloromethane and methanol in a volume ratio of
93:7. The extraction durations were set at 1, 2, 4, 8, 15, 30, and 60
days, the residual solid samples were retained for MIP, NP, and CV−
SANS experiments, and the extracted oils were collected for gas
chromatography and SARA fractionation analyses. For clarity, the
particle samples and extracted oils were named D0 (short for day 0)
for the raw sample and D1, D2, D4, D8, D15, D30, and D60 for
sequentially solvent-extracted samples at different extraction days.

2.4. Contrast Variation-small Angle Neutron Scattering
The remaining 20−35 mesh samples of D0, D1, and D200-ASE were
selected to perform CV-SANS using the Very Small Angle Neutron
Scattering (VSANS) instrument BL-14 at the China Spallation
Neutron Source (CSNS).43,44 The samples were filled into 1 mmthick quartz cuvettes to measure the total pore space of the dry
sample with the Q vector range of 0.00017− 0.49 Å−1. After dry
sample scanning, mixtures of water and deuterated water were
injected into the cuvettes and left to stand for 12 h. Then, the fluidsaturated samples were measured again to determine the wateraccessible pore space. The detailed steps and principles of CV-SANS
followed Zhang et al.46

3.2. Extraction-Induced Pore Structure Evolution

To analyze the pore structure of shale oil reservoirs, solvent
extraction is required to determine the total accessible pores.
In this study, raw and solvent-extracted cubic samples were
measured with MIP to determine the porosity. As shown in
Table 1, X1 and Y2 samples show porosity increases from
5.36% to 6.46% and from 6.34% to 7.28%, respectively.
However, the porosity of Y1 increases from 2.96% to 20.4%,
indicating that a large amount of oil was stored in the pore
space. To further investigate how pore structure changed after
solvent extraction at different stages, MIP and NP analyses
were conducted on D0−D60 samples from a sequential solvent
extraction. Overall, the MIP curves of D0−D60 show that the
pore space increases with increasing solvent-extraction
duration, and the largest pore-space change occurs between
D0 and D1 extraction stages (Figure 3). X1 and Y2 samples
show that the incremental volumes of pore space mainly occur
at the pore-throat diameter less than 40 and 100 nm, which
indicates that oil is stored in the nm-sized pores, which are
controlled by the pore throat less than 40 and 100 nm. Y1,
however, differs substantially from the other two samples. It
shows a small amount of mercury intrusions in the nm-sized
pore throats, but more mercury intrusions in μm-sized pores
throats. After solvent extraction, the pores controlled by pore
throats between 10 nm and 6 μm are dramatically increased
and show a typical MIP curve of clayey siltstone or sandstone.
Since the porosity increased to 20.4% after solvent extraction
in cubic samples (Table 1), the Y1 sample can be classified as
silty shale.
Because MIP is less accurate than NP for nanometer-scale
pores, NP was used to refine the resolution of pore structure
characterization below 300 nm. The X1 and Y2 samples show
substantial pore-space increases between the D0-D1 extraction
stage, followed by gradual increases between D2-D60 to a
similar level from 2 to 300 nm (Figure 4). The Y1 sample
shows a very small pore volume below 10 nm, and a large
number of pores occur at pore diameters larger than 100 nm.
The pores occupied by oil are mainly extracted on the first day
and show only slight increases in the rest of the extraction days.

2.5. Gas Chromatography
The solvent-extracted oils of D1-D30, D60-Powder, D200-PowderASE, and D200-ASE were measured using an Agilent 8890 gas
chromatograph to determine the composition of normal alkanes.
Hydrocarbon fractions were determined from the peak retention
times and peak areas.

2.6. SARA Fractionation
The available solvent-extracted oils were used to quantify the
saturated hydrocarbon, aromatic hydrocarbon, resin, and asphaltene
(SARA) fractions. Detailed steps followed Cheng et al.45 In this study,
the extracted oil of D1 and D60-Powder was directly analyzed because
of the limited amount of oil recovered from the later extraction stages;
the oils collected from D2, D4, D8, D15, and D30 were combined to
obtain sufficient material for SARA fractionation. This combined
sample was used only to represent the overall composition of the
intermediate-to-late conventional extraction products rather than the
SARA composition of each individual extraction stage.

3. RESULTS
3.1. Mineralogy and Lithofacies

The TOC contents of the three shale samples were 3.01%,
9.36%, and 2.82% (Table 1). According to the pyrolysis results,
X1, Y1, and Y2 have low S1 values of 0.87, 1.56, and 2.56 mg/g
but relatively high S2 of 17.44, 72.77, and 17.50 mg/g,
respectively. The Tmax data suggest that these three shales are
in the oil generation zone. The X-ray diffraction results
indicate that the X1 and Y2 samples are enriched in carbonate
minerals, with dolomite accounting for 55.9% and 61.0%,
followed by 1.2% and 3.9% of calcite, respectively (Table 2). In
addition, X1 and Y2 contain 17.9% and 25.7% siliceous
minerals as well as 12.7% and 6.2% clay minerals. In contrast to
these dolomitic shales, the Y1 sample is enriched in siliceous
minerals instead, which accounts for 41.6%, followed by the
carbonate minerals and clay minerals of 27.0% and 11.6%.

4. DISCUSSION
4.1. Extraction Efficiency and Pore Characterization

In the past two decades, studies on shale gas samples were
usually conducted directly on as-received samples because the
fluids within the pore space are gas and water.47,48 In shale oil
research, solvent extraction is required to remove residual oil
and obtain reliable information on accessible pore structure.49,50 Current pore structure characterization lacks standardized protocols for solvent extraction duration, and the
progression of solvent extraction-induced pore structural
changes, whether gradual or abrupt, remains unresolved. To
address this, this study employed complementary approaches
of MIP, NP, and CV-SANS to track pore evolution across
multiple sequential extraction stages. Due to the different
mechanisms of these three methods, the results generated are
used to interpret different aspects of pore structure (Figure 8).

According to the mechanisms, MIP tests overcome capillary
pressures to derive pore-throat distribution over a wide
nanometer-to-micrometer range, although its limited resolution impedes detection of subtle nm-scale changes during
sequential solvent extraction at the nanometer scale.51−53 NP
measures pore structure based on three stages of gas filling
processes, which are single-layer adsorption, multilayer
adsorption, and capillary condensation. Theoretically, it cannot
resolve large pore spaces in the micrometer range, but it has
the advantage of the measuring probe being a gas phase, which
is much more sensitive than MIP in nm-range pore structure
characterization.54,55
The porosities of the X1 and Y2 cubic samples before and
after solvent extraction are similar, showing a typical shale
porosity (Table 1). The Y1 sample shows a very low porosity

of 2.96% before solvent extraction and dramatically increases
to 20.4%. The 20.4% porosity and pore-throat distribution in
the 100 nm to 1 μm range indicate it is not a typical porosity
for shale but a typical porosity for siltstone and sandstone,
which agrees with previous MIP results.56−58 According to the
XRD results and thin-section observations, the Y1 is rich in
siliceous minerals, and the grain size ranges from very fine sand
to silt/clay. Multiple lines of evidence indicate that the Y1
sample is a silty shale reservoir containing a large amount of
oil, rather than a typical shale, which acts as both reservoir and
source rock. The MIP and NP results indicate that, for the
studied 20−35 mesh Kongdian shale samples under the DCM/
MeOH extraction conditions used here, the major porestructure changes in solvent-accessible pores occurred within
the first 1−2 days of extraction (Figures 3,4). Therefore, a 2

4.3. Lithofacies-Dependent Hydrocarbon Partitioning

Although biomarker, isotopic, and molecular-maturity data are

from the same formation.74,75 Therefore, Y2 is compositionally

contain a larger proportion of relatively light hydrocarbons
during the earliest extraction stage.

5. CONCLUSIONS
This study investigated the coupled evolution of the pore
structure, wettability, and oil composition during sequential
solvent extraction of three representative lacustrine shale
samples from the Kongdian Formation. The results show that
solvent extraction can significantly modify the measured pore
system by removing hydrocarbons from accessible pores, but
the magnitude of this effect is strongly lithofacies-dependent.
The two dolomitic shale samples showed only limited porosity
increases after extraction, whereas the silty shale sample
showed a much larger increase, indicating that lithofacies, pore
geometry, and connectivity are important controls of
extraction efficiency and measured reservoir properties.
Sequential extraction results indicate that, for the studied
Kongdian shale samples under the tested DCM/MeOH
extraction conditions, the most significant pore-structure
changes occurred during the early extraction stages. A 2 day
extraction may be used for rapid preliminary screening of
similar samples, whereas a 7 day extraction provides a more
conservative pretreatment. However, these durations should be
regarded as sample-specific guidelines, rather than broadly
applicable protocols. The additional oil recovered after
pulverization and ASE treatment demonstrates that conventional extraction does not completely remove all retained
hydrocarbons. Residual oil may remain as bound, adsorbed,
heavy, or poorly accessible hydrocarbons in low-connectivity
pores or on mineral and organic matter surfaces. The CVSANS results further show that water-accessible pore space
increased after extraction but remained relatively low even after
enhanced extraction, indicating that residual oil films, hydrophobic organic matter, or poorly connected pore domains may
continue to limit water accessibility.
These findings highlight the importance of integrating pore
structure characterization with oil composition analysis when
evaluating shale-oil reservoirs. GC and SARA results show that
relatively mobile light hydrocarbons were preferentially
extracted during the early stages, whereas heavier fractions
became more important during later or enhanced extraction.
Nevertheless, the conclusions of this study are based on a
limited number of representative samples, and the observed
trends should not be generalized to all shale oil systems
without further verification. Future work should include larger
sample sets from different lithofacies and basins and systematically evaluate the effects of solvent type, extraction duration,
