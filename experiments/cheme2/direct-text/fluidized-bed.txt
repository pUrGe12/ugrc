Carbon-Free Ammonia Co-firing in Fluidized Bed Combustion:
Analysis of Advanced Chemical Modeling and Detailed Mechanism

Cite This: ACS Omega 2025, 10, 32712−32727

ABSTRACT: Current proposals to achieve carbon reduction through the co-firing of
ammonia and coal in fluidized bed boilers necessitate further research into the
combustion characteristics of ammonia under fluidized bed conditions. Ammonia cofiring has primarily been explored using either detailed combustion mechanisms in
pulverized coal boilers or coal combustion reactions in fluidized beds. In this study, we
performed experiments and detailed mechanism simulations of coal and ammonia cofiring in a bubbling fluidized bed (BFB) combustion apparatus (operating
temperature: approximately 1123 K) and conducted experiments at various primary
air (PA) and secondary air (SA) ratios. We constructed a 1-D simulation reactor,
mimicking a fluidized bed reactor, based on an equivalent reactor network. We also
used three chemical reaction models, the Okafor, Nakamura, and modified
mechanisms, to simulate NH3 combustion reactions in the BFB. Under variable PA
and NH3 ratios, NO emissions in both models were primarily controlled by NH2, O,
and HNO radical reactions. Reaction pathway analysis revealed that both mechanisms exhibited similar characteristics for NO
formation and reduction. These results indicate that during the initial NH3 oxidation reaction, HNO production is governed by NH2
and O radicals, as illustrated by reactions, such as NH2 + O = HNO + H and NH2 + NH2 = N2H2 + H2, which affect NO formation.
Thus, the advanced model for NH2 and HNO reactions can better simulate ammonia oxidation at lower temperatures. This
enhancement aids in bridging the gap between experimental results and simulations, particularly under fluidized bed conditions,
providing important theoretical support for planned ammonia/coal co-firing in fluidized bed boilers.

1. INTRODUCTION
The increasing recognition of global environmental crises has
led to the implementation of proactive, environmentally friendly
policies in the energy sector. In power generation, various
measures are being explored to reduce greenhouse gas
emissions, such as CO2 and N2O emissions, with a strong
emphasis on achieving carbon-free power generation. In this
global context, research is being actively conducted to achieve
net-zero emissions and reduce or neutralize carbon.1,2 Notable
initiatives include the European Green Deal (2021), which
established rigorous standards for air pollutant emissions from
internal combustion engine vehicles, and the 2020 directive of
the International Maritime Organization, which introduced
stringent emission standards for marine operations.3 Converting
fossil fuels to carbon-neutral or carbon-free fuels in thermal
power plants is a key strategy for achieving net-zero targets and
can be expedited by using existing facilities. In this regard, coal
co-firing with ammonia is recognized as a rapid and effective
approach for reducing CO2 emissions, with research being
conducted in countries, such as Japan, China, the United
Kingdom, Spain, and others.4−8
In the Republic of South Korea, the government has
announced policies to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by
approximately 290 million tons (40%) from baseline levels by

2030. These policies include expanding renewable energy
generation and introducing low-carbon fuels, such as hydrogen
and ammonia, for combustion.9 In line with the recent trend
toward achieving carbon neutrality in the energy sector, the
South Korean government is actively implementing measures,
such as adopting carbon-free power sources, enhancing energy
efficiency, and promoting electrification. These initiatives aim to
ensure a sustainable and environmentally friendly power
generation sector. Specifically, a target has been established to
increase the use of hydrogen and ammonia for energy from 2.1%
in 2030 to 7.1% in 2036.9 Additionally, an ongoing government
project in thermal power generation aims to achieve carbon
reduction through 20% ammonia co-firing in coal-fired power
plants, thereby leveraging this important carbon-free fuel source.

Fluidized bed power plants typically operate at temperatures
ranging from approximately 1123 to 1173 K, which is lower than
the temperatures of pulverized coal-fired boilers.12−14 Thus,
investigating the combustion reaction of ammonia under these
low-temperature conditions is crucial. Moreover, given current
plans to implement ammonia co-firing in fluidized bed boilers,
evaluating the detailed combustion mechanism during ammonia
co-firing with coal under these conditions is essential (1100 K).
Fluidized bed simulations have been investigated using both
3-D simulations, such as computational fluid dynamics (CFD)
and computational particle fluid dynamics (CPFD), and 1-D
simulation approaches. The use of CFD and CPFD in
simulations provides deeper insights into coal combustion
processes,15−17 predicting velocity distributions, hydrodynamic
behaviors, such as bubble size, species concentrations, and other
hydrodynamic parameters within a BFB.18−20 However, the
chemical kinetic mechanisms involved in CFD and CPFD
simulations require significant computational resources,21 and,
the flamelet model could therefore also be considered as an
alternative. Recently, with the widespread availability of massive
parallel computing, carrier-phase direct numerical simulations
(CP-DNS) have become feasible and been applied to pulverized
coal combustion.22 In addition, the use of global reactions in
CFD simulations to minimize computational costs is a widely
adopted practice.15−20 Furthermore, 1-D models can be used to
simulate the gasification reactions of coal and biomass in
fluidized beds, helping to reduce resource consumption.23−28
Some studies have used tools, such as Aspen Plus, which
incorporate hydrodynamics based on two-phase theory in 1-D
simulations.29−31 Notably, other studies have employed a
chemical equivalent reactor network (ERN) method as a tool
for simulating fluidized beds in 1-D.32−34 In this approach,
fluidized bed reactors with complex flow behavior are
represented by using ideal reactor units interconnected in a
network. Regions with strong mixing are modeled as a perfectly
stirred reactor (PSR), while straight flows without back-mixing
are represented by plug flow reactors (PFR). In previous
research on the fluidized bed gasification of solid fuels, including
coal and biomass,32−37 fluidized bed reactors were simulated
using hydrodynamics through PSR and PFR reactors, similar to
the approach used in this study. However, these studies
primarily focused on the reactions of solid fuels and did not
specifically investigate ammonia combustion.
Ammonia gas combustion has been widely investigated.
Among the studies on detailed ammonia chemistry, the Okafor
model38 accurately represents the current methane (hydrocarbon-based fuel) and ammonia co-firing characteristics,
providing precise simulations that have been verified against
other models and experimental data under gas-burner
conditions. However, this model assumes a combustion
temperature of approximately 2000 K, which differs significantly
from the conditions in a fluidized bed furnace. Therefore,
Nakamura et al.39 proposed enhancing the oxidation reaction by
utilizing the NxHy radical at a lower temperature (1400 K).
Their detailed chemical model for ammonia, which improved
the Konnov model,40 successfully simulated ammonia combustion at relatively low temperatures. Nevertheless, this temperature remains higher than the combustion temperature in the
fluidized bed region, making the direct application of these
models to fluidized bed ammonia combustion challenging.
Research on the co-firing of ammonia and coal is a recent field
of growing interest, and associated studies are ongoing.41−45

However, research on the co-firing of ammonia with coal using
detailed ammonia chemistry remains limited. Although
Taniguchi et al.,46 along with similar studies,5,47,48 investigated
ammonia combustion through experiments and detailed
mechanisms, these studies were conducted in pulverized coal
boilers at temperatures higher than those typically found in
fluidized beds. Sousa Cardoso et al.49 and Kim et al.50 conducted
experimental studies on coal and ammonia in pilot-scale
fluidized bed reactors; however, these studies lacked detailed
chemical analyses.
Therefore, the aim of this current study was to investigate the
combustion characteristics of NH3 during the co-firing of NH3
and coal in a fluidized bed reactor. Initially, we conducted
experiments using a laboratory-scale bubbling fluidized bed
(BFB) setup. Subsequently, we performed a simulation study
using the Chemkin-Pro program, based on previous research,32−37,51 and NO and N2O emissions during ammonia
combustion were analyzed.52 Aside from the previous study by
Dai et al.,37 no other research has addressed coal combustion in
these fluidized beds. Given that the objective of this study was to
identify the direction and improvement areas for the detailed
ammonia chemistry model in coal co-firing using 1-D
simulations, some errors in the coal combustion simulation
were anticipated. Thus, a simulation study was conducted using
the Chemkin-Pro program with previous work serving as a
reference. Although the Chemkin-Pro program is limited in
accurately simulating hydrodynamic conditions compared to
other 1-D models, our study primarily aimed to explore an
improved method for the detailed chemical reaction mechanism
of ammonia during co-firing with coal under fluidized bed
temperatures and conditions. Previous 1-D studies5,46 simulated
the devolatilization process and char combustion model (coal →
char + CxHy + light gases), assumed to form through the
reaction, and the present study is based on the same
assumptions.46 Gas combustion model used a detailed chemical
model for ammonia combustion,38,39 and sensitivity and
reaction pathway analyses were conducted based on the
previous model at low temperatures (approximately 1100 K).
Based on these results, we enhanced the model and proposed an
advanced model. These simulation results have significant
implications for comprehensively understanding the chemical
reactions of ammonia and predicting ammonia oxidation in a
fluidized bed boiler.

2. METHODS
2.1. Experimental Methods. KIDECO, a commercially
available imported coal from Indonesia, was selected as the coal
sample for this study. This sub-bituminous coal is widely used as
a fuel in power plants, particularly in fluidized bed boilers.
Analysis of fuel physical properties was conducted according to
the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM)
standards. Proximate analysis was performed using a commercial
industrial analyzer (TGA701; LECO Co., St. Joseph, MI)
following the ASTM D3172 standards. Ultimate analysis was
carried out using an elemental analyzer (6850 GC/5975 MSD;
Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) following the ASTM
D5373-16 standards. The calorific value, a key fuel parameter,
was also measured for each sample using a calorimeter analyzer
(5E-C5508, CKIC Co., Ltd., Hunan, China) according to
ASTM D5865-19 standards.53−55
The experiment was conducted using a BFB combustion

2.2. Numerical Method. To simulate each experiment,
laboratory-scale BFB equipment was designed using an ERN. A
1-D simulation reactor was then constructed to mimic a
circulating fluidized bed reactor based on the ERN reactor
described in a previous study.35,37 Figure 2 presents a schematic
illustration of an ERN-based BFB.
As the detailed chemical composition of volatile matter is not
yet completely understood, it is generally treated as a
hypothetical substance represented by CxHyOz or a combination
of different species.56 In this study, the species composition in
the volatile matter was calculated using thermodynamic
equilibrium and kinetic models based on FLASHCHAIN,
incorporating data from the PC Coal Lab.57−59 The calculated
chemical species from the volatile components of coal included
tar, CH4, H2, CO, CO2, H2O, HCN, and NH3, following a
similar approach to that used in a previous study35 to investigate
coal devolatilization. The compositions of the devolatilization
gas and char obtained from the program were then used as
inputs to a perfectly stirred reactor (PSR1) to perform
devolatilization reactions using Chemkin-Pro. A simplified
schematic diagram of the coal−ammonia mixture is presented
in Figure 3. The volume of PSR1 was set to 2% of the total
reactor volume, following a previously described methodology.35 Subsequently, a bubbling bed was established as the
combustion zone in PSR2, where air was supplied to facilitate
combustion reactions. Coal pyrolysis was precalculated using
the PC Coal Lab program, as previously described.35 PSR
consists of a small, thermally insulated chamber with inlet and
outlet ducts. A steady flow of fuel and oxidizer was introduced in
such a manner that high-intensity turbulent mixing ensured that
the contents of the reactor remained nearly spatially uniform.
Thus, the conversion from reactants to products is primarily
controlled by chemical reaction rates rather than by mixing

ROP and pathway analyses of NO were performed based on

the Nakamura model, it was observed that as the total amount of
NH3 increased, conversion of the initial NH3 to NH2 and N2H2

also increased, leading to reduced NH3 reactivity. Thus, the

the NH3 reaction was suppressed. This suppression occurred
due to the increased consumption of radicals for converting NH3
to NH2. Consequently, NO reduction and its conversion to

HNO. The net rate of NO ROP and the formation of HNO were
more strongly influenced by NH2 and O radicals than by the
previous results associated with the PA/SA ratio.

trend consistent with the experimental results at a concentration
of 10% RNH3. However, as the RNH3 increased beyond 20%, the
NO reduction effect became excessive, resulting in minimal NO
emissions, which was consistent with the trends observed in the
results presented in Section 3.2. This trend was also supported

by the ROP analysis results for NO (Figure 9). Additionally, in
the Okafor model, NH3 slip was observed to begin at 875 ppm
for 20% co-firing and increased to 8320 ppm at 30% co-firing.
These results indicate that consistent with previous simulation
results, the Okafor model exhibited lower reactivity than did the
Nakamura model at low temperatures (1100 K).

an increase in RNH3, the reaction between NH2 and O radicals to
produce HNO was inhibited while the reaction generating N2H4
was promoted. This occurred because O radicals were
consumed during the conversion of NH3 to NH2, leading to
unburned NH3 at 20 and 30% RNH3 due to a shortage of O
radicals. Overall, the simulation results revealed differences in
the NO emissions. However, the ammonia co-firing simulations
indicated that the models exhibited similar trends concerning
the PA/SA ratio, resulting in comparable NO formation and
reduction.

on NOx formation and reduction revealed that the formation of
NO was primarily governed by elementary reactions involving
N-containing intermediate species, such as NH2, O radicals, and
HNO radicals. At a high RNH3, the concentration of the O
radicals decreased with an increasing NH3/O2 ratio, which led to
decreased HNO formation. This decrease occurred because
many of the O/OH radicals were consumed by the dehydrogenation reactions of NH3, which consequently inhibited NO
formation reactions between NH2/HNO and O/OH radicals,
resulting in reduced NO emissions.69,70 According to the BFB
simulation results under these conditions (PA/SA and NH3
ratios), an enhancement in the reaction between NH2 and HNO
is required to further elucidate the detailed chemical
mechanisms and better simulate ammonia oxidation at low
temperatures.
The overall simulation analysis indicated that the trends in
NO emissions simulated by the Nakamura model differed from
those of the experimental results. Similarly, the Okafor model
demonstrated low NO oxidation reactions due to the lower
ammonia reactivity at 1100 K, where the generated NO was
converted to N2O through a relatively high NO reduction
reaction. Consequently, both the Nakamura and Okafor models
failed to accurately represent the NO emissions. Furthermore,
N2O emissions also showed significant discrepancies, indicating
that improvements in low-temperature models are required to
accurately simulate the co-firing of ammonia and coal at low
temperatures.

3.4. Effect of the Advanced Model on NOx Formation
and Reduction. The results of the analyses described in
Sections 3.2 and 3.3 confirmed that the reactions of the NH2
radical significantly affected the consumption rate of NH3 and
the selectivity for forming NO and N2. It was hypothesized that
enhancing the reactivity of NH2 and the formation of HNO
improved the overall reactivity of NH3 under fluidized bed
temperature conditions. Subsequently, we refined the reaction
subset for NH3 oxidation based on the Nakamura model, which
is specifically designed for ammonia oxidation at low temperatures. The reactions used for these improvements are detailed
in Table 4.
As presented in Figure 6, after the conversion of NH3 to NH2
in the Nakamura model, the radicals generated in the NxHy
reactions led to increased NH2 production. In the temperature
range for which the Nakamura model was originally developed,
these reactions work together to promote NH2 consumption
and suppress high NO production.
However, at the fluidized bed temperature of 1100 K, the
decomposition reactions involving NH2 (e.g., NH2 + NO =
NNH + OH; NH2 + NO = N2 + H2O; NH2 + NO2 = N2O +
H2O) decreased due to the low-temperature conditions. Thus,
only the reaction in which NH2 was converted to HNO
maintained high reactivity, resulting in the simulation of
excessive NO production.

Based on the current pathway analysis, the primary factor for
the discrepancy between the current experimental and
simulation results for NO was identified as the rapid NH2 + O
= HNO reaction and the slow NH2 + NO reaction, which was
refined using the Glarborg model. When NH3 is oxidized under
conditions with a high excess air ratio, a substantial amount of
HO2 radicals can form, promoting the oxidation of NO to NO2.
We incorporated this reaction, NO + HO2 = NO2 + OH, into the
model and adjusted it. To better simulate the low N2O emissions
observed, we advanced the existing N2O reaction rates were
advanced. Specifically, to reflect that N2O is reduced to N2 more
rapidly in the presence of O2, we adjusted the frequency factor
coefficients for the reactions of N2O + O = N2 + O2 and N2O +
H = N2 + OH, consistent with a previous report.76
The advanced model demonstrated a prediction accuracy
higher than that of the existing model, as presented in Figures 10
and 11. Figure 10 presents the results of ammonia co-firing
based on the PA/SA ratio, comparing the previously used
Okafor and Nakamura models, the Glarborg model used for
improvements, as well as Nakamura’s 2024 model and the
advanced model. Similarly, Figure 11 illustrates the ammonia cofiring results based on RNH3, comparing the Okafor model,
Nakamura model, Glarborg model, and the advanced model.
This improvement is attributed to the adjustments made to the
HNO production reaction and the NO reduction reaction of
NH2 during NH3 oxidation, as described above.

N2O using experimental data from PFRs.

4. CONCLUSIONS
In this study, we developed a chemical reactor network model of
a 1-D BFB to investigate the influence of low temperatures
(approximately 1100 K) on NOx formation and the reduction
reaction pathways of ammonia/coal co-firing using developed
detailed chemical kinetics. The main points and conclusions are
as follows:
1. Ammonia co-firing with coal was simulated in a BFB
device under low-temperature conditions (1100 K) with
various PA and NH3 ratios, using the coal combustion
reactions from previous studies5,46, and detailed reactions
from the advanced Nakamura39 and Okafor38 models. To
validate the simulation, we compared the results of coal
combustion experiments conducted under varying PA/
SA ratios to the simulation results. Subsequently, we
conducted fluidized bed ammonia co-firing experiments
and verified the simulation model under different
ammonia co-firing conditions, ranging from 0 to 20%.
2. NO emissions simulated by the Nakamura model tended
to be higher than the experimental results with varying
PA/SA and RNH3. The Okafor model excessively
simulated NH2-driven NO reduction, failing to accurately
represent NO emissions. The simulation results from the
two models indicated that previous models cannot
