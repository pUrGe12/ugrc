ABSTRACT: In the SCR systems of heavy-duty diesel engines, urea deposits formed under
real operating conditions differ significantly from laboratory-prepared samples in morphology,
composition, and structure. Under real operating conditions, the urea solution undergoes
multiple dynamic deposition cycles, forming dense and compositionally complex stubborn
deposits. Conventional laboratory methods�such as single evaporation or single-layer liquid
film models�fail to replicate this process. To address this limitation, this study proposes a
multilayer liquid-film heating crystallization method. The method is used to investigate the
fundamental differences between crystallization on pre-existing crystal layers and static singlelayer crystallization. Crystals derived from single-layer and multilayer liquid films of equivalent
total thickness were analyzed using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), Fourier-transform
infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The results indicate that (1) the first urea crystal layer in
multilayer crystallization impedes heat transfer to the second liquid film, promoting the formation of lower-temperature crystal
components; (2) multilayer urea crystals require higher temperatures for thermal decomposition compared to single-layer crystals;
(3) when the temperature decreases (with the first layer’s crystallization temperature being not lower than the heating temperature
of the second layer), the crystal mass from multilayer crystallization exceeds that from single-layer crystallization of equivalent liquid
film thickness; (4) multilayer crystallization, involving secondary deposition and a superimposed thermal history, facilitates the
development of ordered columnar or layered microstructures with relatively clean surfaces.

1. INTRODUCTION
To comply with increasingly stringent environmental regulations (e.g., Euro VI and the U.S. EPA Tier 4 standards),1,2
selective catalytic reduction (SCR) technology has emerged as
the predominant and efficient approach for controlling nitrogen
oxide (NOx) emissions in diesel engine aftertreatment
systems.3,4 In SCR systems, a 32.5% urea−water solution
(UWS) is injected into the exhaust pipe as a reducing agent to
effectively mitigate NOx emissions. However, urea crystallization�caused by factors such as exhaust temperature, flow rate,
urea dosing rate, and aftertreatment system geometry�remains
a significant operational challenge that compromises system
performance.5 The SCR process involves complex physical and
chemical interactions. Accumulation of urea deposit layers
increases exhaust backpressure, reduces engine efficiency, and,
in severe cases, can completely block the exhaust pipe, leading to
engine malfunction. Although various mitigation strategies have
been developed, including refined atomizing nozzles,6 optimized
static or swirl mixers, and real-time urea dosing control
algorithms,7 crystallization persists as a critical issue. Recent
studies have advanced our understanding of this phenomenon.
Börnhorst8 demonstrated through multiple UWS injection
experiments that existing deposits can dissolve into subsequent
urea films, influencing further crystallization. Prabhus9 observed
that higher exhaust flow rates significantly affect urea deposit
formation. Xia10 proposed a detailed urea decomposition

Although such simplifications aid in understanding fundamental

also chemically more complex, frequently containing substantial

Existing methods neglect the kinetic processes of coalescence,
spreading, and secondary crystallization of subsequent droplets
or liquid films on preexisting crystal layers, as well as the
resulting thermal superposition effects. This simplification leads
to systematic differences in the microstructure, chemical
composition, and thermal stability between laboratory-grown
crystals and actual stubborn deposits. Consequently, the
crystallization mechanisms derived from traditional approaches
cannot accurately explain the fouling patterns in practical
systems, nor can they provide a reliable basis for developing
deposit removal strategies. Therefore, developing a crystallization simulation method that captures the effects of multiple
deposition cycles and superimposed thermal history and
systematically elucidating the fundamental differences between
subsequent crystallization (occurring on preexisting crystal
surfaces) and initial single-layer static crystallization have
become key scientific issues for a deeper understanding of
SCR deposit formation mechanisms and for achieving precise
prevention and control.
This study proposes a “multi-layer urea−water solution liquid
film heating crystallization” simulation method. The core
objective of this approach is to systematically reveal and validate

the differences in morphology and composition between
crystallization that occurs on preexisting deposit layers (as a
new substrate) and crystallization from a static single-layer liquid
film.
By focusing on the distinctions between multilayer and singlelayer urea crystallization, this research conducts evaporationcrystallization experiments on single-layer and multilayer liquid
films of equivalent total thickness. The findings are intended to
provide insights into the design and structural optimization of
SCR systems.

2. PREPARATION OF UREA CRYSTALS
2.1. Crystallization Setup

To meet the demand for precise environmental control in this
study, we designed and constructed a custom temperaturecontrolled heating platform. A schematic of the overall setup is
presented in Figure 1.
An infrared liquid crystal heating stage (Model DB-2WJ)
served as the system’s heat source. With a rated power of 4000 W
and a maximum operating temperature of 600 °C, this stage
offers excellent temperature uniformity and rapid heating
response, enabling accurate simulation of the wall temperature
conditions in a diesel engine exhaust system under various
operating modes.
A precision peristaltic pump (Model YZ2515x) was employed
for solution delivery. The pump provides a flow-rate range of 0−
270 mL/min and can be finely adjusted via a digital interface,
ensuring stable delivery of the urea−water solution at a preset
flow rate.
To direct the solution precisely to the heating zone, we
connected a flexible tube to the pump outlet. The outlet of the
tube was vertically positioned above the heating stage by using
an adjustable stand, allowing the distance and lateral position of
the tube orifice to be precisely controlled. This configuration
enables the urea−water solution to be dispensed as a continuous
stream, accurately and reproducibly dripping or spreading onto a

2.2. Fabrication of Multilayer Crystals

To prepare the first crystallization layer, the disk on the heating
stage was heated to a target temperature. A predetermined
volume of urea solution (32.5%) was then dispensed onto the
disk and heated until complete crystallization was achieved.
Subsequently, with the first layer remaining on the disk, the
heating stage was set to the desired temperature for the second
layer. Another aliquot of urea solution was added and heated
until it was fully crystallized, thereby forming the second layer.
Finally, the obtained crystalline products were measured, and
their corresponding parameters were systematically recorded.

3.1. Comparative Analysis of Urea Crystallization Mass

Each individual experiment was repeated three times. The
average mass was calculated (with a standard deviation below
0.01), and the crystalline sample whose mass was closest to the
average was selected for compositional analysis.

Among the various parameters characterizing urea crystallization, the mass of the crystallized deposit is the most direct and
readily measurable parameter. By systematically comparing the
mass of deposits formed under multilayer and single-layer
control conditions with equivalent total liquid film thickness, the
quantitative impact of the “layering effect” on the final solid yield
can be directly elucidated. This comparative approach not only
reveals differences in deposition efficiency between the
sequential and single-step crystallization processes but also
provides critical preliminary evidence and a data-driven entry
point for probing the underlying mechanisms. These mechanisms may include variations in evaporation efficiency due to
interfacial thermal resistance or mass loss attributable to the
generation of volatile byproducts from side reactions.

2.3. Selection of Experimental Operating Conditions

The thermal decomposition of urea proceeds through three
distinct mass-loss stages,5 with the third stage initiating above
380 °C. When the temperature exceeds 400 °C and is
maintained for a sufficiently long duration, the residual mass
approaches zero. At heating temperatures below 100 °C, the
crystallization rate decreases significantly, hindering subsequent
experimental procedures. Therefore, the temperature range of
100 to 400 °C was selected as the operating condition for this
study.
The heating crystallization time of a urea liquid film is
proportional to its thickness. For example, a urea film with a
thickness of 0.5 mm requires approximately 1 h to fully
crystallize at 200 °C. To avoid environmental variations caused
by excessively long experimental durations that may affect the
results, the film thickness should be controlled to remain below
0.5 mm. However, an excessively thin film leads to brittle
crystals, making collection more difficult. Therefore, the
thickness should be no less than 0.1 mm. In summary, the
urea film thickness should be maintained between 0.1 mm and
0.5 mm. For multilayer urea crystallization�formed by

4. CHARACTERIZATION OF UREA CRYSTALLINE
DEPOSITS
In this study, the following samples were characterized:
multilayer urea crystals with a fixed first-layer crystallization
temperature of 300 °C and second-layer crystallization temperatures of 200 °C, 300 °C, and 400 °C, respectively, and singlelayer urea crystals prepared at crystallization temperatures of
200 °C, 300 °C, and 400 °C, respectively. The obtained
crystalline deposits were characterized as follows: Fouriertransform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, model: Thermo Fisher
Scientific Nicolet iS20) was used to measure the absorption
peaks and deduce the functional group composition; thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, model: Netzsch STA449 F3) was
employed to obtain the mass change as a function of
temperature; and scanning electron microscopy (SEM, model:
ZEISS Gemini SEM 300) was utilized to observe the
microstructure and analyze the variation in elemental
composition.
The data obtained from these techniques were subsequently
analyzed to elucidate the differences in composition, thermal
stability, and microstructure between the single-layer and
multilayer crystallization systems.

4.2. Results and Analysis of Thermogravimetric Analysis
(TGA)

This study employed thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) to
characterize both multilayer and single-layer urea crystallization
samples.25 By precisely measuring the mass change of the
samples during programmed heating and analyzing the
characteristic decomposition temperatures and mass-loss steps
in their thermal profiles, the differences in chemical composition
and thermal stability between the two types of crystallization can
be effectively discerned. This analysis not only quantifies the
relative abundances of urea and its thermal decomposition
products (e.g., biuret, cyanuric acid) within different crystalline
samples but also provides critical thermodynamic evidence for
elucidating potential interfacial thermal barrier effects that may
occur during the multilayer crystallization process.
As shown in Figure 11, the thermogravimetric (TG) curve of
the 300 °C−200 °C multilayer urea crystallization exhibits three
distinct mass-loss steps. The first step, occurring at approximately 100 °C, is attributed to the decomposition of residual
urea. The second mass loss, centered around 200 °C,
corresponds to the thermal degradation of biuret. Finally, the
third step near 300 °C indicates the presence and decomposition

additional mass-loss step compared to that of the 300 °C singlelayer control group. This indicates the presence of distinct
chemical components in the multilayer urea crystallization that
are absent in the single-layer urea crystallization. Notably, this
additional decomposition event occurs within the temperature
range of 200−250 °C, which aligns with the characteristic
thermal degradation zone of cyanuric acid (CYA). Therefore,
this observation strongly supports the incorporation of CYA
within the 300 °C−300 °C multilayer structure.

engineering guidance for optimizing urea injection strategies,

reliability and emission reduction efficiency of aftertreatment
systems.

6. SUMMARY
6.1. Key Findings

In summary, the key findings of this study are as follows:
1 The first urea crystal layer in multilayer crystallization
impedes heat transfer to the second liquid film, promoting
the formation of lower-temperature crystal components.
2 Multilayer urea crystals require higher temperatures for
thermal decomposition compared to single-layer crystals.
3 When the temperature decreases (with the first layer’s
crystallization temperature being not lower than the
heating temperature of the second layer), the crystal mass
from multilayer crystallization exceeds that from singlelayer crystallization of equivalent liquid film thickness.
4 Multilayer crystallization, involving secondary deposition
and a superimposed thermal history, facilitates the
development of ordered columnar or layered microstructures with relatively clean surfaces.

7.1. Next Research Steps and Discussions

Based on the key findings revealed in this study�including heat
transfer barriers, enhanced thermal stability, and structural
ordering in multilayer crystallization�subsequent research will
be carried out in the following aspects. First, a more in-depth
investigation is required into the influence of the first urea crystal
layer with different thicknesses on the crystallization of the
second layer. Second, the effect of the first urea crystal layer on
the second layer, which leads to changes in the critical
temperature thresholds for compositional variations within the
urea crystals, also warrants further study. Furthermore, it is
planned to compare and validate the experimental findings with
actual field data collected from heavy-duty truck SCR systems in
real-world operation.

7. CONCLUSIONS
In this study, a “multi-layer liquid film heating crystallization”
simulation method was developed to mimic the multilayer
dynamic deposition characteristics of urea crystals in actual SCR
systems. The method was designed to reveal the unique
behavior of subsequent crystallization on preexisting crystal
surfaces and to elucidate the fundamental differences from
single-layer static liquid film crystallization of equivalent
thickness. The main contributions of this work are summarized
as follows:
(1) Heat transfer barrier and formation of low-temperature
components:
The first urea crystal layer significantly hinders heat transfer to
the second liquid film, directly leading to the formation of
crystalline components at lower temperatures in the multilayer
system. This finding provides a new theoretical perspective for
explaining the common compositional gradients and lowtemperature-stable phases observed in real deposits.
(2) Difference in thermal decomposition behavior:
The thermal decomposition temperature of multilayer urea
crystals is considerably higher than that of single-layer crystals,
indicating that the superimposed thermal history and secondary
crystallization alter the phase stability. Consequently, the
pyrolysis regeneration temperature conditions derived from
single-layer crystallization models may be insufficient to
effectively remove actual deposits.
