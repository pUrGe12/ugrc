1. Introduction
Chemical warfare agents (CWAs), especially vesicant mustard
gas, pose severe threats to global security and public health due
to their high toxicity and persistent hazard.1–4 Eﬃcient materials capable of simultaneous adsorption and detoxication of
CWAs are urgently required for advanced protective
applications.5–7 Traditional protective materials, such as activated carbon, synthetic polymers, and powder-based porous
substrates, suﬀer from inherent limitations: low adsorption
eﬃciency, poor reusability, or risk of powder dispersion, which
restrict their practical deployment.8 With the rapid and largescale development of nanocarbon materials, a variety of novel
nanocarbon adsorbents have continuously emerged, among
which carbon nanotube (CNT) lms stand out as the most
prominent. CNT lms with interconnected three-dimensional
(3D) porous networks have emerged as promising carbon-

The development of catalytic materials for biochemical
warfare agent (CWA) degradation has evolved from monometallic to multimetallic nanoparticles. Early studies focused
on monometallic nanoparticles (e.g., Fe, Co, Ni, Mn, Cu) and
their oxides, which exhibited basic activity for CWA decomposition but suﬀered from limited active sites, poor stability, and
low resistance to poisoning. Štengl et al. prepared Fe/MnO
nanoparticles by hydrolysis and demonstrated that they have
good degradation properties for HD and GD.10 Prasad et al.
prepared MnO particles by hydrothermal method and demonstrated that it did not completely degrade 2-chloroethyl ethyl
sulphide (2-CEES) within 7 h.11,12 To address these drawbacks,
bimetallic and trimetallic nanoparticles were developed,
leveraging synergistic electronic interactions and geometric
eﬀects between diﬀerent metals to enhance catalytic activity
and durability. That showed improved degradation eﬃciency
toward mustard gas simulants. Kumar et al. prepared NiO/CoO/
ZnO/Fe2O3 nanoparticles by hydrolysis method and achieved
10 h degradation of HD.13 However, these conventional multimetallic systems are oen limited by element miscibility, phase
separation, and insuﬃcient tunability of active sites, restricting
their performance in complex CWA degradation reactions.

In recent years, high-entropy alloy nanoparticles (HEA NPs),
composed of ve or more principal elements in nearequiatomic ratios, have emerged as a new class of catalytic
materials. Beneting from the high-entropy stabilization eﬀect,
severe lattice distortion, sluggish diﬀusion, and cocktail
synergistic eﬀects, HEA NPs form single-phase solid solutions
with abundant multivalent metal sites and diverse local electronic environments.14 These unique features provide multiple
active sites for tandem redox reactions, enabling eﬃcient
adsorption and deep degradation of CWAs. Compared with
traditional monometallic and multimetallic nanoparticles, HEA
NPs exhibit superior catalytic activity, structural stability, and
anti-poisoning ability, making them ideal candidates for
advanced CWA detoxication materials.
Herein, we report an ultrafast carbothermal shock (CTs)
strategy to fabricate FeCoNiCrMn high-entropy alloy (HEA)
nanoparticles (50–150 nm) uniformly anchored on interconnected three-dimensional (3D) carbon nanotube (CNT) lm
networks within 55 ms. Compared with conventional carbon
materials loaded with single-metal nanoparticles, the distinctive advantage of this composite lies in the integration of
a porous CNT framework with multicomponent HEA nanoparticles. The interconnected CNT network provides abundant
accessible sites and transport pathways for 2-CEES vapor
adsorption, while the FeCoNiCrMn nanoparticles oﬀer chemically diverse metal active sites that facilitate the subsequent
catalytic transformation of 2-CEES. The phase structure,
elemental composition, and surface valence states of the
composite are systematically characterized. The adsorption
performance toward 2-CEES vapor and the catalytic degradation
performance following the direct addition of liquid 2-CEES are
evaluated separately, and the underlying tandem redox mechanism is investigated. This work demonstrates the potential of

2.

Experimental

2.1

Materials

The primary chemical reagents used in this experiment include:
cobalt chloride hexahydrate (CoCl2$6H2O, 98%), manganese
acetate tetrahydrate (Mn(CH3COO)2$4H2O, 98%), ferric chloride (FeCl3, 99%), nickel chloride hexahydrate (NiCl2$6H2O,
analytical grade), chromium trichloride (CrCl3, $99%), nitric
acid (HNO3, analytical grade), 2-chloroethyl ethyl sulde (2CEES, analytical grade), and high-purity argon gas (Ar, $99%)
2.2

Preparation steps

In this work, HEA NPs were synthesized and attached to CNTs
using CTs method. First, 0.005 mol each of FeCl3, NiCl2,
CoCl2$6H2O, CrCl3, and Mn (CH3COO) 2$4H2O were sequentially added to 500 mL of an ethanol solution. The mixture was
stirred uniformly to create a precursor solution. For the pretreatment of the CNTs, they were functionalized by treatment
with a 45% HNO3 solution for a duration of 30 min, and then
dried at 60 °C for 15 min. The pre-treated CNTs were exposed to
the precursor solution for 30 min to ensure thorough saturation, followed by a 20 min drying phase at 60 °C. Once
completely dry, the samples were xed to the electrodes in
a Joule heating chamber using a conductive adhesive. FeCoNiCrMn@CNTs were synthesized by shocking at 30 V and 4.5 A
for 55 ms under an Ar atmosphere. Fig. 1(a) shows the preparation process.

2.3

Experimental methods

2-CEES possesses a functional group conguration highly
similar to that of sulfur mustard; however, its inherent toxicity
is markedly lower. Consequently, it is universally accepted as
a standard simulant for this blistering chemical warfare agent
in research and development. Therefore, 2-CEES was selected in
this paper as the experimental compound for the static
adsorption simulation and subsequent elimination work of
sulfur mustard.
For static adsorption performance testing, experiments were
conducted in a constant temperature and humidity chamber.
This chamber automatically regulated the temperature and
relative humidity to maintain constant conditions of 25 °C and
50% relative humidity. As shown in Fig. 1(b), the experimental
setup consisted of a 10 mL outer beaker and a 5 mL inner
beaker, with 50 mL of liquid 2-CEES placed in the inner beaker. A
5 mg sample was positioned outside the inner beaker but within
the outer beaker, and the entire system was sealed with cling
lm. During this process, liquid 2-CEES served exclusively as
the vapor source, and the composite lm was exposed to the
generated 2-CEES vapor without direct contact with the liquid
phase. The adsorption system was maintained at 25 °C for 48 h
to allow suﬃcient exposure to 2-CEES vapor. Following this
step, the apparent adsorption capacity of the sample for 2-CEES
was determined.
Aer equilibrium was reached, the vessel was opened and
the adsorbed sample was placed in a dry dish to equilibrate for
1 h. Ultimately, the change in mass of the sample before and
aer adsorption was measured by weighing the sample and the
results were expressed in terms of adsorbed amount per unit
mass of sample (mg g−1).
For the degradation test, 50 mg of FeCoNiCrMn@CNTs
nanocomposites were rst added to a 10 mL glass container,
which was subsequently placed in a constant temperature and
humidity chamber set at 25 °C and 50% relative humidity. Aer
0.5 h of reaction, the container was sealed and 5 ml of 2-CEES
solution was injected into it using a micro syringe. Aer varying
durations of sustained reaction, the whole reaction system was
extracted using CDCl3 as the extractant. Aer the system was le
to stand and reach equilibrium, 0.5 mL of the supernatant was
taken and transferred to a Nuclear Magnetic Resonance tube,
and the signal peaks were determined by Nuclear Magnetic

Resonance (NMR, Bruker), which led to the calculation of the
conversion rate of 2-CEES.
To enable detailed morphological analysis, the HEAs were
examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Sigma300)
and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL JEM-2100
Plus). Sample preparation for TEM involved dispersing the
CNTs decorated with alloy nanoparticles in ethanol via ultrasonication. Following this dispersion, a micropipette was used
to transfer the suspension onto copper grids for observation.
The phase composition was conrmed using X-ray diﬀraction
(XRD, D8 Advance), followed by inductively coupled plasma
atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES, Agilent ICPOES730) to
determine the relative elemental content. The chemical
composition was characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher ESCALAB Xi+). For 2-CEES, the
static adsorption was determined by a universal analytical
balance; the chemical dissipation was characterized by NMR
hydrogen spectroscopy results of the adsorbed samples by
600 M NMR spectrometer.

Results and discussion

FeCoNiCrMn@CNTs

signicant support for functional applications in the eld of

However, during the period from 30 min to 30 h, the
apparent adsorption capacity of FeCoNiCrMn@CNTs remains
lower than that of pristine CNTs. This behavior may be attributed to the coexistence of adsorption and catalytic degradation
processes. Pure CNTs primarily rely on physical adsorption

have been reported to promote nucleophilic substitution and

to partial degradation during the adsorption process.28,29 As
a result, part of the adsorbed 2-CEES may undergo catalytic

the net gravimetrically measured mass gain during this

porous structure, ultimately resulting in a substantially higher
equilibrium adsorption capacity at 48 h (636 mg g−1). The ve

3.3 Degradation properties of FeCoNiCrMn@CNTs for 2CEES
To elucidate the degradation performance of FeCoNiCrMn@CNTs toward the mustard gas simulant 2-CEES,
samples were characterized using 1H NMR spectroscopy31 aer
diﬀerent reaction times. The corresponding results are shown
in Fig. 7. The initial 1H NMR spectrum of 2-CEES exhibits four
characteristic signal peaks at chemical shis of 3.60 ppm,
2.85 ppm, 2.56 ppm, and 1.25 ppm, corresponding to four
independent hydrogen atoms within the 2-CEES molecule.
Specically, the triplet peak at 3.60 ppm is attributed to the CH2
group, the triplet peak at 2.85 ppm to the CH2Cl group, the
quartet peak at 2.56 ppm to the SCH2 group, and the triplet peak
at 1.25 ppm to the CH3 group. During the catalytic degradation
of 2-CEES, the 1H NMR spectrum exhibited distinct peak
evolution. Starting at 30 min post-reaction, a new peak gradually emerged at 1.57 ppm, while the original four characteristic
peaks (3.60, 2.85, 2.56, and 1.25 ppm) progressively weakened in
intensity over time, becoming nearly absent by 12 h. This
phenomenon indicates the continuous consumption of 2-CEES
molecules alongside the formation of new compounds. The
chemical shi of the 1.57 ppm peak resides in the methylene

Experiments reveal that the degradation of 2-CEES by
FeCoNiCrMn@CNTs is achieved not only through surface
catalytic reactions but also through the synergistic participation
of multiple elements within the HEA.35 XPS analysis conrms
the coexistence of multiple metal valence states on the catalyst
surface, including Co0/Co2+/Co3+, Fe2+/Fe3+, Ni2+, Cr3+, and
Mn4+. Rather than acting independently, these metal centers
can provide complementary functions involving electron
redistribution, substrate activation, and intermediate stabilization. During the initial reaction stage, low-valent metal
species, particularly Co0, can serve as electron-donating centers
to facilitate activation and cleavage of the C–Cl bond in 2-CEES,
initiating reductive dechlorination.36 This process generates
hydroxyethyl ethyl sulde (HEES, HO–CH2–CH2–S–CH2–CH3),37
corresponding to the newly emerged 1.57 ppm peak in the 1H
NMR spectra. The continuous accumulation of HEES indicates
that C–Cl cleavage proceeds more readily than its subsequent
conversion.
In addition to the directly involved Co and Fe redox couples,
Ni2+ may contribute to the catalytic process through local
electronic-structure regulation and interfacial charge redistribution. In the multicomponent HEA lattice, Ni2+ can interact
electronically with neighboring Co/Fe sites, thereby modulating
the electron density of adjacent active centers and facilitating
electron transfer during the sequential activation of 2-CEES and
its intermediates. Meanwhile, Cr3+, which is relatively stable
under the present reaction conditions, is less likely to function
as a primary redox donor but may contribute through its strong
metal–ligand interactions. In particular, Cr3+ can provide
coordination sites for polar or oxygen-containing surface
intermediates derived from HEES, helping stabilize adsorbed
intermediates and maintain their favorable adsorption congurations for subsequent transformation. Thus, Ni2+ and Cr3+
can complement the redox-active Co/Fe/Mn centers by regulating the local electronic environment and stabilizing reaction
intermediates, respectively.

rapid adsorption of the pollutant within the porous network
and initiating the catalytic degradation reaction at the interface.
It was clearly observed that the extracted liquid changed from
colorless to pale yellow, which macroscopically conrms the
occurrence of the reaction.
From a micro perspective, under the participation of trace
water provided by environmental humidity (50% relative
humidity), the degradation of 2-CEES by FeCoNiCrMn@CNT
follows a two-step tandem reaction pathway. First, low-valent
metal species (Co0) on the catalyst surface act as electron
donors, attacking the C–Cl bond of 2-CEES molecules to initiate
a reductive dechlorination reaction, yielding HEES as a stable
intermediate. Subsequently, high-valent metal ions (Mn4+, Co3+)
exert an oxidative eﬀect, causing partial C–S bond cleavage and
side-chain oxidation of HEES to generate small-molecule fragments such as ethanol. The stable accumulation of HEES
indicates that C–S bond cleavage is the rate-limiting step for
deep mineralization in this system.
