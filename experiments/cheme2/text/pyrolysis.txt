speciﬁc inﬂuence of alkali metals on nitrogen evolution during fast co-pyrolysis remains unclear. This study

1. Introduction
Amidst the global transition toward sustainable energy systems,
biomass has emerged as an indispensable carbon-neutral
resource, oﬀering a viable pathway to mitigate the global
greenhouse eﬀect and promote resource circularity.1,2 Its valorization can proceed either through catalytic upgrading, in
which the biomass-derived platform molecules are selectively
converted into liquid biofuels and ne chemicals over tailored
solid catalytic materials,3–5 or through thermochemical
conversion, in which the whole feedstock is converted directly
into heat and power. The latter remains the dominant route for
large-scale deployment. Despite its environmental advantages,
the large-scale deployment of biomass as a primary monofuel is
severely constrained by its intrinsic physicochemical limitations, most notably its low energy density, high moisture
content, and seasonal supply uctuations.6,7 These drawbacks
necessitate the co-ring of biomass with traditional coal as

a pragmatic and techno-economically viable strategy.8 In coalred power plants, this integrated approach not only
enhances the energy utilization of biomass but also signicantly reduces the overall NOx emissions owing to the inherent
low sulfur/nitrogen content of biomass.9 Notably, pyrolysis
represents the foundational stage in the thermochemical
conversion of solid fuels as it precedes and dictates the subsequent gas-phase and heterogeneous combustion reactions.
Fundamental to this stage is the evolution of fuel-bound
nitrogen (fuel-N), which governs the initial devolatilization
(volatile-N) and char formation (char-N) during pyrolysis, playing a vital role in the ultimate formation and reduction of NOx
emissions.10 Therefore, elucidating the fuel-N migration and
transformation pathways during the co-pyrolysis of coal and
biomass is a critical prerequisite for developing precisioncontrolled, low-emission combustion technologies.
In fact, pyrolysis is a sophisticated process wherein the
partitioning of fuel-N is modulated by multiple factors. While
nitrogen evolution during the pyrolysis of individual biomass
components has been extensively studied, scientic consensus
remains elusive.11 Currently, NH3 and HCN are universally
recognized as the primary gaseous NOx precursors, with HCN
typically prevailing under high-heating-rate conditions due to

its favorable formation kinetics from the cleavage of heterocyclic nitrogen functionalities.12,13 Given that proteins are the
predominant nitrogen-containing components in biomass,
researchers have utilized amino acids or their pyrolytic derivatives as model compounds to decipher these pathways. These
studies have highlighted the critical catalytic inuence of alkali
and alkaline earth metals (AAEMs) on nitrogen migration.14 For
example, Wei et al.15 utilized TG-FTIR to demonstrate that K, Na,
Ca, and Mg inhibited the formation of NH3, HCN, and HNCO
during glutamic acid pyrolysis. In contrast, Zhou et al.16 reported that K+ and Na+ promote NH3 formation while inhibiting
HCN, the primary gaseous nitrogen product. In addition,
density functional theory (DFT) studies by Zhang et al.17 and Liu
et al.18 further revealed that K+ and Na+ primarily lower the
activation energy barriers for nitrogen-containing ring decomposition, thereby accelerating volatile-N release. Overall, existing research is insuﬃcient to elucidate complex nitrogen
transformation and migration in actual pyrolysis environments,
which remains a subject of intense debate. In the scenario of
biomass and coal co-pyrolysis, the underlying mechanisms are
even more ambiguous. Lin et al.19 and Yuan et al.20 observed
that the co-pyrolysis of sub-bituminous coal and wheat straw led
to a reduction in NH3 release, but an increase in HCN emission.
Considering the signicant impact of alkali metals on singlefeedstock pyrolysis, the variations in gaseous and solid
nitrogen precursors during co-pyrolysis are likely driven by the
abundant alkali metals (predominantly soluble K and Na)
present in the biomass.15,21 Nevertheless, a systematic elucidation of how specic alkali metals (e.g., Na) mechanistically
inuence the transformation and migration of gas/solid-phase
nitrogen during biomass and coal co-pyrolysis is still lacking,
presenting a critical gap between fundamental molecular
theories and complex industrial applications.
Herein, this work elucidates the mechanistic inuence of
alkali metals (Na) on nitrogen transformation and migration
during fast co-pyrolysis of biomass and coal. Using sawdust (SD)
and lignite coal (LC) as feedstocks in a vertical xed-bed reactor,
we systematically compared the generation of volatile-N
between acid-washed (demineralized) and alkali (Na)-loaded
samples at a range of temperatures. Furthermore, X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to characterize the eﬀects of the alkali metal Na on the speciation and
abundance of char-N functionalities, while Raman spectroscopy
was applied to probe the evolution of char carbon skeletons,
especially the relative proportions of small and large aromatic
ring structures. By correlating volatile-N release, char-N speciation, and carbon structural parameters from Raman spectroscopy, this study aims to clarify how Na regulates nitrogen
retention and transformation during the co-pyrolysis of coal
and biomass. The results demonstrate that Na promotes the
release of NH3 and HCN during the pyrolysis of SD at 500 °C but
inhibits it at 800 °C, whereas for LC and all blended samples, Na
suppresses the release of both species at 500 °C and 800 °C. This
study is expected to provide critical theoretical insights for
optimizing biomass-coal co-combustion technologies and
achieving the reduction of NOx emissions at the source.

2.

Experimental methods

2.1

Sample preparation and characterization

The biomass feedstock selected for this study was sawdust (SD,
original), and the coal sample was lignite coal (LC, original).
Both raw materials were ground and sieved to collect particles
in the size range of 150–180 mm. The samples were subsequently
dried in an oven at 105 °C for 12 h. The proximate and ultimate
analyses of the raw samples are presented in Table 1 and were
conducted in accordance with Chinese national standards GB/
T212-2008 and GB/T31391-2015. The sodium (Na) content in
the raw materials was quantied using ion chromatography (IC,
DIONEX AQ-1100), yielding 0.27% for SD-original and 2.75% for
LC-original. To remove alkali metals, a demineralization
process was applied to both the coal and biomass. A 50 g sample
was mixed with 500 mL of 3 mol L−1 hydrochloric acid in
a beaker. The mixture was stirred using a magnetic stirrer in
a water bath at 60 °C for 24 h. The residue was then ltered via
vacuum ltration and washed repeatedly with deionized water
until the ltrate reached a neutral pH (∼7). Finally, the samples
were dried at 105 °C for 24 h to obtain the demineralized
samples. The proximate and ultimate analysis results for these
samples (SD/LC) are also shown in Table 1.
Sodium-loaded biomass was prepared using a wet impregnation method. Specically, NaCl was mixed with the acidwashed biomass at a ratio of 0.02 g g−1 and dissolved in deionized water. The solution was then subjected to rotary evaporation. Upon drying, a biomass sample impregnated with 2 wt%
NaCl was obtained. All samples used for pyrolysis in this study
underwent acid washing. This corresponds to approximately
0.79 wt% Na, about 3 times the content originally present in the
untreated sawdust and well below that of the untreated coal, so
that the catalytic eﬀect is resolvable. The acid-washed sample
loaded with Na was designated as SD-Na. Blends were prepared
by mechanically mixing biomass with coal at mass percentages
of 5% and 15%; the acid-washed blends were labeled 5SD/LC and
15SD/LC, while the blends containing Na-loaded biomass were
designated as 5SD/LC-Na and 15SD/LC-Na.

Product analysis

During pyrolysis, fuel-N is partitioned among NOx precursors,
tar-bound nitrogen, N2 and char-N.22 Since NH3 and HCN are
the two species that are subsequently oxidized to NOx in the
combustion zone, they were selected as the typical N-containing
products and quantied by online FTIR gas analysis. The term
volatile-N is used hereaer to denote the sum of these two
species in this work. The distribution of char-N functionalities
was determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) N 1s
deconvolution, which resolves the relative speciation of char-N.
This combination of online FTIR and XPS has been widely
adopted to examine nitrogen evolution during rapid pyrolysis of

present system.

2.4

Raman spectroscopy analysis

Raman analysis of the samples was performed using a confocal
Raman microscope (Thermo Fisher DXR2). The rst-order
Raman spectra of coal and biomass chars typically exhibit two
characteristic bands: the G band and the D band. Following the
ten-peak tting method established for coal chars, a ten-peak
tting method was used to deconstruct the Raman spectra,
and the relative intensities of the tted peaks were used to
characterize the relative content of various structures in the
sample chars25,26 (Fig. 2).
In this method, the VR, VL, and GR peaks represent amorphous carbon structures and small aromatic ring structures.
The D peak represents the C–C structures between aromatic
systems containing not less than six rings.27 The AVR+VL+GR, AD,
and Aall represent the peak areas of amorphous carbon, large
aromatic rings, and the total peak area, respectively. The
parameters AVR+VL+GR/AD and AVR+VL+GR/Aall reect the ratio of
small aromatic rings to large aromatic rings and the relative
content of small aromatic rings, respectively.

3.

Results and discussion

3.1 Nitrogen evolution characteristics during individual
pyrolysis of biomass/coal
3.1.1 Volatile-N transformations. During fast pyrolysis of
biomass and coal, fuel-N is transformed into various volatile
nitrogen (volatile-N) species, including NH3, HCN, N2, HNCO,
and NO. Since extensive studies28–30 have identied NH3 and
HCN as the primary volatile-N precursors of NOx during the

© 2026 The Author(s). Published by the Royal Society of Chemistry

pyrolysis of coal and biomass, this work specically focused on
their generation.
Fig. 3 illustrates the evolution proles of apparent NH3 and
HCN during rapid individual pyrolysis of SD and LC. At a pyrolysis temperature of 500 °C, the conversion of fuel-N to NH3 was
signicantly higher than that to HCN for SD. In contrast, LC
exhibited generally low conversion rates for both NH3 and HCN.
At 800 °C, the conversion to HCN surpassed that of NH3 for both
SD and LC. Additionally, the HCN yield from SD was higher than
that from LC, whereas its NH3 yield was relatively lower. These
phenomena can be attributed to diﬀerences in fuel characteristics and the redistribution of nitrogen functional groups induced
by temperature variations. During low-temperature pyrolysis (500
°C), the thermally unstable N-A present in biomass preferentially
decomposes to release NH3, while the stable pyrrolic and pyridinic nitrogen forms in coal are diﬃcult to crack at low
temperatures.31,32 As a result, the conversion of fuel-N to volatileN was signicantly higher for biomass than for coal at lower
temperatures. Under high-temperature pyrolysis conditions,
HCN formation is positively correlated with volatile matter
content; thus, SD featuring high volatile content exhibited
a higher HCN conversion compared to LC. Notably, NH3 is
primarily derived from the hydrogenation of N-Q.24 As the N-Q
content in LC is higher than in SD, the coal demonstrated
a higher conversion rate to NH3 at elevated temperatures.
3.1.2 Evolution of Char-N structures. In this study, the
assignment of binding energies to specic nitrogen forms and
the subsequent peak deconvolution were conducted based on
established literature.10,33 The primary nitrogenous component
in biomass is identied as N-A, whereas N-5 is the dominant
form in coal.34 Fig. 4 presents the XPS (N 1s) spectra of the
samples and their chars, and Fig. 5 illustrates the quantitative
results of these nitrogen functional groups.
As shown in Fig. 4a and d, the XPS spectra of SD exhibited
a single peak corresponding to N-A, while LC displayed one
main peak and two additional shoulder peaks, indicating that
the predominant structure was N-5, accompanied by a small
amount of N-X.10 During rapid pyrolysis at 500 °C, the N-A in SD
char decreased signicantly, with N-6 becoming the dominant
char-N structure at 43% (Fig. 4b). This transformation occurs

(specically HCN) via ring-opening reactions, while the

increasing temperature. Note that the N-Q in raw coal is typically associated with pyridinic structures involving hydroxyl
groups, whereas char-N in the form of N-Q represents nitrogen
incorporated in the graphitic layers through heterocyclic sixmembered rings, possessing higher stability.36
In summary, during low-temperature pyrolysis (500 °C), the
labile N-A in SD decomposes to generate NH3, while the stable
N-A converts into heterocyclic char-N via condensation, serving
as a precursor for high-temperature HCN formation.24,34 Due to
the stability of heterocyclic rings at low temperatures, SD
releases a signicant amount of NH3 but only trace HCN,
whereas coal releases minimal volatile-N with little change in
char-N composition. During high-temperature fast pyrolysis
(800 °C), the labile N-A in SD continues to release NH3. Meanwhile, stable N-A converts into heterocyclic nitrogen, and
a larger proportion converts to N-5 than during low-temperature
pyrolysis. These heterocyclic structures further decompose to
form HCN or gradually transform into stable N-Q. In LC,
heterocyclic nitrogen rst undergoes ring-opening driven by
radicals to form HCN, followed by a gradual transformation into
N-Q. The N-Q in coal also undergoes slow hydrogenation to
form NH3. Consequently, compared to SD, pyrolysis of LC favors
the solid-phase transformation of N-5 into N-6 and N-Q,
resulting in lower HCN emissions.

4. Conclusions
This study investigated the migration and transformation of
fuel-N during rapid pyrolysis and co-pyrolysis of sawdust (SD)
and lignite coal (LC) at 500 °C and 800 °C, using acid-washed
and Na-loaded samples to clarify the regulatory role of Na. The
two fuels followed distinct nitrogen evolution pathways: in SD,
the labile N-A underwent thermal decomposition to release
NH3, while the stable N-A converted into heterocyclic char-N
via condensation; whereas in LC, the heterocyclic nitrogen
required ring-opening to form HCN, so that the pyrolysis of LC
favored the solid-phase transformation of N-5 into N-6 and N-Q
and resulted in lower volatile-N release. For individual pyrolysis of SD, the addition of Na promoted the formation of NH3
and HCN at 500 °C but inhibited their generation at 800 °C,
while for the pyrolysis of LC and all blended samples, Na
inhibited the evolution of NH3 and HCN at both 500 °C and
800 °C, indicating that Na suppressed the conversion of fuel-N
to the gaseous NOx precursors. This inhibition originated from
two coupled eﬀects: Na suppressed the ring-opening of
heterocyclic nitrogen into HCN and the hydrogenation of N-Q
into NH3, and Raman analysis showed that Na inhibited the
polycondensation of small aromatic rings, with the volatile-N
yield of the blended chars decreasing monotonically as the
proportion of small aromatic rings increased at both temperatures, so that the char-N was redistributed towards N-5 and NQ. These ndings indicate that alkali metals in co-red
systems act not only as a source of slagging and fouling, but
also as an active regulator of gaseous NOx precursor formation.
In particular, demineralization pretreatments commonly
adopted to mitigate ash-related problems may therefore
inadvertently enhance the release of NH3 and HCN so that
alkali metal management in biomass-coal co-ring involves
a trade-oﬀ between ash behavior and the suppression of
primary NOx precursor formation.
