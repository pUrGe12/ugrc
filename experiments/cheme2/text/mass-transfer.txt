ABSTRACT: Comprehensive research on phase behavior during the
staged conversion of producing oil reservoirs into underground natural
gas storage remains limited, particularly under cyclic injection−
withdrawal conditions where interphase mass transfer continuously
evolves. In this study, we performed high-pressure/high-temperature
(HP/HT) visualization experiments using a multistage protocol that
mimics reservoir-to-UGS conversion, including primary depletion
followed by six rolling cycles of gas injection−withdrawal (upper
pressure fixed at 29 MPa; lower pressure stepwise decreased to 10
MPa). To quantify the interphase mechanism in a cycle-resolved
manner, we introduced the net mass transfer (NMT) and the net mass
transfer rate (NMRT), defined as the net interphase transfer magnitude
normalized by the pressure change during each injection or withdrawal
step; NMRT > 0 indicates net gas-to-oil transfer dominated by condensation/dissolution, whereas NMRT < 0 indicates net oil-togas transfer dominated by vaporization/extraction.The results reveal three distinct stages: (1) Depletion Stage, characterized by oilphase vaporization and initial gas cap formation, with recovery efficiency controlled by depletion pressure; (2) Rolling Storage
Construction (Cycles 1−4), which injection shows positive NMRT (0.115 mL/MPa; condensation/dissolution), lightening the oil
and improving the phase envelope, whereas withdrawal shows negative NMRT (−0.357 mL/MPa; vaporization/extraction),
enriching C7+ and weakening extraction over cycles;(3) Late-Stage Construction (Cycles 5−6), where condensation/dissolution and
vaporization/extraction processes approach equilibrium, with net mass transfer rates nearing zero. After depletion and six rolling
cycles, the cumulative oil recovery reached 56.02%, and the available gas storage capacity under standard conditions reached 9.98 ×
103 cm3, corresponding to 68.6% of the reactor pore volume. These findings clarify how cycle-wise mass transfer governs phase
behavior evolution and provide guidance for optimizing operational parameters during reservoir-to-UGS conversion.

1. INTRODUCTION
Underground gas storage (UGS) is a key component of
modern natural gas supply systems, providing an effective
means to regulate seasonal imbalances and short-term peak
demand. It operates by injecting and withdrawing natural gas
from subsurface geological formations, including depleted oil
and gas reservoirs, salt caverns, and aquifers, in conjunction
with dedicated surface and underground facilities. Owing to
their favorable sealing capacity and mechanical stability, these
formations allow gas to be stored safely under high pressure for
extended periods. Gas is typically injected during periods of
surplus supply and produced during peak consumption,
thereby enhancing supply flexibility, market stability, and
overall energy system resilience. With advantages such as large
storage capacity, relatively low cost, high operating pressure,
and long service life, UGS is widely regarded as the most
practical large-scale energy storage technology available at
present.1,2

Among the various types of UGS, facilities converted from
depleted oil reservoirs have attracted growing interest,
particularly in regions characterized by mature oilfields and
limited availability of salt cavern resources. This development
mode integrates enhanced oil recovery (EOR) and gas storage
within a single reservoir system. During cyclic gas injection,
natural gas accumulates in the upper part of the reservoir to
form a secondary gas cap, which gradually expands through
gravity segregation and displaces crude oil downward, leading
to improved oil recovery. At the same time, the injected gas
occupies the pore space vacated by produced oil, resulting in a

continuous increase in effective storage volume. Once the
designed storage capacity and operating pressure range are
achieved, the reservoir can be fully converted into an
underground gas storage facility.3−6 In China, where many
oilfields have entered the late stages of development, the
conversion of depleted or near-depleted oil reservoirs into
UGS has been identified as a practical and strategic option for
enhancing national energy security and improving the natural
gas storage infrastructure.7,8 Based on operational characteristics and functional objectives, the conversion process is
commonly divided into three stages.2,7 During the depletion
stage, oil is produced under natural reservoir energy until
pressure declines to the threshold required for conversion.
This is followed by the phased conversion stage, in which
cyclic gas injection and coordinated oil−gas production are
implemented. During this stage, a secondary gas cap develops
and storage capacity increases through mechanisms such as gas
displacement, gravity drainage, and vaporization of light
hydrocarbons.9−11 In the final cyclic injection−production
stage, the reservoir functions as a mature gas storage facility,
supplying gas through repeated injection and withdrawal
cycles.
Although cyclic injection−withdrawal behavior has been
extensively studied in gas reservoirs, condensate gas reservoirs,
and gas reservoirs with oil rims, research specifically addressing
the conversion of pure oil reservoirs into UGS remains
relatively scarce. Early experimental investigations were mainly
focused on condensate gas reservoirs and gas-cap oil reservoirs.
Tang et al. performed constant volume depletion (CVD),
constant composition expansion (CCE), and cyclic injection−
production experiments, showing that condensation and
vaporization processes coexist during cyclic operation, with
intermediate components progressively enriching the oil phase
until a dynamic equilibrium is established.12,13 Subsequent
studies combining laboratory experiments with compositional
simulation further demonstrated that evaporation and
extraction dominate during the initial injection cycles, whereas
dissolution and diffusion become more significant during stable
cyclic operation, resulting in gradual stabilization of fluid
properties.14,15 More recent work has begun to explore oil
reservoir−based UGS systems. Experimental and numerical
results indicate that mutual oil−gas displacement can enhance
both oil recovery and gas storage capacity, while deterioration
of the remaining oil properties in later cycles may limit
incremental recovery efficiency.16 Investigations of gascondensate reservoirs with oil rims have further revealed the
strong influence of oil zones on phase behavior and mass
transfer during depletion and cyclic operation, with a
noticeable decline in evaporation efficiency in later cycles.17
Compared with gas- and condensate reservoir−based UGS,
phase evolution and interphase mass transfer in oil reservoir−
based systems are considerably more complex due to the
simultaneous presence of crude oil, injected gas, and residual
hydrocarbons under alternating high-intensity operating
conditions. Despite progress in understanding phase behavior
in gas and condensate reservoirs, systematic studies focusing
on oil reservoir conversion�particularly with respect to phase
evolution and interphase mass transfer during the depletion
and staged conversion processes�are still lacking.18−23 To
address this deficiency, reservoir fluids were synthesized in the
laboratory using an autoblender to accurately reproduce in situ
fluid compositions. An experimental platform based on an
ultrahigh-pressure phase behavior analysis system was

established to simulate oil−gas interactions during gas

physical simulation experiments were carried out to assess
dynamic storage capacity and recovery efficiency during the
initial conversion stage, providing a sound theoretical basis for
storage capacity evaluation and operational optimization of oil
reservoir−based UGS.

2. MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1. Experimental Installation
This study developed an autonomous Cyclic Injection-Production
Simulation System for Gas Storage (CIPSS-GS), based on ultrahighpressure fluid phase behavior analysis technology. The system
architecture comprises three core functional modules: Phase Behavior
Simulation Unit; Injection-Production Process Control Unit;
Intelligent Linkage Control Unit.
This system can comprehensively simulate the phase evolution
processes throughout the entire lifecycle of a UGS facility (Reservoir
Conversion → Gas Injection → Gas Withdrawal). It is capable of
conducting experiments under conditions up to 200 °C and 100 MPa.
The system includes a 400 mL high-temperature, high-pressure
reactor equipped with a pressure sensor with 0.1% full-scale accuracy
and a temperature sensor with ± 0.1 °C accuracy.
The observation system consists of sapphire viewports coupled
with a high-speed camera system (capable of a 5000 fps acquisition
rate). The key functionalities of the system include fluid
reconstitution under reservoir conditions and the measurement of
phase behavior parameters. The system offers several significant
advantages, including synchronous multiparameter acquisition (e.g.,
PVT relationships, phase transition characteristics), fully automated
program control, continuous cyclic simulation experiments, and highprecision phase transition capture.

2.3. Experimental Process
The experimental procedure for the Rolling Phase Equilibrium Study
during Injection-Production in Oil Reservoir Gas Storage is illustrated
in Figure 1. The specific experimental steps are as follows:
(1) Under the conditions of formation temperature and saturated
formation pressure, the prepared experimental fluid is
transferred into the reactor, and the key phase parameters of
the formation fluid under the original formation conditions are
measured after the system stabilizes.
(2) From the original formation pressure constant volume
depletion mining to the reservoir pressure, approximately
30% of the formation oil within the reservoir is produced based
on the total reservoir volume.
(3) After gas injection reaches the upper pressure limit of the gas
storage operation (29 MPa), a three-level pressure level
constant volume depletion mining to 20 MPa. During this
depletion phase, the volume of oil and gas within the
observation window is monitored, which is the first cycle of
the gas storage injection and production rolling reservoir
construction.
(4) Step (3) is repeated to carry out the rolling construction of gas
storage. Each cycle is divided into n+2 pressure level constant
volume depletion mining to the lower pressure limit of 20−2·
(n−1) MPa. The process continues until the oil and gas
volume change is less than 1% and the lower pressure limit
reaches 10 MPa, at which point the rolling construction is
complete. During the depletion phase, the oil and gas volume
is monitored through the observation window, and the
properties of the produced gas are analyzed. Starting from
the second cycle, each stage involves a 5% recovery rate at the
lower pressure limit, followed by testing of the oil and gas
properties within the kettle.

According to Figure 14, the extraction at the lower pressure
limit of the first cycle (20 MPa) increases the crude oil
recovery rate by 3.68%, much smaller than that of the second
cycle (6.93%). The reason is that the reservoir pressure of 20
MPa at the time of depletion exploitation coincides with the
pressure interval of the first cycle, and its pressure disturbance
fails to increase the sweep coefficient. The gas recovery is
dominated by oil phase evaporation extraction, with the gas
phase condensate dissolution playing the auxiliary part. The
components of depletion mining and the first cycle mining are

relative to the oil phase extraction. The gas extraction in the
second cycle increases the oil recovery rate by 6.93%. The
average oil recovery rate of Cycles 2−6 is about 5%, consistent
with the conclusions in the literature. With increased injectionproduction cycles, the crude oil produced by gas extraction

increases the crude oil recovery rate by 0.8%.

4. DISCUSSION
The interphase mass transfer during underground gas storage
(UGS) construction is governed by vaporization extraction (oil
to gas) and condensation dissolution (gas to oil), with the net
mass transfer rate (NMRT) indicating the prevailing direction
(Figure 17). During constant volume depletion, vaporization
extraction dominates, leading to oil volume reduction, gas cap

vaporization-driven extraction (negative NMRT) predominates during the production stage, whereas condensation−
dissolution processes (positive NMRT) dominate during gas
injection. For Cycles 1−4, injection-stage NMRT values range
from 0.063 to 0.146 mL/MPa, with an average of 0.115 mL/
MPa, indicating a persistent net mass transfer from the gas
phase to the liquid phase during injection. By contrast,
production-stage NMRT values vary from −0.254 to −0.476

The effect of mass transfer patterns on recovery and storage
performance is significant. In the first cycle at 20 MPa,
recovery increases only by 3.68%, far lower than the 6.93% of
the second cycle, because depletion and early stage injection
preferentially extract light components (CO2 + C1−C6),
thereby limiting additional efficiency in Cycle 1. From Cycles

decreases progressively as heavy C7+ components accumulate
and extraction weakens, with only 0.8% improvement in Cycle
6. After depletion (27.8%) and sixrolling cycles, overall
recovery reaches 56.02%. Storage capacity shows a parallel
evolution: depletion and the first cycle contribute 19.6% of the
final capacity, driven by vaporization-induced gas cap
formation, while Cycles 2−6 contribute ∼6% per cycle under
condensation-dominated injection. Across all cycles, the
average NMRT of the oil phase is 0.079 mL/MPa during
injection and −0.238 mL/MPa during production, confirming
that evaporation−extraction is more intense than condensation−dissolution and thus plays the decisive role in enhancing
both oil recovery and storage capacity. These findings highlight
that controlling the balance between extraction and dissolution
is critical for optimizing UGS rolling construction strategies.

5. CONCLUSION
This study conducted physical simulations to explore the phase
behavior and mass transfer dynamics during the conversion of
oil reservoirs into gas storage. The process was divided into the
depletion, early rolling construction (Cycles 1 to 4), and later
rolling construction (Cycles 5 to 6) stages. Each stage
displayed distinct mass transfer patterns and crude oil property
changes.
(1) The depletion stage was dominated by evaporation
extraction, transferring lighter oil components into the
gas phase. In Cycles 1 to 4, gas injection enhanced
condensation and dissolution into the oil, temporarily
improving crude oil properties, while subsequent
production shifted the process back to evaporation
extraction and heavier oil composition. During Cycles 5
to 6, the mass transfer rates between evaporation and
condensation reached equilibrium, indicating the
effectiveness limit of the extraction process.
(2) Experimental results showed that the gas−liquid mass
transfer increased oil recovery by about 5%, mainly
during the early cycles. With continued cycling, the
heavy component content (C7+) in crude oil increased,
hindering gas-phase extraction and reducing incremental
recovery. By the sixth cycle, oil recovery from gas-phase
extraction was only 0.8%.
(3) After depletion and six rolling cycles, the final oil
recovery was 56.02%, and storage capacity reached
68.6% of the reservoir volume. The first cycle accounted
for 19% of storage growth, while subsequent cycles
