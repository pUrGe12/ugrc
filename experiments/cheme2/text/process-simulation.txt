ABSTRACT: The recovery of biobased isoprene from dilute
fermentation off-gas remains a major challenge due to low product
concentrations and energy-intensive downstream separations. In this
study, a process intensification strategy based on absorption−desorption
was developed and evaluated to efficiently recover isoprene from air
mixtures. The proposed system integrates absorption using 3methyltridecane, vacuum degassing, steam stripping, and condensation
with multiple recycle loops for solvent, water, and residual isoprene. Of
the thermodynamic models examined, the Peng−Robinson equation of
state was used for the absorption section while UNIFAC was used for
steam stripping. Process optimization demonstrated up to 95% isoprene
recovery and 98% product purity while significantly reducing energy
consumption. Sensitivity analyses revealed that solvent temperature, solvent-to-feed ratio, and feed isoprene concentration strongly
influence process efficiency, with energy demand decreasing from 26 to 10 MJ kg−1 as feed concentration increased. Compared to
literature benchmarks, the proposed design achieved up to 40% reduction in steam usage. These results highlight absorption-based
separation as a viable and energy-efficient pathway for recovering volatile hydrocarbons from dilute bioprocess streams and provide
design insights for future scale-up and experimental validation.

1. INTRODUCTION
The growing global demand for renewable alternatives to
fossil-derived products has driven the development of
biorefineries. These are advanced facilities that convert
biomass into an array of biofuels, biochemicals, and
biomaterials. Unlike conventional petroleum refineries, biorefineries use renewable feedstocks such as lignocellulosic
biomass, agricultural residues, and microalgae to produce highvalue compounds, reducing greenhouse gas emissions.1 This
shift from fossil-based petroleum refineries to renewable
biorefineries has positioned biorefineries as cornerstones of
the green industrial revolution, with projections suggesting that
biobased chemicals could account for over 20% of the global
chemical market by 2030.2 Among these chemicals, isoprene is
a volatile C5 hydrocarbon, notable for its widespread use in
manufacturing synthetic rubber, adhesives, and specialty
polymers. Its importance lies in its role as a precursor to
polyisoprene, which provides the elasticity and durability
required for high-performance applications. Traditionally,
isoprene has been produced from fossil-based processes such
as naphtha cracking or as a byproduct of ethylene production,
both of which are energy-intensive and dependent on finite
resources.3 The emerging transition to biobased isoprene
production within biorefineries not only aligns with sustainability goals but also mitigates supply chain vulnerabilities tied
to fossil feedstocks.4

Following production, recovering isoprene from biobased
fermentation presents significant downstream separation
challenges because the crude product stream contains water,
residual sugars, alcohols, and other volatile impurities, all of
which necessitate robust purification steps to achieve marketready quality. 9 Another barrier in biobased isoprene
production is the extremely low concentration of isoprene in
feed gas.10,11 Reported titers can be as low as 0.0009 g L−1 in
aerobic fermentation and 0.0021 g L −1 in fed-batch
fermentation.12 These low yields arise from inherent metabolic
and pathway limitations, including restricted flux through
biosynthetic routes and inefficiencies in enzyme activity or
cofactor availability.13 Isoprene concentration in the off-gas
depends primarily on microbial productivity and oxygen flow
rate. Attempting to increase titers is further constrained by
oxygen limitation in air. Increasing the oxygen flow rate would
require excess oxygen and substantially raise fermentation
costs.

as pervaporation or gas stripping during fermentation. Those
have been investigated to enhance hydrocarbon recovery and
productivity from dilute biostreams.14,15 Distillation, including

sustainability for large-scale deployment.16,17 Lower-energy

2. METHODS
2.1. Process Design and Simulation
In our paper, we first designed a process that was able to extract
isoprene from the fermenter off-gas into a liquid form. Additional
components were added to the process to make the purity of isoprene
higher in the final product. Different parameters in the process were
analyzed using Aspen Plus sensitivity tools to keep the energy
requirements as low as possible while maintaining high recovery and
purity of isoprene. Figure 1 illustrates the process diagram of the
integrated absorption and desorption system. The process consists of
a 32-stage absorber and a 35-stage stripper column, both operating
with 3-methyltridecane as the absorption solvent. 3-Methyltridecane
was selected as a representative heavy, branched C14 hydrocarbon
whose properties suit this separation: its very low volatility minimizes
solvent carryover into the treated gas and product and allows easy
regeneration by steam stripping, while its nonpolar character gives
good affinity for isoprene, keeps the solubility of the permanent gases
(air and CO2) low, and provides near-complete immiscibility with
water for clean water−solvent separation downstream. A pressure
drop of 0.5 bar is included in the absorber, which processes a large gas
throughput (∼1500 kg h−1). The stripper carries a much smaller
vapor (steam) flow and therefore has a comparatively negligible
pressure drop, which was neglected; it was modeled at constant 1 bar.

Three distinct recycle loops are incorporated: the solvent recycle
loop (stream-10), the water recycle loop (stream-13), and the residual
isoprene recycle loop (stream-16). For the solvent and water recycling
loops, a fixed flow rate has been established, and a makeup stream is
employed to make up for losses of solvent and water that occur during
the process. Additionally, the residual isoprene recycle loop (stream16) is implemented to loop back the isoprene that escaped into the
gas phase to the feed, thereby enhancing the overall efficiency of the
process and reducing material losses. Two small purge streams
prevent water and CO2 from accumulating in the recycle loops. The
P-1 stream (∼1.08 kg h−1) corresponds to decantation of the aqueous
phase, which separates from the hydrocarbon solvent owing to the
very low mutual solubility of water and 3-methyltridecane. P-2 stream
(∼0.006 kg h−1) removes CO2 that accumulates in the gas recycle
loop. Both streams contain negligible isoprene and solvent (<0.01% of
throughput), so the recovery, purity, and energy results are unaffected.

2.3.1. Absorption. Modeling the absorption of isoprene from a
gas mixture into 3-methyltridecane solvent in Aspen Plus requires an
appropriate thermodynamic model. Due to limited availability of
experimental vapor−liquid equilibrium data for this multicomponent
system, model selection was based on expected phase behavior and

solubility in the solvent. PRWS is best suited for polar−nonpolar
interactions, but our process primarily involves nonpolar species. The
complexity in parameter tuning can introduce additional uncertainties
in each EoS. While PRWS performed well for CO2 solubility, its
excessive prediction of air solubility makes it unsuitable.
UNIFAC and NRTL work well for highly nonideal liquid mixtures
but struggle with permanent gases like air. UNIFAC overpredicted air
absorption, indicating misrepresentation of noninteracting gases in
solvent, a weakness consistent with its focus on liquid-phase
nonidealities rather than gas−liquid equilibria, as outlined by
Fredenslund et al.31 The NRTL model is specifically designed for
strong liquid-phase interactions, not weak van der Waals interactions
like air and hydrocarbons.32 Gas-phase behavior is not well modeled,
leading to inconsistencies in predicting phase equilibria. Both
methods had weaknesses in handling nonpolar gas−liquid systems,
making them unsuitable.
Among the evaluated models, the Peng−Robinson (PR) equation
of state33−36 was selected for the absorption section. PR is a cubic
equation of state extensively validated for nonpolar hydrocarbon and
gas-processing systems; at the low absorber pressure it reduces to
near-ideal vapor behavior while representing the weakly nonideal
hydrocarbon liquid through its standard mixing rules. A practical
advantage of the equation-of-state route here is that the supercritical
permanent gases (air and CO2) are represented directly within the
same model, without the separate Henry’s-law constants that an
activity-coefficient formulation would require. PR produced physically
reasonable, low air and CO2 solubilities�consistent with the
expected behavior of permanent gases in a heavy hydrocarbon, and
in contrast to the overprediction given by the activity-coefficient
methods (Figure 2). A fully predictive equation of state such as
PSRK37,38 was judged unnecessary, since it targets high-pressure
systems and vapor-phase nonidealities that are negligible here.
Because system-specific binary parameters were not regressed, the
absolute gas solubilities carry some uncertainty; however, the
screening in Figure 2 confirms that PR reproduces the physically
expected trends, supporting its use for this nonpolar absorber.
2.3.2. Desorption. After the isoprene is absorbed into the solvent,
it needs to desorb in order to be extracted. Steam stripping is a viable
method to do so. In a steam stripping column designed to remove
isoprene from a heavy 3-methyltridecane solvent using superheated
steam, the choice of the thermodynamic model influences the
predicted phase behavior, particularly in systems involving water and
hydrocarbons (Figure 3). As in the absorber, the stripper operates
near atmospheric pressure, so the vapor phase is again close to ideal.

By this stage the permanent gases (air and CO2) have largely been
removed in the absorber overhead and the vacuum degasser, so the
stripper processes an essentially condensable mixture of water,
isoprene, and solvent; the modeling emphasis therefore falls entirely
on the strongly nonideal, partially miscible water−hydrocarbon liquid,
and no Henry’s-law treatment of dissolved gases is required here
either.
Cubic equations of state (EoS), such as Peng−Robinson (PR) and
Soave−Redlich−Kwong (SRK), provide a consistent framework for
vapor−liquid equilibrium calculations39 and are widely used for
hydrocarbon systems. However, these models have known limitations
in representing highly nonideal interactions involving polar
components such as water.40−42 In particular, systems involving
water−hydrocarbon mixtures may exhibit phase behavior that is not
fully captured by cubic EoS formulations.43
Activity coefficient models such as UNIFAC are commonly used to
represent liquid-phase nonidealities and phase separation behavior in
systems involving water and hydrocarbons.44 These models are
suitable for describing systems where strong immiscibility exists
between phases and provide a practical representation of liquid-phase
behavior. Moreover, being a predictive group-contribution method,
UNIFAC does not require experimental binary interaction parameters�which are unavailable for this system�making it well suited
here.31,44
Hybrid approaches such as the Predictive Soave−Redlich−Kwong
(PSRK) model combine a cubic equation of state with a groupcontribution activity-coefficient model and are typically employed for
higher-pressure systems and where binary interaction parameters are
unavailable.37,38 Because the stripper operates near atmospheric
pressure, where vapor-phase nonideality is negligible, the added
complexity of a predictive equation of state is not warranted, and a
direct activity-coefficient description of the liquid phase was preferred.
In this study, UNIFAC was used as the property method for the
steam-stripping section because it captures the strong liquid-phase
nonideality and the water−hydrocarbon phase separation that govern
desorption, without requiring experimental binary parameters that are
unavailable for this system. In the absence of measured multicomponent VLE data, the selected methods were assessed against
physically expected behavior, the low solubility of permanent gases in
a heavy hydrocarbon (Figure 2) and the near-complete immiscibility
of water and the hydrocarbon solvent, and the predicted stripping
performance is benchmarked against the isoprene-recovery data of
Feher et al.24
Because the two sections present different phase behavior, sectionspecific property methods were used, PR for the absorption section
and UNIFAC for the stripping section, as is standard in Aspen Plus.

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Absorber

High efficiency in the separation of isoprene from the feed gas
was demonstrated by the absorption column, with 97% of the
isoprene being recovered into the solvent. Simultaneously,
99.8% of the air and 98% of the CO2 were removed. To
achieve this separation, a solvent-to-feed gas ratio of 1.2 was
required. However, through effective recycling, net solvent loss
was minimized to only 15 g kg−1 isoprene, highlighting the
sustainability of the process. A sensitivity analysis on solvent
temperature, as presented in Figure 4, revealed a significant
influence on the absorption efficiency of isoprene. It was found
that the lowest energy requirement per kilogram of isoprene
occurred at 2 °C. At lower temperatures, isoprene absorption
efficiency was markedly increased. This enhancement was
attributed to the high volatility of isoprene; reduced molecular
energy at lower temperatures decreased isoprene’s tendency to
remain in the gas phase, thereby promoting its dissolution into
the liquid solvent phase. Conversely, a sharp decline in
absorber efficiency was observed with increasing solvent

It should be noted that the simulations relied on simplified
thermodynamic models and steady-state operation, assuming
ideal column performance and compensated solvent/water
recycle loops. Dynamic effects, solvent degradation, and
potential impurity accumulation were not considered.
Furthermore, the purges were implemented as idealized
component separations. The water purge is supported by
water−hydrocarbon immiscibility (decantation); complete
CO2 removal is an approximation whose effect is negligible
given the very small purged quantity. These necessary
assumptions enabled tractable process design but highlight
the need for future pilot-scale validation. Future work should
focus on the development and operation of a pilot-scale system
to validate the simulation results. Additionally, the design and
selection of improved solvents may further lower energy
demand and overall process costs as the major energy demand
comes from the solvent heating. Overall, this study provides a
clearer understanding of how feed concentration and process
integration influence the efficiency of isoprene recovery,
offering design principles that were not previously established.

4. CONCLUSION
A comprehensive process simulation study was conducted to
assess the technique and optimize energy consumption for
purifying isoprene from a dilute air stream representative of
microbial fermentation off-gas. The objective was to recover
high-purity liquid isoprene while minimizing energy consumption and material losses. The separation scheme
employed an integrated design comprising absorption, vacuum
degassing, steam stripping, and condensation, with rigorous
thermodynamic modeling guiding process decisions. The
proposed system achieved 95% isoprene recovery with 98%
product purity. Recycle loops for solvent, water, and residual
isoprene were effectively incorporated to reduce material loss.
The process demonstrated strong scalability and compatibility
with biobased chemical production platforms, offering a
practical solution for the efficient recovery of dilute volatile
hydrocarbons.
Absorption was shown to be an effective method for
isoprene separation from fermenter off-gas, particularly when
integrated with heat recovery and recycling strategies. A key
finding was the sensitivity of energy consumption to the
isoprene concentration in the feed gas; enhanced aerobic
fermentation processes that yield higher isoprene concentrations could significantly reduce energy requirements per
kilogram of product.
