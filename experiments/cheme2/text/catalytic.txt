ABSTRACT: This study evaluates an integrated resource-recovery route for spent fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) catalysts,
consisting of alkaline roasting, water leaching, adsorption/desorption, precipitation recovery, and leaching-residue valorization.
Using the treatment of 1 t of spent FCC catalyst as the functional unit, environmental impacts were assessed in SimaPro 10.1 using
Environmental Footprint 3.1 (adapted) as the primary life-cycle impact assessment method, complemented by an operating-level
cost assessment, sensitivity analysis, and scenario analysis. Before product-substitution credits, the climate-change impacts of the wetrecovery and leaching-residue-valorization stages were 1,536.56 and 1,940.11 kg CO2-eq, respectively. After system expansion, all 16
main impact categories showed net environmental benefits under the adopted substitution assumptions; when the two freshwaterecotoxicity subindicators are included, all 18 displayed results were also net negative. The net climate-change impact was − 2,257.14
kg CO2-eq, while resource use for minerals and metals and fossil resources showed net reductions of 0.817 kg Sb-eq and 37,306.47
MJ, respectively. The estimated operating cost was 5,763.35 Chinese yuan (CNY) per t of spent catalyst, while the potential product
revenue was 53,254.45 CNY/t under the adopted market-price assumptions. These results indicate favorable operating-level
economic potential but should not be interpreted as evidence of industrial profitability. Electricity consumption is the dominant
driver of energy- and atmosphere-related impacts, whereas CuSO4·5H2O and ammonium-containing reagents are important
contributors to several toxicity- and eutrophication-related impacts. The results therefore support a coordinated optimization
strategy combining energy-efficiency improvement, low-carbon electricity, and reduction or substitution of high-impact reagents.

1. INTRODUCTION

Previous studies have explored several routes for utilizing the
inorganic framework of spent FCC catalysts. Zhang et al. used
spent FCC catalysts as fine aggregates in nonfired bricks and
evaluated the associated environmental risks, while Al-Jabri et
al. investigated their use as partial substitutes for cement or
sand in mortars.3,4 In ceramic and refractory applications,
Restrepo et al. demonstrated the use of La-containing spent
FCC catalysts as feedstock for mullite-based refractories,5 and

Spent fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) catalysts are continuously
generated during petroleum refining as catalyst activity declines
because of coke deposition, pore blockage, metal contamination, and structural deterioration. Owing to their aluminosilicate-rich matrix and the presence of recoverable metals,
spent FCC catalysts represent both an environmental-management challenge and a potential secondary resource. Long-term
stockpiling or landfilling may increase management burdens
and forego opportunities to recover the aluminosilicate
framework and valuable elements such as Ni, V, La, W, and
Mo.1,2 Developing treatment routes that combine resource
recovery with environmental-risk control is therefore important for the circular utilization of petrochemical solid wastes.

Ramezani et al. combined spent FCC catalysts with other solid

to eco-porous ceramics, asphalt fillers, and synthetic
zeolites.7−9 The thermo-engineering treatment method
proposed by Mymrin et al.10 also underscores the practical
engineering significance of coupling stabilization with the
material valorization of hazardous spent petrochemical
catalysts. Collectively, these studies demonstrate the valorization potential of the aluminosilicate framework, although
many routes remain concentrated on construction-material
applications or a single material product.
Metal recovery represents a complementary pathway that
targets the intrinsic value of recoverable elements in spent
catalysts. Ferella et al.11 summarized roasting, leaching,
separation, and purification as core operations for recovering
metals from refinery spent catalysts, while Hindawi et al.12
emphasized the secondary-resource value of molybdenumbearing spent catalysts. Hydrometallurgical leaching, bioleaching, and advanced separation technologies have continued to
develop; for example, Lv et al.13 reported molybdenum and
aluminum recovery from spent hydrotreating catalysts, and
Pathak et al.14 reviewed fungal bioleaching of refinery spent
catalysts. Advanced separation technologies are also increasingly important for improving recovery efficiency and product
quality.15 These studies provide useful methodological
references for metal extraction and separation. However,
refinery spent catalysts differ substantially in carrier composition, metal content, and metal occurrence state; therefore,
process conditions and conclusions derived from other catalyst
types cannot be directly transferred to spent FCC catalysts
without feedstock-specific verification.
Despite this progress, current spent-FCC-catalyst valorization strategies remain insufficiently integrated. A route
focused only on valuable-metal recovery may transfer part of
the environmental burden to the management of leaching
residues, particularly when residual metals or soluble salts are
not adequately controlled.2,3 Conversely, direct use of the
catalyst or its residue in low-value construction materials can
reduce disposal demand but may not fully utilize the value of
recoverable metals and the inorganic framework.10,11 An
integrated strategy should therefore connect valuable-metal
recovery, residual-risk control, and functional utilization of the
leaching residue within a single process chain. Environmental

burdens, avoided virgin-material production, and operatinglevel economic performance should also be evaluated within a
consistent system boundary.16,17
Life cycle assessment (LCA) provides a standardized
framework for identifying environmental hotspots and
potential burden shifting in complex resource-recovery
systems. The LCA framework adopted in this study follows
the principles of the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 14040 and ISO 14044.18,19 LCA has been
widely applied to waste management and resource recovery to
distinguish contributions from energy supply, chemical
production, transportation, and product substitution.20−22
For life-cycle impact assessment (LCIA), the Environmental
Footprint (EF) 3.1 (adapted) method provides a harmonized
set of midpoint indicators for multidimensional environmental
evaluation.23,24 ReCiPe 2016 Midpoint (H)25 is retained only
as a supplementary robustness check for global warming
potential (GWP), while ecoinvent v3.10.126 provides the
background inventories for electricity, chemicals, and materials.
On this basis, the present study evaluates an integrated
recovery and residue-valorization route for a W- and Mobearing spent FCC catalyst. Sodium−carbonate roasting is
used to convert W- and Mo-bearing phases into soluble
species, followed by water leaching, adsorption/desorption,
and precipitation, with ammonium paratungstate (APT) as the
principal tungsten product. The resulting leaching residue is
subsequently subjected to hazardous-component immobilization, porous-ceramic preparation, and loading with an
inorganic phase-change salt system. This downstream route
is intended to convert the leaching residue from a waste stream
requiring further management into a functional phase-change
energy-storage material. Porous ceramic matrices are widely
used as supporting frameworks for form-stable phase-change
materials because of their thermal stability, pore structure, and
encapsulation capacity.27,28
Three research gaps motivate the present work. First, most
reported studies emphasize either valuable-metal recovery or
material utilization of the catalyst framework/residue, whereas
integrated evaluation of both stages remains limited. Second,
environmental-risk control of leaching residues is often
considered separately from upstream metal recovery, limiting
assessment of whether burden shifting occurs across the full
process chain. Third, environmental impacts, avoided virginmaterial production, and economic performance are rarely

Accordingly, this study uses the treatment of 1 t of spent
FCC catalyst as the functional unit and combines LCA, an
operating-level cost assessment, sensitivity analysis, and
scenario analysis. The objectives are to (i) quantify the
stage-specific environmental burdens of wet recovery and
leaching-residue valorization, (ii) evaluate the potential
environmental credits associated with product substitution,
(iii) identify the main environmental and cost drivers, and (iv)
derive differentiated optimization priorities for energy use and
chemical inputs.
The main contribution of this study lies in the integrated
evaluation of valuable-metal recovery and leaching-residue
valorization rather than in assessing the two stages
independently. The analysis distinguishes foreground process
burdens from system-expansion credits and links environmental hotspots with operating-level economic drivers. In
addition, the sensitivity and electricity-scenario analyses reveal
that energy decarbonization and reagent management address
different impact categories, providing a more differentiated
engineering basis for process optimization than a single
carbon-reduction strategy.

2.2. Life Cycle Inventory
The spent FCC catalyst investigated in this study was obtained from
Jiangxi Minmetals Gao’an Nonferrous Metal Co., Ltd. According to
compositional characterization, the material contained 50.30 wt % Al
and 11.67 wt % Si within an aluminosilicate-rich matrix, while the
principal recoverable metals were 15.08 wt % W and 1.13 wt % Mo.
The alkaline-roasting/water-leaching route was therefore designed to
convert W- and Mo-bearing phases into soluble species, followed by
their separation and recovery, with APT as the principal tungsten
product.

99.50% of Mo were leached, corresponding to approximately 141.12
kg W and 11.24 kg Mo transferred to the leachate. After adsorption/
desorption and precipitation, 79.32% of the initial W, equivalent to
approximately 119.60 kg W, was recovered in 170 kg of APT. The

3.1. Environmental Impacts of the Integrated
Resource-Recovery Process

Sensitivity analysis was used to identify the foreground inputs to
which the environmental results are most responsive. Sensitivity
coefficients were calculated according to eq 1 following ref 29 as the
ratio of the relative change in an environmental impact result to the
relative change in the corresponding input parameter. A larger
absolute coefficient indicates a stronger response of that impact result
to the input parameter. Because the foreground inventory model is
linear with respect to the varied inputs, these elasticity coefficients are
directly related to the contribution of each input to the corresponding
impact result. The sensitivity analysis was performed for all 16 main
EF 3.1 (adapted) impact categories. Freshwater ecotoxicity was
additionally disaggregated into inorganic and organic subindicators for
graphical presentation, resulting in 18 displayed sensitivity results.

3.1.1. Environmental Impacts of the Wet Recovery
Stage. The environmental burdens of the wet-recovery stage
were evaluated for the treatment of 1 t of spent FCC catalyst.
The discussion below distinguishes climate change, resource
use, eutrophication/ecotoxicity, human toxicity, and atmosphere-related impacts to identify stage-specific environmental
hotspots.
Under EF 3.1 (adapted), the wet-recovery stage generated a
climate-change impact of 1,536.56 kg CO2-eq per t of spent
catalyst. Electricity was the largest contributor, accounting for
31.0%, followed by the 350 kg Na2CO3 input at 29.2%; direct
foreground CO2 emissions contributed approximately 3.0%
(Figure 2b). The importance of electricity reflects the carbon
intensity of the baseline Chinese electricity mix. Therefore,
carbon mitigation in this stage should combine lower
electricity demand with a lower-carbon electricity supply,
while also reducing or optimizing Na2CO3 consumption where
process performance permits.
A supplementary GWP comparison was performed using
ReCiPe 2016 Midpoint (H) and EF 3.1 (adapted). The
corresponding values were 1,553.75 and 1,536.56 kg CO2-eq, a
difference of approximately 1.1%. Sodium carbonate contributed 461.35 kg CO2-eq under ReCiPe and 448.20 kg CO2eq under EF 3.1, while electricity contributed 465.74 and
475.95 kg CO2-eq, respectively. The small differences arise
from differences in characterization models and factors

wet-recovery stage vary by impact category and cannot be
reduced to a single carbon hotspot.
Overall, the wet-recovery stage requires a differentiated
optimization strategy. Electricity and Na2CO3 are the primary
targets for reducing climate-change and fossil-resource
burdens, while CuSO4·5H2O is particularly important for
mineral-resource use, freshwater eutrophication, and noncancer human toxicity. Ammonium-containing reagents also
contribute substantially to selected toxicity and eutrophication
indicators. Accordingly, process optimization should combine
energy-efficiency improvement and low-carbon electricity with
reagent reduction, recycling, and, where technically feasible,
substitution of high-impact chemicals.

generates 890 kg of leaching residue, which enters the

phase-change-material preparation. Under EF 3.1 (adapted),
the climate-change impact of this stage is 1,940.11 kg CO2-eq
per t of spent FCC catalyst. Electricity accounts for 48.9% of
this impact, followed by silicon carbide (16.2%), magnesium
chloride (16.0%), sodium carbonate (7.1%), and direct
foreground CO2 emissions (4.7%). Electricity is also the
dominant contributor to fossil-resource use. The immobilization and porous-substrate-preparation units together consume
1,379.5 kWh, representing most of the 1,602 kWh electricity
demand for the residue-valorization stage. This confirms that
the environmental performance of this stage is strongly linked

The GWP robustness check gives 1,936.86 kg CO2-eq with

contributes 929.16 and 949.53 kg CO2-eq under the two

models, both identify electricity, silicon carbide, and

the residue-valorization stage.

more diversified. Electricity is the main contributor to fossilresource use and also contributes 38.0% of land use, followed
by magnesium chloride (16.0%) and silicon carbide (15.7%).

3.4. Scenario Analysis

The scenario analysis examines two complementary routes for
reducing electricity-related burdens. The business-as-usual
(BAU) case uses the baseline electricity mix and 2,405 kWh
of electricity per t of spent catalyst. The process-wide energyefficiency case applies a 30% reduction in total process
electricity demand, giving approximately 1,684 kWh/t. This
prospective scenario represents combined improvements in
furnace efficiency and thermal-energy utilization. The lowcarbon-electricity case keeps process electricity demand
unchanged and applies the projected 2060 electricity mix
(10.31% nuclear, 36.50% wind, 14.43% hydropower, 36.69%
solar, and 2.07% thermal power). This scenario isolates the
effect of electricity-supply decarbonization from process
energy-efficiency improvements.

4. CONCLUSION
This study evaluates an integrated route coupling valuablemetal recovery from W- and Mo-bearing spent FCC catalysts
with downstream valorization of the leaching residue. Using EF
3.1 (adapted), an operating-level cost assessment, sensitivity
analysis, and electricity-related scenario analysis, the study
identifies the principal environmental burdens, potential
substitution credits, and optimization priorities. The main
conclusions are as follows:
1. The two process stages have different environmental
hotspot structures. Under EF 3.1 (adapted), the climatechange impacts of wet recovery and leaching-residue
valorization are 1,536.56 and 1,940.11 kg CO2-eq per t
of spent catalyst, respectively. Electricity is important in
both stages; Na2CO3 and CuSO4·5H2O are additional
hotspots in wet recovery, while silicon carbide and
magnesium chloride are important contributors in
residue valorization.
2. Under the adopted system-expansion assumptions, all 16
main EF 3.1 impact categories exhibit net environmental
benefits; the same conclusion applies to all 18 displayed
results when the two freshwater-ecotoxicity subindicators are included. The integrated process achieves a net
climate-change impact of −2,257.14 kg CO2-eq/t,
together with net reductions of 0.817 kg Sb-eq in
resource use-minerals and metals and 37,306.47 MJ in
resource use-fossils. Because these credits depend on the
assumed conventional products, product mass balance,
and functional substitution, they should be interpreted
as scenario-dependent circular-economy benefits.
3. The estimated foreground operating cost is 5,763.35
CNY/t. Based on outputs of 170 kg APT and
approximately 1,481 kg composite phase-change material, the potential product revenue is 53,254.45 CNY/t
under the adopted market-price assumptions. This
indicates favorable operating-level cost-recovery potential. However, the present economic assessment excludes
capital investment, depreciation, labor, maintenance,
financing, taxation, transportation, and environmentalmanagement costs and therefore does not establish
industrial profitability.
4. Sensitivity and scenario analyses show that electricity
and chemical reagents govern different impact categories. Electricity primarily controls climate change and
several energy- and atmosphere-related indicators,

and illustrates a potential burden trade-off across impact
categories. The scenario therefore improves many, but not all,
environmental indicators.
The illustrative full wind- and hydropower-substitution cases
further reveal technology-specific trade-offs (Figure 8). In each
case, baseline process electricity is fully replaced by the
specified electricity source while all other foreground inputs
remain unchanged. Relative to the BAU case, wind and
hydropower reduce climate change by 39.3% and 40.7%,
particulate matter by 43.4% and 45.2%, and photochemical
ozone formation by 40.5% and 42.3%, respectively. Ozone
depletion shows more modest reductions of 6.6% and 8.1%.
Hydropower generally provides slightly larger reductions than
wind power for several categories, including freshwater
ecotoxicity and land use; land-use impacts decrease by 27.2%
under hydropower compared with 16.7% under wind power.
The trade-offs are also category specific: under the wind-power
case, freshwater ecotoxicity-organic, human toxicity-cancer,
human toxicity-noncancer, and ionizing radiation-human
health increase by approximately 6.3%, 6.1%, 2.0%, and 2.5%,
respectively, whereas the corresponding hydropower changes
are smaller or favorable. Resource use-minerals and metals
changes only slightly under either option. These results show
that selection of a low-carbon electricity source should
consider locally relevant environmental priorities rather than
climate change alone.
Energy-efficiency improvement and low-carbon electricity
substitution are therefore complementary. The former reduces
the absolute energy demand of the process, whereas the latter
reduces the upstream burden per unit of electricity. Their
effectiveness varies by impact category, and trade-offs-such as
the increase in ionizing radiation under a higher-nuclear-power
scenario-should be reported explicitly. Further improvement
could combine furnace optimization and other high-temperature energy-saving measures with low-carbon electricity and
targeted replacement or recycling of high-impact reagents.
These results also show that electricity decarbonization
alone cannot deliver comprehensive environmental optimization. Energy- and combustion-related impacts respond strongly
to a cleaner electricity mix, whereas ecotoxicity and some
toxicity/eutrophication indicators remain strongly influenced
by chemical-production chains. A combined strategy of energy
conservation, low-carbon electricity, and reagent reduction/
recycling/substitution is therefore required.
