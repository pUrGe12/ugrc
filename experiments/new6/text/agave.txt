Genomic Potential and Climate Resilience of Agave angustifolia
Haworth in Raicilla Production
Dolores Javier Sánchez-González
Postgraduate and Research Section, Higher School of Medicine, National Polytechnic Institute,
Mexico City 11340, Mexico; djsanchezg@ipn.mx or javiersglez@yahoo.com

Abstract
Agave angustifolia Haw. is one of the primary sources for the production of mezcals such
as raicilla and tuxca, in western Mexico. In Jalisco, Agave angustifolia evolved from being
a pre-Hispanic ritual and food resource (200–1500 CE) to becoming the primary basis
for 16th-century vino mezcal, the precursor to all modern mezcals, including raicilla and
tequila. This review synthesizes current genomic, epigenetic, and metabolic evidence to
elucidate the species’ evolutionary and adaptive potential. Our analysis suggests that the
domestication of A. angustifolia is characterized by a “domestication paradox,” where intensive clonal management ensures short-term productivity but creates genetic bottlenecks
that limit long-term adaptive capacity. We synthesize empirical evidence to show that
adaptive plasticity is driven by the integration of Crassulacean Acid Metabolism (CAM)
flexibility and dynamic global DNA methylation (GDM) profiles, which, while currently
correlative, suggest an environmental response mechanism. We conclude that securing the
socio-economic viability of Agave spirits requires a transition from intensive monocultures
to regenerative agroforestry, incorporating sexual propagation to maintain evolutionary
potential. Furthermore, we outline a roadmap for Genomics-Assisted Breeding (GAB 4.0),
integrating marker-assisted selection for juvenile traits to accelerate the release of resilient
biotypes, and precision diagnostic tools to reduce the ecological footprint of Agave production. This framework secures the genomic integrity of traditional spirits within a circular,
climate-resilient bioeconomy.
Keywords: genetic diversity; epigenomics; CAM metabolism; chloroplast genome;
transcriptomics; micropropagation; tequila; Agave angustifolia; raicilla; domestication
syndrome

The genus Agave L. represents one of the most remarkable examples of biological and
cultural evolution in the Americas [1–3]. With Mexico as its primary center of origin and
diversification, these plants have been utilized for over 9000 years as sources of food, fiber,
and fermented beverages [4,5]. Archeological evidence demonstrates that Agave species,
specifically A. angustifolia and A. maximiliana, were integral to the dietary, economic, and
ceremonial frameworks of the populations in Colima and Jalisco from 200 to 1500 Common
Era (CE) [6].
The primary use of agave lies in the production of spirituous beverages. Etymologically and historically, all distillates derived from cooked agave are classified as mezcals (a
term rooted in the Nahuatl mexcalli, from metl, ‘maguey,’ and ixcalli, ‘cooked’) and originated in western Mexico during the colonial period [4,7]. Consequently, mezcal serves as a

Agave angustifolia Haw. constitutes a fundamental biological resource owing to its
extensive geographic distribution and its role as a putative wild ancestor for several
agronomically significant taxa. These include the fiber-producing A. fourcroydes and various local landraces utilized for mezcal production [22,23]. Agave angustifolia is the second most utilized species in the raicilla industry with a 31.6% adoption rate, following
Agave maximiliana [24,25]. The biological potential of this species is not only of botanical
interest, but is a socio-economic necessity for the thousands of families whose livelihood
depends on its sustainable exploitation [26].
The current state of research into A. angustifolia has transitioned from classical morphological descriptions to a holistic framework integrating high-throughput genomic data,
metabolic profiling, and environmental adaptability [27–31]. The chloroplast (cp) is a
semi-autonomous organelle of cyanobacterial origin, found in plants and algae, that contains its own genome and performs photosynthesis by converting sunlight into chemical
energy [32,33]. Recent studies have successfully assembled its cp genome (plastome), providing critical data for phylogenetic resolution within the Agave genus [34]. Furthermore,
the species’ remarkable climate resilience is attributed to its water-conserving CAM and a
significant degree of photosynthetic plasticity, allowing flexible CO2 uptake under varying
environmental stresses [23,35–38]. At the molecular level, this adaptation is supported by
complex epigenetic mechanisms, such as global Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) methylation,
which regulates physiological responses to light intensity and helps maintain plant vigor
during in vitro propagation and environmental transitions [39,40].
Despite ongoing progress, the domestication status and genomic health of A. angustifolia
remain subject to conflicting hypotheses [41]. Certain researchers classify it as a wild
or partially domesticated taxon, while others identify distinct domestication syndromes,
including elevated carbohydrate concentrations and diminished leaf dentition within landraces managed under traditional agricultural frameworks [26]. A significant controversy
involves the genomic consequences of human intervention. Intensive clonal management
has paradoxically induced an unexpected excess of heterozygosity in specific regions,
whereas wild populations exploited for regional spirits like bacanora reveal concerning
levels of inbreeding and weak genetic structuring [42,43]. These divergent evolutionary
paths highlight the decisive role that socioeconomic policies play in shaping the Agave
genome [44].
While this review focuses primarily on A. angustifolia Haw., data from A. tequilana
Weber, A. maximiliana Baker, and other congeners are included as essential comparative
proxies to address specific knowledge gaps where A. angustifolia-specific empirical data
remain limited. These inclusions are necessary to validate broader evolutionary hypotheses
regarding CAM metabolic flexibility, fructan biosynthetic pathways, and the genomic
consequences of human-mediated selection within the Agave genus. To systematically
address the critical knowledge gaps, this review is structured to: (1) synthesize the current
landscape of A. angustifolia genomics [45,46]; (2) critically evaluate the empirical evidence
linking molecular mechanisms, such as epigenetic regulation and metabolic profiling,
to climate resilience and industrial performance [47]; and (3) formulate a hierarchical
data framework that distinguishes between published research findings in A. angustifolia,
evidence extrapolated from other Agave species, and emerging research hypotheses, thereby
providing a robust foundation for future germplasm conservation and precision breeding
strategies [25,48].

transition from intensive monocultures to regenerative agroforestry, incorporating sexual

2. Scope and Literature Synthesis Approach
This study provides an integrative narrative review of the genomic architecture and
climate resilience of A. angustifolia [7,19,49]. While not a systematic review, as it does not
employ PRISMA protocols, it utilizes a structured, comprehensive search strategy across
leading repositories to synthesize current knowledge and address knowledge gaps between
molecular research and artisanal production [44,50].
To ensure the rigor of this synthesis, a systematic screening procedure was
implemented [51,52]. A comprehensive search was executed across leading academic
repositories, namely PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar,
utilizing Boolean operators and specific keyword strings, such as “Agave angustifolia genomics”, “raicilla production sustainability”, “Crassulacean Acid Metabolism resilience”,
“SNP population structure Agave”, and “DNA methylation abiotic stress” [53–56]. The
literature search encompassed eight decades, from foundational botanical descriptions
in 1944 to 2026 advancements in high-throughput sequencing and Artificial Intelligence
(AI) for Agave classification [57]. Preference was given to peer-reviewed studies in English
and Spanish published between 2010 and 2024 to prioritize modern genomic methodologies [44,45]. Inclusion criteria focused on studies providing primary genomic data, identifying biosynthetic pathways, such as Cinnamyl Alcohol Dehydrogenase (AaCAD) genes
or evaluating physiological responses to environmental stressors in A. angustifolia [46].
Conversely, studies were excluded if they lacked primary empirical data, were restricted
to non-spirit-producing varieties, or were published outside the specified date range,
thereby ensuring the review focuses on modern genomic methodologies and their practical
applications [23,34,39,40].
Findings were synthesized through a comparative narrative analysis to resolve conflicting
hypotheses regarding the impact of clonal propagation on genetic variability [38,44,58,59].
The review acknowledges inherent regional research biases, as the majority of A. angustifolia
studies remain concentrated in Mexico [60,61].

3. Taxonomy and Genomic Diversity of Genus Agave
The genus Agave L. (Asparagaceae, Agavoideae) constitutes a monophyletic lineage
of recent origin currently undergoing rapid diversification centered in Mexico, which
hosts approximately 75 percent of the more than 200 recognized species [22,62]. Infrageneric classification identifies two primary subgenera based on inflorescence morphology: Agave, characterized by paniculate forms, and Littaea, defined by spicate or thyrsoid
structures [63–65]. However, this reliance on classical morphological descriptors often fails
to capture the high levels of intraspecific variation, particularly within the A. angustifolia
complex, historically obscuring the evolutionary relationships among wild, managed, and
fully domesticated populations.
Within this framework, the Agave angustifolia Haw. complex exhibits significant
taxonomic complexity, encompassing a gradient of wild, managed, and fully domesticated
populations across Mesoamerica [5,50]. In specific regions such as Oaxaca, this complex
includes wild varieties like A. angustifolia var. angustifolia and var. rubescens, alongside the
extensively cultivated ‘Espadín’ and partially cultivated taxa like A. rhodacantha [22,66].
Recent genomic and ethnobotanical evidence suggests that Agave tequilana Weber is a
domesticated cultivar derived from its closest wild relative, Agave angustifolia [67,68].

4. Agave angustifolia Haw. Biology and Socioeconomic Importance
4.1. Geographical and Ecological Distribution
The English botanist Adrian Hardy Haworth formally described Agave angustifolia
Haw. in 1812, publishing it on page 72 of his Synopsis Plantarum Succulentarum [72,73].
This taxon possesses the widest distribution within the genus, ranging from Sonora and
Tamaulipas, Mexico, south to Panama [74–77]. This remarkable distribution allows the
species to thrive in diverse ecosystems, ranging from coastal dunes at sea level to oak-pine
forests at elevations of 2200 m [23,43].
Historically, this plant has been a cornerstone of Mesoamerican cultures, utilized
for food, fiber, medicine, and the production of traditional fermented and distilled
beverages [42].

4.2. Reproductive Biology and Propagation Strategies
Agave angustifolia employs a mixed reproductive strategy encompassing one sexual
and two distinct asexual pathways, which collectively ensure its ecological resilience and
agricultural viability [41,78]. As a monocarpic perennial, the species flowers once at the
conclusion of its 6 to 10 year life cycle, generating a massive paniculate inflorescence between 3 and 8 m in height [7]. The hermaphroditic flowers are protandrous and strictly
self-incompatible, with anthers releasing pollen before stigmas reach receptivity. This mechanism necessitates cross-pollination, primarily by nectar-feeding bats such as Leptonycteris
spp., alongside diurnal birds and insects, to produce dehiscent capsules containing flat,
black seeds (Figure 2) [68].

Asexual reproduction occurs via two primary vegetative mechanisms. The first involves the continuous production of subterranean rhizomatous offshoots, locally termed
‘hijuelos’ (suckers), which emerge from the base of the maternal plant (Figure 3) [4,79].
A. angustifolia employs a robust strategy for asexual propagation through vegetative
apomixis, primarily utilizing rhizomatous offshoots and the formation of aerial bulbils from
axillary meristems on the inflorescence, which serve as a critical survival mechanism when
sexual reproduction or floral development is compromised following floral abscission or
injury [79,80]. Clonal propagation is widely leveraged in agricultural practice to maintain
phenotypic uniformity and accelerate crop establishment [81]. Within the A. angustifolia
complex, apomixis serves as a critical evolutionary mechanism to preserve polyploid
lineages and bypass meiotic instability (Figure 4) [28].

4.3. Phytochemical Reservoir and Industrial Versatility
A. angustifolia serves as the primary biological substrate for distilled spirits, including mezcal in Oaxaca (>86% of state output), bacanora in Sonora and raicilla in Jalisco.
This species defines the characteristic flavor and organoleptic profile of these spirits; consequently, the vast majority of globally consumed mezcal is a distillate of this specific
taxon [43]. The stem or piña is harvested after 6 to 10 years, roasted, fermented, and
distilled to produce these culturally and economically vital mezcals [28,82].
Beyond distillates, the species represents a diverse reservoir of bioactive compounds
and raw materials. While historically utilized for construction and ixtle fiber, contemporary research emphasizes the utility of agro-industrial by-products like leaves and
bagasse [83,84]. Bagasse is currently evaluated as a feedstock for biofuels, biodegradable packaging, and the extraction of high-value phytosterols like β-sitosterol (BSS), a
potent bioactive sterol used as a therapeutic agent and nutritional supplement [85,86].
Furthermore, the branched neo-fructans identified as agavins within the plant exhibit significant prebiotic potential, while various extracts have long been employed in traditional
medicine to treat sprains, bone fractures, and digestive ailments due to their inherent
anti-inflammatory properties (Figure 5) [84,87].

5. Genomic Potential and Molecular Architecture of Agave angustifolia
5.1. Genomic Architecture and Population Genetics
The conserved diploid karyotype of A. angustifolia (2n = 2x = 60), characterized
by 10 macrochromosomes and 50 microchromosomes with a nuclear DNA content of
8.17–8.21 pg, provides a structural foundation for metabolic stability in arid environments [80,90,91]. While the macro-structure exhibits evolutionary stasis, microchromosomal rearrangements serve as primary drivers for regional cultivar differentiation [92,93].
Ploidy variability ranging from diploidy in traditional landraces to triploidy in ‘Espadín’
and hexaploidy in specific wild populations, remains a critical, yet under-investigated,
factor in ecological fitness [94]. Furthermore, high-throughput genomic data, including
Restriction Site-Associated DNA sequencing (RADseq) and Amplified Fragment Length
Polymorphism (AFLP), indicate that anthropogenic management intensity is the principal
force shaping divergent genetic frameworks within the species [92,95].
Although the conserved chloroplast genome structure facilitates phylogenetic resolution, the functional correlation between micro-structural genomic variations remain poorly
understood, representing a significant knowledge gap in our ability to correlate karyotypic
diversity with specific adaptive traits and deploy this information in precision breeding
pipelines [4,32–34] (Figure 6).

5.2. Population Genomics and Anthropogenic Management
Recent population genomics, utilizing over 50,000 SNPs, reveals that intensive anthropogenic management fundamentally alters the evolutionary trajectory of A. angustifolia [42,43].
The defining feature of this process is the ‘domestication paradox’. Intensive clonal management, historically employed to maintain desirable agronomic traits, paradoxically preserves
individual heterosis while simultaneously eroding population-level allelic richness. This
creates a severe genetic bottleneck characterized by high observed heterozygosity (HO > HE )
in cultivated lineages, contrasting sharply with the concerning inbreeding coefficients
(FIS ≈ 0.13) observed in fragmented wild populations. This disparity indicates that while
clonal propagation stabilizes the performance of landraces, it limits the genetic reservoir
necessary for long-term adaptive responses to environmental stochasticity, necessitating
robust conservation strategies [44,69,70].
In contrast, wild populations utilized for spirits such as bacanora (A. angustifolia var.
pacifica) maintain higher total variability but show concerning signs of inbreeding and
shallow population structure, likely resulting from overexploitation and restricted gene
flow. This underscores the need to integrate data on gene flow with genomic monitoring, as
the long-term sustainability of these populations remains vulnerable to unchecked habitat
fragmentation [42–44].
5.3. Functional Genomics: Candidate Genes for Industrial and Adaptive Traits
Transcriptomic profiling identifies key regulatory hubs that underpin the commercial
and biological potential of A. angustifolia. The AaCAD family, particularly AaCAD2 and
AaCAD14, regulates lignification, providing a structural basis for fiber quality and stem
integrity essential for raicilla production [38,46,96]. Simultaneously, the identification of
Radical-Induced Cell Death (SRO) genes, specifically AhSRO2 and AhSRO3, serves as a
molecular diagnostic for abiotic stress, displaying significant up-regulation under thermal
and oomycete-induced stress [34,45].
Furthermore, proteomic analysis of somaclonal variants reveals that metabolic reprogramming, specifically the differential accumulation of key enzymes like AaCM, AaALS,
AaBCAT, and AaIPMS1, is critical for physiological adjustment in chloroplast-deficient
lineages. These genomic and proteomic markers provide a robust technical foundation for
marker-assisted selection. Transitioning from these correlative associations to predictive
breeding requires targeted functional validation, such as CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene
editing (envisioned here as a future direction for functional validation), to explicitly indicate
their contribution to climate resilience and industrial performance [32,33].
5.4. Evolutionary Plasticity: Epigenetic Regulation and Meiotic Restitution
The evolutionary plasticity of Agave angustifolia Haw. is underpinned by a sophisticated integration of epigenetic regulation and meiotic flexibility. GDM levels, ranging
from 13% to 22%, appear to constitute a dynamic regulatory layer governing physiological
vigor. However, following the direct evidence versus hypothesis hierarchy, these current
data remain correlative and require longitudinal, bisulfite-sequencing-based validation
to verify transgenerational memory. Within this epigenetic stress-memory framework,
hypomethylation events triggered by low-light conditions correlate directly with phenotypic transitions in somaclonal lineages. Simultaneously, specific chromatin modifications,
including H3K4me3 and H3K27me3, enable the plant to precisely reprogram CAM regulation in response to microclimatic stressors [46,51,83]. Nevertheless, characterizing these
epigenetic patterns as a memory mechanism necessitates further longitudinal evidence to
confirm their persistence and transgenerational stability.

Complementing these mechanisms, the species exhibits genomic flexibility through
meiotic restitution, characterized by the formation of unreduced diploid number (2n) pollen
grains via Second Division Restitution (SDR) and abnormal spindle formations resulting in
triads [79]. Although genetically determined, these events are significantly modulated by
environmental factors such as temperature and water deficit. This process provides the
fundamental raw material for polyploidization, a common evolutionary strategy within
the Agave genus used to enhance adaptive potential and biomass production in fluctuating
environments [5,97,98].
5.5. Future Perspectives: Genomics-Assisted Breeding 4.0 (GAB 4.0) and the Agavinome
The transition in Agave angustifolia Haw. research from descriptive botany to a comprehensive genomics framework is driven by the high-resolution characterization of the
agavinome, which comprises the conceptual space and molecular infrastructure of all
genomic, transcriptomic, and epigenetic resources within the genus Agave. This framework
is now accessible through high-resolution tools like the chromatin immunoprecipitation
(ChIP) assay [39,40,99].
Complementarily, integrating metabolomics via Fourier-Transform Mid-Infrared
(FT-MIR) and Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS) has enabled rapid
agavinome profiling [89,100,101]. These analyses indicate a metabolic superiority in
A. angustifolia, with fructan concentrations approximately 100 mg/g higher than regional
averages [37,83,102]. This metabolic foundation dictates raicilla yield and quality through
molecular signatures varying by plant age and environmental terroir, providing the
data necessary to synergize genomics, transcriptomics, and epigenomics within the
GAB 4.0 frontier, which represents the latest paradigm shift in crop improvement that
integrates multi-omics data with AI, deep learning, and automated high-throughput phenotyping for precise predictive breeding [5,42,103].
Integrating multi-omics with high-throughput phenotyping, Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats/Cas (CRISPR/Cas) genome editing, and AI-driven
predictive modeling, constitutes a promising prospective framework for future crop improvement. While the long generation time of Agave (6–10 years) inherently limits the
speed of traditional breeding, GAB 4.0 provides immediate, near-term applications that
bypass this temporal bottleneck. By leveraging genomic prediction models, we can perform marker-assisted selection for critical juvenile traits, such as early sugar accumulation
patterns, stress-responsive gene expression (e.g., the AhSRO gene family), and fiber quality
markers, as early as the seedling stage. This allows for the selection of superior phenotypes
within the first 12–18 months of development. Compared to traditional long-term breeding
timelines in other CAM crops, which often rely on slow, labor-intensive field phenotyping,
the integration of GAB 4.0 enables a high-throughput, predictive pipeline that significantly
compresses the selection cycle and accelerates the release of climate-resilient biotypes

6. Mechanisms of Climate Resilience and Adaptation
6.1. Metabolic and Physiological Plasticity
The exceptional climate resilience of Agave angustifolia Haw. within arid and semi-arid
environments is anchored in the plasticity and efficiency of its CAM. This water-conserving
pathway minimizes transpirational loss by restricting stomatal opening to nocturnal hours.
Under standard conditions, nocturnal CO2 fixation contributes approximately 75% of the
total daily carbon gain [5,23,106].
However, the species retains significant metabolic flexibility under extreme environmental shifts. For instance, withholding nocturnal CO2 while providing elevated
concentrations during the day can trigger a transition toward C3 -like light-only uptake.
This enables optimized carbon assimilation during transient fluctuations in gas availability
or atmospheric pressure [41]. These synergistic traits, alongside specialized architectural
adaptations, position A. angustifolia as a premier climate-ready crop for marginal lands
facing severe drought and a warming global climate. While these metabolic responses
confer superior drought resilience, extrapolating laboratory findings to field conditions
requires caution, as real-world interactions with soil microbiomes and extreme hydric
variability may significantly modulate these assimilation rates [83].
6.2. Respiratory Metabolism and Thermal Limits
Isothermal microcalorimetry across clonal lines identifies 35 ◦ C as the physiological
optimum for growth-related metabolic activity in A. angustifolia [23,41]. Beyond the 45 ◦ C
threshold, most clonal lines exhibit a marked decrease in metabolic heat rates and CO2
production, coupled with reduced substrate-to-carbon conversion efficiency and increased
enthalpy loss. However, intraspecific variation persists; the Agave angustifolia clonal line
CL24b, derived from bulbils, functions as a high-performance biotype characterized by exceptional respiratory efficiency and thermal resilience. Comparative metabolic assessments
identify CL24b as the most productive genotype, exhibiting superior biomass conversion
and leaf-mass accumulation under extreme thermal stress. Although the CL24b biotype
exhibits superior efficiency under controlled settings, broader comparative studies encompassing diverse clonal lines and variable edaphoclimatic conditions are necessary to
determine whether this trait is stable or a niche-specific genotypic response [23,41].

6.3. Epigenetic Regulation and Environmental Signaling
Beyond physiological responses, A. angustifolia utilizes complex epigenetic mechanisms to modulate environmental stress. GDM acts as a dynamic switch regulating
phenotypic variation and physiological vigor [39,40]. In environments with fluctuating
light intensity, the species modulates these methylation levels; for instance, exposure to
total darkness or low light triggers hypomethylation events correlating with phenotypic
transitions, such as the emergence of variegated or albino shoots in micropropagated lines.
This epigenetic stress-memory is crucial for survival, allowing the plant to reprogram its
chromatin state in response to abiotic stressors [39,40]. Furthermore, the identification
of Radical-Induced Cell Death (SRO) genes, specifically AhSRO3, which are significantly
up-regulated under low-temperature stress, suggests an integrated genomic-epigenetic
network that protects the plant against thermal and oxidative damage in arid landscapes.
In line with our evidence hierarchy framework, the precise mechanism of this epigenetic
regulation and its heritability in populations managed under traditional asexual propagation systems remains a hypothesis requiring further empirical evidence before being
integrated as a practical selection tool in breeding programs [45].
6.4. Osmoprotection and Secondary Metabolites
Agavins function in both carbon storage and osmotic adjustment. During drought,
these carbohydrates serve as osmoprotectants that maintain cellular turgor [87,88]. The
concentration and degree of polymerization of these fructans are dynamic and speciesspecific. A. angustifolia exhibits a higher accumulation of high-degree polymerization
(HDP) fructans upon maturation, enhancing structural and metabolic resilience against
progressive aridization in traditional cultivation zones. Despite these observations, further
research is required to ascertain whether the accumulation of HDP fructans is a direct
adaptive response to water stress or a secondary phenological trait that may fluctuate
significantly across different terroirs [5,7,29,88].

7. Bioeconomy and Phytochemical Potential in Raicilla
7.1. The Raicilla Bioeconomy and Regional Value Chains
Raicilla production has evolved into a cornerstone of Western Mexico’s regional bioeconomy, significantly bolstered by its 2019 Denomination of Origin (DO) status [7,107,108].
This industry operates through complex value chains where ancestral knowledge
and community-governed decision-making ensure distillate authenticity. Raicilla production utilizes a diverse range of taxa, primarily exploiting Agave angustifolia Haw.
and Agave rhodacantha Trel. in coastal regions, while Agave maximiliana Baker and
Agave inaequidens Koch predominate in the sierra, where they are locally known as
‘lechuguillas’ [109–111]. Raicilla is defined as a shade-grown spirit (Mezcal de sombra)
because Agave maximiliana and Agave inaequidens populations occur naturally within mountainous ecosystems where they develop beneath the forest canopy [49].
The protected region for the raicilla DO comprises 17 municipalities, with 16 located
in Jalisco and the municipality of Bahía de Banderas in Nayarit. In Jalisco, the established
territory covers Talpa de Allende, Mascota, San Sebastián del Oeste, Guachinango, Mixtlán,
Atenguillo, Cuautla, Ayutla, Atengo, Tecolotlán, Tenamaxtlán, Juchitlán, Chiquilistlán,
Tomatlán, Cabo Corrientes, and Puerto Vallarta [7,49]. In municipalities like Mascota
and Talpa de Allende, Jalisco, the raicilla industry has catalyzed social innovation, linking artisanal production with emerging sectors such as agrotourism [108,112,113]. This
socio-economic framework depends on the biological performance of A. angustifolia and
A. maximiliana, as their growth rates and carbohydrate yields determine distillery scalability
(Figure 7) [25,114,115].

7.2. Phytochemical Reservoir and Bioactive Potential
The phytochemical profile of A. angustifolia and its sympatric relatives represents a
vast, untapped resource for the pharmaceutical and nutraceutical sectors. These species
function as multifunctional sources of bioactive compounds, including steroidal sapogenins,
saponins, and various phenolic acids [2,112,120,121]. Notably, the leaves, often treated as
agro-waste, contain significant antioxidant and hypoglycemic metabolites [122,123]. Recent
extraction protocols have demonstrated the feasibility of recovering BSS and its derivatives
from agave bagasse through microwave-assisted processes, providing a pathway for the
valorization of industrial by-products [38,85]. While traditional ethnopharmacological
records suggest efficacy in treating gastrointestinal ailments and wound healing, these
properties require further validation through rigorous molecular characterization to confirm
their therapeutic potential. Consequently, these medicinal claims should be regarded as
preliminary until clinical or well-controlled in vitro studies elucidate specific dose–response
relationships [2,112,120,121].
7.3. Valorization of Agavins and Prebiotic Potential
The metabolic dynamics of agavins constitute a core component of the agave bioeconomy, serving as both the primary fermentable sugars for spirit production and as potent
prebiotics. This biochemical framework is exemplified in pulque, a viscous, slightly acidic
fermented beverage (4–7% ABV) derived from fresh agave sap, or aguamiel. Pulque remains distinct from mezcal because its fermentation utilizes unroasted must, bypassing
the thermal hydrolysis of fructans required for traditional distilled spirits [89,100,124,125].

7.4. Industrial Biorefineries and Sustainable Feedstocks
Transitioning to sustainable biorefinery models is essential to mitigate the environmental footprint of raicilla production [59,127,128]. It is estimated that every liter of
distillate generates approximately 15 to 20 kg of wet bagasse, posing a significant waste
management challenge. However, this fibrous residue possesses a high cellulose content
of up to 67%, making it an ideal feedstock for biofuels, bioplastics, and sustainable fiberbased materials [36,128,129]. Integrating advanced biotechnological tools, such as deep
learning for high-resolution biomass estimation and automated shredding optimization,
enables more efficient utilization of the entire plant [24,50,130]. By shifting focus from
spirit-only extraction to a circular bioeconomy, the raicilla industry can mitigate the risks
of intensive monocultures while enhancing the long-term conservation of Agave genetic
resources [11,67,114,123].

8. Biotechnological Strategies for Sustainable Production
8.1. Advanced Propagation Systems: In Vitro Culture and Micropropagation
The escalating demand for Agave angustifolia and A. maximiliana within the raicilla
industry necessitates high-efficiency mass propagation systems to mitigate wild population
overexploitation [131]. Recent biotechnological advancements have established robust
protocols, including an indirect somatic embryogenesis system for A. maximiliana achieving a 90% embryo-to-plantlet conversion rate. Additionally, axillary shoot proliferation
methods provide strategic pathways for ecological restoration and sustainable commercial
plantations [38,85]. These micropropagation frameworks utilize a diverse array of plant
growth regulators (PGRs), integrating classic agents such as 6-benzylaminopurine (BAP),
2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D), and indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) for shoot multiplication with non-traditional regulators like abscisic acid (ABA) and putrescine to optimize
embryo maturation [132]. Specifically for A. angustifolia, optimized Murashige and Skoog
(MS) media protocols have attained germination rates exceeding 80% and 100% survival
during the ex vitro transition [48]. Beyond clonal multiplication, these in vitro systems
facilitate critical molecular discoveries; for instance, using albino somaclonal variants has
elucidated links between GDM and the disruption of CAM machinery, characterized by
a failure to accumulate nocturnal malate [7,133,134]. Furthermore, de novo transcriptome
sequencing within these platforms has identified pivotal gene families, such as AaCAD,
offering targeted pathways for the genetic improvement of fiber and biomass traits to
transform underutilized biomass into value-added products for the food, pharmaceutical,
and bioenergy sectors [48,49,59,82].

8.3. Digital Precision and Sustainable Plantation Management
The modernization of agave cultivation requires digital precision to reconcile industrial yields with the regenerative goals of raicilla production. Integrating advanced deep
learning architectures, such as the nano iteration of You Only Look Once (YOLOv8n), provides a critical strategy for sustainable management [57]. This lightweight convolutional
neural network (requiring only 2.87 GFLOPs) enables real-time, automated monitoring
of plantation health and early disease detection. By implementing this tool, producers
can significantly reduce the excessive, blanket application of agrochemicals, replacing
them with localized, intervention-based actions [57,81]. Consequently, YOLOv8 serves as
a central pillar in our proposal for sustainability, as it optimizes resource use, minimizes
chemical leakage into the fragile mountainous ecosystems of the raicilla denomination
zone, and improves the economic efficiency of traditional distilleries through data-driven
planning [38,48,136–138].

9. Conservation, Challenges, and Future Directions
9.1. Conservation Genomics and Regenerative Agroecology
Regenerative agriculture transitioned from the conceptual frameworks established by
Medard Gabel in 1979 and Robert Rodale in 1983 into a rigorous ecological paradigm. A
2019 agronomic study analyzed this genealogy through historical comparative definitions [47].
This progression provided the scientific foundation for California’s 2025 official standard
prioritizing soil health and ecosystem resilience (https://www.cdfa.ca.gov/RegenerativeAg/
accessed on 11 July 2026).
Transitioning toward regenerative agroforestry models replaces intensive monocultures with traditional, diversified systems prioritizing intercropping, soil conservation,
and microbiome restoration. This paradigm maintains the genetic diversity essential for
securing long-term ecological resilience and sustaining spirit production [26,83]. Notably,
livestock integration constitutes a definitive feature of Traditional Agroecosystems with
Polyculture (ATP), where 60% of producers employ grazing compared to 40% in Conventional Monocultures (ACM). This strategy fulfills three primary ecological functions:
facilitating biological soil fertilization, reducing manual weed control expenditures, and
decreasing dependence on external commercial feed [139]. Integrating A. angustifolia and
A. maximiliana into raiciller ecosystems preserves agrobiodiversity and fosters eubiosis
within associated microbial consortia (Figure 8) [7,26,49].
Genomic monitoring remains essential to maintain gene flow between wild and
managed gene pools, preventing the genetic erosion associated with strict clonal propagation [42–44]. By combining ancestral knowledge with high-throughput genomic data,
producers can implement management plans that balance industrial productivity with the
preservation of the species’ evolutionary integrity [10,11,26].
To maintain the evolutionary potential and long-term viability of A. angustifolia, management strategies must shift from monocultural clonal systems to diversified approaches.
We propose that producers incorporate a minimum threshold of 20% seed-derived (sexual)
individuals within new plantations. This integration of sexually propagated lineages is

9.2. Climate Resilience and Future Biotechnological Innovation
Climate resilience in A. angustifolia is not merely a product of constitutive CAM
metabolism but is driven by a sophisticated epigenetic switch mediated by GDM. This
mechanism allows the plant to reprogram growth and pigment synthesis in response to
light and thermal stress [23,40,83]. The identification of stress-responsive genes, such as
the SRO and CAD families, marks a transition toward precision biotechnology in agave
cultivation [45,46]. Future research must prioritize the functional validation of these
candidate genes through CRISPR/Cas9 or similar gene-editing tools, alongside exploring
the agave holobiont, given the vital role of endophytes in mitigating drought and thermal
limits [45,106,140,141].
Regenerative agriculture and biotechnological tools are essential to ensure the socioecological resilience of agave spirits [51,142], facilitating gene flow and scaling production while preserving biological integrity and cultural heritage through community-led

10. Discussion
The biocultural identity of Agave angustifolia Haw. in western Mexico represents a
critical evolutionary nexus, marking the ontological transition from a pre-Hispanic ritual
staple (200–1500 CE) to the 16th-century ‘vino mezcal,’ the common ancestor for modern
agave spirits such as raicilla, mezcal, tuxca, bacanora, sisal, and tequila [7,13]. The genotype
and phenotype of A. angustifolia reflect a domestication syndrome driven by centuries of
management tailored for distilled spirit production. This trajectory underpins the fourth
generation of mezcal, tequila, and raicilla, marking a shift toward the technological and
genetic integration of traditional agave lineages [7,18,145].
Integrating the complex molecular architecture of A. angustifolia requires a holistic agavinome framework that encompasses nuclear and chloroplast structures, LTRretrotransposon dynamics, and epigenetic networks. This unified paradigm, rather than
isolated datasets, clarifies the evolutionary crossroads between wild reservoirs and domesticated landraces [5,50]. Furthermore, the species’ ability to reorganize its chromatin and
metabolic state in response to environmental fluctuations reflects fundamental principles
of plant cellular organization and signal transduction [146,147]. Consequently, the characterization of the A. angustifolia genome provides the definitive framework for elucidating
the evolutionary resilience and industrial potential inherent to this species within raicilla
production systems [109,110,112,148]. Precision mapping of 18S rDNA loci to the secondary
constriction of a specific macrochromosome pair in landraces like ‘Cimarrón’ and ‘Lineño’
provides the necessary cytogenetic resolution to decipher the taxonomic turbulence within
the A. angustifolia complex [94]. Such structural robustness facilitates the species’ persistence across the heterogeneous agroecological gradients of Jalisco while establishing
definitive evolutionary trajectories toward elite industrial hybrids like A. H11648 [34,46].
Agave hybrid (Ah) H11648 is the world’s only commercially exploited Ah, developed
in Tanzania (c. 1931) through a strategic backcross, (A. angustifolia × A. amaniensis) ×
A. amaniensis. This genotype integrates the high cellulose content (67%) of A. angustifolia
with superior biomass, making it the pillar of the global sisal industry in Africa, Asia, and
Brazil [45,90,149].
A significant genomic schism exists between fragmented wild populations and the
anthropogenic landraces of the raicilla industry. While wild variants, such as A. angustifolia
var. pacifica, exhibit substantial allelic richness, they are frequently compromised by elevated inbreeding coefficients and homozygosity, a direct consequence of disrupted pollinator networks. In stark contrast, the ‘Espadín’ and coastal raicilla lineages display
reduced total polymorphism but maintain a significant excess of observed heterozygosity
(HO > HE ) [42,44]. This heterosis fixation is a mechanistic byproduct of multi-decadal clonal
propagation and the selective preservation of vigorous individuals by producers, creating
a vital genetic buffer against environmental stochasticity in arid habitats [50].
Resilience in A. angustifolia is orchestrated by the transcriptomic diversification of
67,314 unigenes, particularly within the AaCAD family. Specifically, AaCAD3, 5, and
7 dictate tissue-specific lignification, providing a structural shield for the “piña” against
high-intensity irradiance [46]. This is augmented by SRO genes. Notably AhSRO2 and
AhSRO3, which calibrate oxidative responses to thermal stress and oomycete pathogens [45].
Such molecular pathways are intrinsically coupled to the flexibility of CAM, where nocturnal CO2 uptake provides 75% of carbon gain, ensuring survival under acute water

https://doi.org/10.3390/jgbg1020013

deficits [23]. Furthermore, the metabolic equilibrium observed at 35 ◦ C in genotypes like
the CL24b clone underscores a superior adaptive threshold, positioning these lineages as
elite candidates for thermally volatile regions [23,41].
Non-mutational adaptation is further mediated by an epigenetic stress memory, where
GDM (13–22%) and meristematic hypomethylation function as rapid physiological responses to environmental fluctuations [39,40]. These light-dependent chromatin remodeling patterns constitute the molecular foundation for terroir in raicilla production [39,40].
Concurrently, meiotic disruptions such as SDR yield 2n pollen, serving as a cytogenetic engine for polyploidization and novel genetic combinations [79]. Adopting a
GAB 4.0 framework remains imperative to reconcile escalating spirit demand with wild
germplasm preservation [103–105,150].
The genomic diversity of A. angustifolia shows that management reduces allelic richness while preserving heterozygosity, a balance maintained by high gene flow facilitated
by Leptonycteris curasoae and dynamic chromatin-based stress memory [50,64,102,135]. To
ensure long-term sustainability, we propose a strategic framework that integrates markerassisted selection of SRO genes for climate resilience, industrial valorization of bagasse
through microwave-assisted extraction, and the restoration of genomic diversity via sexual propagation and meiotic restitution, thereby securing the species as a foundational
biocultural and economic resource [10,19,99].

The synthesis of A. angustifolia molecular architecture creates a framework for shifting
from descriptive botany to a precision bioeconomy. Future research must prioritize three
